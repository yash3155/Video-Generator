### Oscillator Usage in Conjunction with Trend

* **Subordination to Basic Trend Analysis**: Oscillator analysis is a **secondary indicator** and must always be subordinated to primary trend analysis [1-3]. Traders must first establish the overall trend of the market and align trading strategies accordingly—taking buy signals in uptrends and short-sale signals in downtrends [1, 2].
* **Optimal Conditions for Use**:
  * **Horizontal / Sideways Trading Ranges**: Oscillators provide their greatest value in non-trending markets where prices fluctuate within a horizontal range and traditional trend-following systems (like moving averages) produce whipsaws [3, 4].
  * **Market Extremes**: They alert traders to short-term overextended conditions (overbought or oversold) near market tops and bottoms [5, 6].
  * **Maturing Trends**: They warn that a trend is losing momentum or nearing completion prior to visible price reversals [5-7].
* **Limitations & When to Ignore**: In the **early stages of a major trend breakout**, oscillators quickly reach extreme overbought or oversold territory and remain there for extended periods [7, 8]. Liquidating positions or trading counter to a new breakout based solely on extreme oscillator readings often leads to premature exits or missed moves [7, 9]. At the start of a move, oscillator readings should be subordinated to basic trend analysis and given greater weight only as the trend matures [7].

### Measuring Momentum and Rate of Change (ROC)

#### 1. Momentum
* **Core Concept**: Momentum measures the **velocity or rate of ascent/descent** of price changes rather than absolute price levels [10, 11]. Because of its construction, momentum is a **leading indicator** that turns ahead of the actual price trend [12, 13].
* **Mathematical Formula**:
  \\[M = V - V_x\\] [10]
  * **\\(M\\)**: Momentum value [10].
  * **\\(V\\)**: Latest closing price [10, 14].
  * **\\(V_x\\)**: Closing price \\(x\\) days ago [10, 14].
* **Midpoint & Boundaries**:
  * The midpoint is a **zero line** [10, 15]. Positive values (\\(V > V_x\\)) plot above zero; negative values (\\(V < V_x\\)) plot below zero [16].
  * Fixed upper and lower boundaries do not exist natively; they are established via **visual inspection of historical highs and lows** on the chart and drawn as horizontal bands [17].
* **Interpretation**:
  * **Above 0 and rising**: Uptrend is accelerating [11].
  * **Above 0 and flattening**: Price gains equal those from \\(x\\) days ago; velocity has leveled off [11, 18].
  * **Above 0 and falling**: Prices are still rising, but at a decelerating rate (losing momentum) [11, 18].
  * **Crossing 0 to the downside**: Latest price is below the price \\(x\\) days ago, establishing a near-term downtrend [12, 18].
  * **Below 0 and falling**: Downtrend is accelerating [18].
  * **Below 0 and rising**: Downtrend is decelerating [18].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Zero-Line Crossover**: Crossing above zero generates a **buy signal** (valid only if the major trend is bullish); crossing below zero generates a **sell signal** (valid only if the major trend is bearish) [12, 15].

#### 2. Rate of Change (ROC)
* **Core Concept**: Rate of Change calculates momentum as a **ratio of closing prices** rather than a simple subtraction, preventing sharp single-day historical price distortions [19, 20].
* **Mathematical Formula**:
  \\[\text{ROC} = 100 \cdot \left(\frac{V}{V_x}\right)\\] [20]
  * **\\(V\\)**: Latest closing price [20].
  * **\\(V_x\\)**: Closing price \\(x\\) days ago [20].
* **Midpoint**: The **100 line** acts as the midpoint centerline [20]. Readings above 100 indicate price advances (\\(V > V_x\\)), while readings below 100 indicate price declines (\\(V < V_x\\)) [20].

### Commodity Channel Index (CCI)

* **Developer**: Donald R. Lambert [21].
* **Core Concept & Normalization**: CCI measures the difference between current price and its moving average (typically 20 days) and normalizes the output by dividing by a constant factor based on **mean deviation** [21].
* **Scale & Range**: Fluctuates within a normalized vertical range, typically anchored between **\\(+100\\) and \\(-100\\)** [21].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Lambert’s Original Trend Rules**: Initiate **long positions** when CCI moves above \\(+100\\); initiate **short sales** when CCI falls below \\(-100\\) [21, 22].
  * **Overbought / Oversold Usage**: Modern chartists frequently treat readings above \\(+100\\) as **overbought** and readings below \\(-100\\) as **oversold** [23].
* **Applications**: Applicable to commodities as well as stock index futures and options (e.g., S&P 100 / OEX) [23].

### Relative Strength Index (RSI)

* **Developer**: J. Welles Wilder, Jr. (1978, *New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems*) [24].
* **Core Concept**: Solves two major problems of basic momentum lines: (1) smooths out erratic price shifts caused by sharp changes dropping off \\(x\\) days ago, and (2) establishes a fixed, constant vertical scale of **0 to 100** [24, 25]. *(Note: "Relative Strength" in RSI is distinct from comparative asset ratio analysis)* [25].
* **Mathematical Formula**:
  \\[\text{RSI} = 100 - \left(\frac{100}{1 + \text{RS}}\right)\\] [26]
  where:
  \\[\text{RS} = \frac{\text{Average of } x \text{ days' up closes}}{\text{Average of } x \text{ days' down closes}}\\] [26]
* **Variables & Definitions**:
  * **\\(x\\)**: Calculation period (Wilder’s default is **14 days** or **14 weeks**) [26].
  * **Average of \\(x\\) days' up closes**: Total points gained on up days during \\(x\\) days divided by \\(x\\) [26].
  * **Average of \\(x\\) days' down closes**: Total points lost on down days during \\(x\\) days divided by \\(x\\) [26].
  * **\\(\text{RS}\\)**: Relative Strength ratio [26].
* **Sensitivity Adjustments**: Shortening time spans (e.g., 5, 7, or 9 days) increases indicator amplitude and volatility for short-term trading [27, 28]. Lengthening spans (e.g., 21 or 28 days) smooths the line and narrows amplitude [27].

### 70/30 Overbought-Oversold Lines

* **Standard Thresholds**:
  * **Overbought**: RSI values above **70** [28, 29].
  * **Oversold**: RSI values below **30** [28, 29].
* **Market-Specific Line Shifts**:
  * **Bull Markets**: Strong uptrends shift boundaries upward—**80** becomes the overbought line, while **40 or 30** acts as oversold support [28].
  * **Bear Markets**: Strong downtrends shift boundaries downward—**20** becomes the oversold line, while **60 or 70** acts as overbought resistance [28].
* **Confirmation & Signal Rules**:
  * **Buy Signal**: Wait for RSI to dip below 30 into oversold territory, form a reversal structure, and then **cross back above the 30 line** [30].
  * **Sell Signal**: Wait for RSI to rise above 70 into overbought territory, form a setup, and then **cross back below the 70 line** [30].
  * **50 Midpoint Line**: Serves as support during pullbacks and resistance during bounces; crossovers above/below 50 are used by some traders as directional signals [30].

### Failure Swings

Failure swings are high-probability trend reversal patterns in the RSI that occur strictly when the indicator is in extreme territory (\\(>70\\) or \\(<30\\)) [31, 32]. They originate from Charles Dow's failure swing principles [33].

```
TOP FAILURE SWING (Bearish)              BOTTOM FAILURE SWING (Bullish)

  RSI                                      RSI
  ^                                        ^
  |   Peak 1 (>70)                         |
  |     /\      Peak 2 (< Peak 1)          |           Point 3 (Peak)
  |----/--\------/\--------------- 70 Line |--------------/----\---------------- 30 Line
  |   /    \    /  \                       |             /      \    Trough 2 (> Trough 1)
  |  /   Point 3    \ (Sell Signal)        |  Trough 1  /        \   /\
  | /   (Trough)     \                     |   (<30)   /          \_/  \ (Buy Signal)
  |/                  \                    |     \    /                 \
  +-------------------------> Time         +------\--/------------------------> Time
```

#### 1. Top Failure Swing (Bearish Reversal)
1. **First Peak**: RSI moves above 70 (Peak 1) [31, 34].
2. **Pullback**: RSI declines to form an intervening trough (Point 3) [31, 34].
3. **Failed Second Peak**: RSI rallies again but fails to exceed Peak 1 (forming a lower peak, Peak 2) [31, 34].
4. **Confirmation / Sell Signal**: RSI breaks below the middle trough (Point 3) [31, 34].

#### 2. Bottom Failure Swing (Bullish Reversal)
1. **First Trough**: RSI dips below 30 (Trough 1) [31, 34].
2. **Reaction Peak**: RSI rallies to form an intervening peak (Point 3) [31, 34].
3. **Failed Second Trough**: RSI declines again but holds above Trough 1 (forming a higher trough, Trough 2) [31, 34].
4. **Confirmation / Buy Signal**: RSI breaks above the middle peak (Point 3) [31, 34].

### RSI Divergences

* **Definition**: Wilder identifies divergence as *"the single most indicative characteristic of the Relative Strength Index"* [35]. It occurs when price action and RSI move in opposing directions while RSI is in extreme territory (\\(>70\\) or \\(<30\\)) [32, 35].
* **Bearish (Negative) Divergence**:
  * Price achieves a **new high**, but RSI fails to exceed its prior peak (forming a lower peak or double top above 70) [9]. Signals fading upside momentum and an impending market top [9, 35].
* **Bullish (Positive) Divergence**:
  * Price establishes a **new low**, but RSI fails to set a new low (forming a higher trough or double bottom below 30) [9]. Signals diminishing selling pressure and a market bottom [9, 35].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * The initial entry into overbought/oversold territory is only a warning; strong trends can maintain extreme readings [9, 32].
  * Look for non-confirmation on the **second swing** into the extreme zone [9].
  * Full confirmation occurs when RSI breaks its intermediate trough (for tops) or peak (for bottoms) [9].
  * Trendline breaks drawn directly on the RSI line provide additional early confirmation [32, 35].

USING THE 70 AND 30 LINES TO GENERATE SIGNALS Horizontal lines appear on the oscillator chart at the 70 and 30 values. Traders often use those lines to generate buy and sell signals. We already know that a move under 30 warns of an oversold condition. Suppose the trader thinks a market is about to bottom and is looking for a buying opportunity. He or she watches the oscillator dip under 30. Some type of divergence or double bottom may develop in the oscillator in that oversold region. A crossing back above the 30 line at that point is taken by many traders as a confirmation that the trend in the oscillator has turned up. Accordingly, in an overbought market, a crossing back under the 70 line can often be used as a sell signal. (See Figure 10.14.)"
- ""Failure swings," as Wilder calls them, occur when the RSI is above 70 or under 30. A top failure swing occurs when a peak in the RSI (over 70) fails to exceed a previous peak in an uptrend, followed by a downside break of a previous trough. A bottom failure swing occurs when the RSI is in a downtrend (under 30), fails to set a new low, and then proceeds to exceed a previous peak. STOCHASTICS (K%D) The Stochastic oscillator was popularized by George Lane (president of Investment Educators, Inc., Watseka, IL). It is based on the observation that as prices increase, closing prices tend to be closer to the upper end of the price range. Conversely, in downtrends, the closing price tends to be near the lower end of the range. Two lines are used in the Stochastic Process—the %K line and the %D line. The %D line is the more important and is the one that provides the major signals."
