### 1. Drawing Tentative vs. Valid Trendlines

* **Core Concepts**: A trendline is one of the simplest yet most valuable tools in technical analysis [1]. It acts as a dynamic support level in an uptrend or resistance level in a downtrend and defines the rate of speed (slope) of an ongoing price trend [1-3].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Tentative Trendline**: A straight line drawn connecting two initial reaction lows (in an uptrend) or two rally highs (in a downtrend) [4, 5].
  * **Valid Trendline**: A tentative trendline that has been confirmed by a **third test** where prices touch the line and successfully bounce off it in the direction of the trend [4, 6].
* **Mathematical & Geometric Principles**:
  * Any straight line requires a minimum of two points to be drawn [5].
  * Mathematically, if \\(P_1(t_1)\\) and \\(P_2(t_2)\\) are two reaction lows at times \\(t_1\\) and \\(t_2\\), the tentative trendline equation is:
    \\[P(t) = P_1 + \left( \frac{P_2 - P_1}{t_2 - t_1} \right) (t - t_1)\\]
  * Confirmation occurs when a third coordinate \\(P_3(t_3)\\) satisfies \\(P(t_3) \approx P_3\\) and price reverses away from the line [4, 6].
* **Chart Patterns**:
  * **Up Trendline**: Drawn upward and to the right along successive, higher reaction lows [1, 4].
  * **Down Trendline**: Drawn downward and to the right along successive, lower rally peaks [1, 4].
  * **Price Range Rule**: Trendlines must be drawn over or under the **entire day's price range** (including high and low shadow extremes), not just closing prices [7].
  * **Handling Minor Penetrations**: If price dips beneath an up trendline intraday but closes back above it, a compromise technique is to retain the original trendline while drawing a secondary dotted trendline until market action resolves which line is truer [8, 9].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * **2-Point Requirement**: Requires two ascending reaction lows (with the peak between them penetrated or tested) to establish a tentative line [5].
  * **3-Point Validation**: A third successful test (point 5) is strictly required to convert a tentative trendline into a valid trendline [4, 6].
  * **Significance Criteria**: The significance of a trendline increases with **length of time intact** (e.g., 9 months vs. 9 days) and **number of times tested** (e.g., 8 successful tests vs. 3) [10].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Buying/Selling Boundaries**: In an uptrend, dips toward the up trendline are primary buying areas; in a downtrend, rallies toward the down trendline are selling areas [3].
  * **Violation Rule**: A decisive closing break of a valid trendline signals a trend change and mandates liquidating positions held in the direction of the prior trend [3, 11].
* **Author Case Study**:
  * **KBW Bank Index (Weekly Bar Chart)**: Murphy highlights a long-term up trendline drawn along two 1995–1996 reaction lows [12]. A third reaction low at the start of 1998 bounced directly off this trendline, confirming its validity and keeping the major uptrend intact [12].

### 2. Price and Time Filters (3% and 2-Day Rule)

* **Core Concepts**: Filters are objective rules used to separate valid trendline or support/resistance breakouts from bad signals and false intraday breaches ("whipsaws") [13].
* **Definitions & Mathematical Formulas**:
  * **3% Price Filter**:
    * *Definition*: Requires price to break and close beyond a major trendline (or support/resistance level) by at least 3% of the price level at the point of penetration [13, 14].
    * *Formula (Downside Breakout)*:
      \\[P_{\text{trigger}} = P_{\text{line}} \times (1 - 0.03)\\]
      *(Where \\(P_{\text{line}}\\) is the trendline price at breakout; a valid short signal occurs when \\(P_{\text{close}} \le P_{\text{trigger}}\\))* [13, 14].
    * *Short-term Adaptation*: Shorter-term trading uses a 1% filter; stock chartists may require a 1 full point move [14]. *(Note: The 3% rule does not apply to interest rate futures)* [13].
  * **2-Day Time Filter**:
    * *Definition*: Requires prices to close beyond the trendline for **two consecutive trading days** to confirm a valid breakout (a 1-day breach is ignored) [15].
  * **Friday Close Filter**:
    * *Definition*: Requires a Friday close beyond the breakout point to validate weekly signals [15].
* **Chart Patterns**: Applied to major trendline breaks, support/resistance level penetrations, double top/bottom confirmations, and Head and Shoulders neckline breakouts [15-17].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * Intraday penetrations do not constitute a breakout; a valid break requires a **closing price** that satisfies either the percentage threshold (1–3%) or the time duration (2 consecutive closes) [13, 15].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Filter Tradeoff**: If a filter is too small, it fails to eliminate whipsaws; if too large, a significant portion of the initial price move is missed before entry [14].
  * Traders must select filters appropriate for the timeframe/trend degree being followed [14].
* **Author Case Study**:
  * **Gold Market Numerical Example**: Murphy illustrates a major up trendline in gold broken at \$400 [14]. To satisfy the 3% price filter, gold must close \$12 below the line (\\(400 \times 0.03 = \\)12), requiring a closing price at or below **\$388** before a valid sell signal is confirmed [14].

### 3. Trendline Role Reversal

* **Core Concepts**: When a support or resistance level (or trendline) is broken by a significant margin, it reverses its structural role and becomes its opposite [18-20].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Broken Up Trendline (Support \\(\rightarrow\\) Resistance)**: Once an up trendline is broken on the downside, it acts as an overhead resistance ceiling on subsequent price rallies [20-22].
  * **Broken Down Trendline (Resistance \\(\rightarrow\\) Support)**: Once a down trendline is broken on the upside, it acts as a support floor on subsequent price dips [20, 21].
* **Psychological Rationale**:
  * Driven by market participants (longs, shorts, uncommitted) [23, 24]. Longs who bought at support suffer losses when price drops; on subsequent bounces back to that price level, they rush to liquidate at break-even, transforming prior buying interest into heavy sell orders (overhead resistance) [23, 24].
* **Chart Patterns**:
  * **Up Trendline Break & Return Move**: Price falls through an up trendline, rallies back to test the underside of the broken trendline, fails at the line, and turns down [20-22].
  * **Down Trendline Break & Return Move**: Price breaks above a down trendline, dips back to test the top side of the line, holds support, and turns up [20, 21].
* **Confirmation Rules**: Role reversal takes effect **only after a decisive/significant penetration** confirmed via price/time filters [20, 25, 26].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Line Extension**: Always extend all broken trendlines far out to the right on charts, as they frequently serve as major support/resistance barriers in the future [20].
  * **Trade Execution**: Sell/short rallies that bounce into a broken up trendline from below; buy dips that retest a broken down trendline from above [20-22].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **U.S. Dollar Index Daily Chart (Figure 4.10c)**: A major up trendline was broken to the downside [21, 22]. On the subsequent rally attempt, the underside of the broken trendline acted as a precise overhead resistance barrier [22].
  * **Nasdaq 100 Index Daily Chart (Figure 4.5c)**: An early 1997 resistance peak was broken on the upside; a year later, an intermediate market decline stopped right at that prior resistance peak, which had reversed roles to become new support [27].

### 4. The Fan Principle

* **Core Concepts**: As an advancing or declining trend loses momentum, prices flatten out and require a sequence of three trendlines drawn from the same starting peak or low [28-30]. The lines fan out horizontally, giving the formation its name [30].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Fan Lines**: A set of three trendlines (Lines 1, 2, and 3) radiating from a single major high/low point as corrective price waves fail to cross previous lines [28-30].
* **Mathematical & Angular Structure**:
  * Fan lines exhibit progressively flatter angles (\\(\theta_1 > \theta_2 > \theta_3\\)) radiating from the origin point [28-30].
* **Chart Patterns**:
  * **Fan Top Reversal**:
    1. Up Trendline 1 is broken on the downside [28, 29].
    2. Price rallies back to the bottom of Line 1 (resistance) but fails; Trendline 2 is drawn underneath the new reaction low [28-30].
    3. Line 2 is broken; price rallies back to Line 2 (resistance) and fails again; Trendline 3 is drawn [29, 30].
    4. **The breaking of Line 3 completes the downside trend reversal** [29, 30].
  * **Fan Bottom Reversal**: Mirror image where three down trendlines fan out across successive rally peaks; breaking the third line completes the upside reversal [29, 30].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * Violations of Line 1 or Line 2 indicate momentum loss but do **not** confirm a full trend reversal [29, 30].
  * **The breaking of the third trendline is the valid signal for a total trend reversal** [29, 30].
  * Broken Fan Lines 1 and 2 often function as resistance (at tops) or support (at bottoms) [29, 30].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Do not initiate full trend-reversal positions on the break of Line 1 or 2 [29, 30].
  * Await the **closing break of the third fan line** before executing reversal trades [29, 30].
* **Author Case Study**:
  * **Russell 2000 Index Daily Bar Chart (Figure 4.11c)**: At a market bottom, three distinct fan lines were drawn along successive rally peaks [29, 30]. The upside breaking of the third fan line confirmed the end of the bear move and launched a new bull uptrend [29, 30].

### 5. Importance of the Number Three

* **Core Concepts**: The number three recurs throughout technical analysis, serving as a primary numerical threshold for market structure, cycle division, and pattern confirmation [31].
* **Comprehensive Inventory of Manifestations**:
  1. **Trendline Validation**: 2 points draw a line; the **3rd point** confirms validity [4, 6].
  2. **Fan Principle**: Requires **3 fan lines** to signal a valid trend reversal [31].
  3. **Major Trend Phases**: Bull and bear markets contain **3 major phases** (Dow Theory & Elliott Wave) [31].
  4. **Trend Classifications**: **3 degrees of trend** (Major, Secondary/Intermediate, Minor) [31-33].
  5. **Trend Directions**: **3 market directions** (Up, Down, Sideways/Flat) [31, 34].
  6. **Reversal Chart Patterns**: **3 prominent peaks/troughs** form Triple Tops/Bottoms and Head & Shoulders patterns [31].
  7. **Continuation Triangles**: **3 primary triangle types** (Symmetrical, Ascending, Descending) [31].
  8. **Price Gaps**: **3 principal gap types** (Breakaway, Runaway/Measuring, Exhaustion) [31].
  9. **Primary Technical Data Sources**: **3 core information inputs** (Price, Volume, Open Interest) [31].
  10. **Percentage Retracements**: Trends divide into **thirds** (\\(1/3\\) or 33% minimum retracement; \\(2/3\\) or 66% maximum retracement) [31, 35-37].
  11. **Speed Resistance Lines**: Constructed by dividing vertical price range into **thirds** (\\(1/3\\) and \\(2/3\\) speedlines) [37-39].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Treat 3-part confirmations (3 touches on a line, 3 fan lines, 3 pattern peaks) as a minimum requirement for structural completeness [6, 30, 31].

### 6. 45-Degree Steepness

* **Core Concepts**: The most sustainable major trendlines advance or decline at an average angle approximating **45 degrees**, representing a harmonic balance between price and time [40, 41].
* **Definitions & Mathematical Principles**:
  * **Gann 1x1 Line**: A \\(45^\circ\\) trendline drawn from a major top or bottom where price moves by 1 unit per 1 unit of time (\\(1 \times 1\\)) [41].
  * **Steepness Dynamics**:
    * **Slope \\(> 45^\circ\\) (Too Steep)**: Price is advancing too rapidly; the rate of ascent is unsustainable [41].
    * **Slope \\(< 45^\circ\\) (Too Flat)**: The uptrend is too weak, suspect, and unreliable [41].
* **Chart Patterns**:
  * **Accelerating Trend**: As a trend speeds up, a series of increasingly steeper trendlines must be drawn [42, 43].
  * **Steep Line Break**: The breaking of a steep trendline (\\(>45^\circ\\)) usually represents a corrective adjustment back toward a sustainable \\(45^\circ\\) slope, rather than an immediate full trend reversal [41, 42].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * A trendline adhering to a \\(45^\circ\\) angle inspires the highest confidence for long-term trend survival [40, 41].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Avoid Overreacting to Steep Breaks**: Do not assume a major trend reversal when an overly steep line is broken; expect prices to settle onto a flatter, \\(45^\circ\\) support line [41, 42].
  * **Transition to Moving Averages**: When an accelerating trend forces the drawing of multiple steeper trendlines, switch to a **moving average** (which functions as a curvilinear trendline) [42, 43].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **S&P 500 Stock Index Weekly Bar Chart (Figure 4.14b)**: Demonstrates an accelerating uptrend requiring three progressively steeper trendlines (Lines 1, 2, and 3), where the steepest line (Line 3) becomes the critical near-term line to monitor [43].
  * **Slope Comparison Diagram (Figure 4.12)**: Contrasts steep Line 1 (unsustainable rate of advance), \\(45^\circ\\) Line 2 (ideal price/time balance), and flat Line 3 (weak, suspect uptrend) [40, 41].

### 7. Channel Lines

* **Core Concepts**: A channel line (or return line) is drawn parallel to the basic trendline to enclose price action inside a trending channel [44, 45].
* **Definitions & Construction**:
  * **Basic Trendline**: Drawn along reaction lows in an uptrend (points 1, 3) or rally highs in a downtrend [44, 45].
  * **Channel Line**: A dotted line projected parallel to the basic trendline starting from the first prominent peak (point 2) in an uptrend, or first reaction low (point 2) in a downtrend [44, 45].
* **Mathematical & Measuring Formulas**:
  * **Channel Width (\\(W_{\text{channel}}\\))**:
    \\[W_{\text{channel}} = |P_{\text{channel\_line}} - P_{\text{basic\_trendline}}|\\]
  * **Breakout Price Objective**:
    \\[\text{Target Price} = P_{\text{breakout}} \pm W_{\text{channel}}\\]
    *(Add channel width to upside breakouts; subtract from downside breakouts)* [46].
* **Chart Patterns**:
  * **Bullish Channel**: Upward-sloping parallel boundaries [45].
  * **Bearish Channel**: Downward-sloping parallel boundaries [45, 47].
  * **Channel Failure**: Prices fail to reach the upper channel line in an uptrend \\(\rightarrow\\) early warning that the lower basic trendline will break [48, 49].
  * **Channel Breakout**: Prices break above the upper channel line in an uptrend \\(\rightarrow\\) signals trend acceleration [48, 50].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * Channel validity is confirmed when price reaches the channel line (point 4), backs off to test the basic trendline (point 5), and holds [45].
  * **Hierarchy Rule**: The basic trendline is far more important and reliable than the channel line [46].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Profit Taking**: Initiate long positions at the basic up trendline; take short-term profits when price reaches the upper channel line [47].
  * **Adding Positions**: Breakouts above a rising channel line signal trend acceleration and justify adding to long positions [48].
  * **Early Warning Action**: If a rally fails to reach the channel line, tighten protective stops under the basic trendline [49].
  * **Redrawing Channel Boundaries**:
    * If upper channel breaks: Draw a new steeper basic up trendline parallel to the new upper channel line [50, 51].
    * If upper channel fails: Draw a new down-sloping resistance line over the two declining peaks, then project a parallel support line from the intervening low [50, 51].
* **Author Case Study**:
  * **Dow Utilities Monthly Line Chart (25-Year Study, Figure 4.16c)**: Demonstrates prices fluctuating precisely between upper and lower parallel channel lines over a 25-year span [47]. Major tops in 1987, 1989, and 1993 peaked right at the upper channel line, while the 1994 major bottom bounced directly off the lower channel line [47].

- "TREND HAS THREE DIRECTIONS We've mentioned an uptrend, downtrend, and sideways trend for a very good reason. Most people tend to think of markets as being always in either an uptrend or a downtrend. The fact of the matter is that markets actually move in three directions—up, down, and sideways. It is important to be aware of this distinction because for at least a third of the time, by a conservative estimate, prices move in a flat, horizontal pattern that is referred to as a trading range. This type of sideways action reflects a period of equilibrium in the price level where the forces of supply and demand are in a state of relative balance.
