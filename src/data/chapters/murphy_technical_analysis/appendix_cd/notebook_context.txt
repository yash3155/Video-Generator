## Part 1: The 5-Step Plan for Building and Testing an Objective Trading System

### Core Concepts & Definitions
* **Mechanical (Objective) System**: A system that defines all entry, exit, and money management parameters using explicit, non-subjective rules so that 100 people following the same instructions arrive at identical trading decisions [1, 2]. It eliminates human emotion (fear and greed) and provides disciplined execution [3, 4].
* **System Objectives & Benefits**:
  * **Back-Testing**: Allows testing of trading concepts on historical price data prior to committing capital [3].
  * **Objectivity**: Removes emotional stress during active market conditions by automating trade triggers [3].
  * **Efficiency & Scale**: Enables traders to cover multiple markets, system variations, and timeframes simultaneously [3, 4].

---

### The 5-Step Plan

#### Step 1: Start with a Concept (an Idea)
* **Design Principles**:
  * Concepts must possess a **positive mathematical expectation** over the long run [5].
  * Rules should be straightforward, fit the trader’s personality, trade in the direction of the major trend, let profits run, and cut losses short [5].
  * **Exits vs. Entries**: Designing exit logic is more critical and difficult than designing entry rules [6]. Non-auto-reversing systems (exiting a trade before opening a new position in the opposite direction) are preferred [6].
  * **Minimal Optimization**: Keep parameters to a minimum to prevent curve-fitting and over-optimization [6].
* **System Categories**:
  1. **Trend-Following**: Trades in the direction of the primary trend (e.g., Moving Averages, Donchian’s Weekly Rule) [7].
  2. **Counter-Trend**:
     * *Support/Resistance*: Buys at support levels and sells at resistance levels [7].
     * *Retracements*: Buys pullbacks in bull markets or sells rallies in bear markets (e.g., 50% retracements) [7].
     * *Oscillators*: Buys oversold conditions and sells overbought conditions, especially when combined with momentum divergences [8].
  3. **Pattern Recognition**: Visual formations (e.g., Head and Shoulders) or statistical regularities (e.g., seasonal price tendencies) [8].

#### Step 2: Turn Your Idea into a Set of Objective Rules
* Translate abstract concepts into explicit rules without ambiguity, ensuring any two analysts implementing the rules produce identical signals [2].

#### Step 3: Visually Check It Out on the Charts
* Perform an informal chart inspection to verify that signals execute as intended and confirm potential profitability before writing computer code [9].

#### Step 4: Formally Test It with a Computer
* **Data Selection**: Begin back-testing with continuous spread-adjusted data series (e.g., Schwager's method), followed by testing on individual futures contracts [10].
* **Transaction Costs**: Slippage and commission costs are factored in after initial raw testing to maintain clean comparative evaluations [11].
* **Multi-Dimensional Robustness Rules**:
  * **Parameter Stability**: Nearby parameter settings must yield consistent profitability (e.g., if a \\(5/20\\) moving average crossover works, settings such as \\(6/18\\), \\(6/23\\), \\(4/21\\), and \\(5/19\\) must also perform well) [11].
  * **Time Period Consistency**: The strategy must maintain performance across non-overlapping multi-year periods (e.g., 1981–1986 vs. 1990–1995) [12].
  * **Cross-Market Universality**: A robust model must perform across multiple markets within a sector (e.g., Crude Oil, Heating Oil, Unleaded Gasoline) and across unrelated asset classes [12].

#### Step 5: Evaluate Results
* Evaluate performance metrics, drawdown characteristics, trade-by-trade behavior, and equity curves to ensure psychological comfort during real-time execution [13]. Less than 5% of tested trading concepts demonstrate real-world viability [14].

### Mathematical Formulas & Variables

1. **Profit Factor**:
   \\[\text{Profit Factor} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit on Winning Trades}}{\text{Gross Loss on Losing Trades}}\\]
   * *Variables*: Gross Profit = total dollar gain from all winning trades; Gross Loss = absolute total dollar loss from all losing trades [15].
   * *Rule*: Long-term systems should target a Profit Factor of **2.00 or higher** [15].

2. **Average Trade (Mathematical Expectation)**:
   \\[\text{Average Trade} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit} - \text{Gross Loss}}{\text{Total Number of Trades}}\\]
   * *Rule*: Must significantly exceed combined slippage and commission costs per trade [15].

3. **Maximum Intraday Drawdown**:
   \\[\text{Max Drawdown} = \max_{t} \left( \text{Peak Equity}_t - \text{Trough Equity}_t \right)\\]
   * Measures the largest peak-to-trough decline in account equity (evaluated in both absolute dollar and percentage terms) [15].

4. **Zweig 4% Model (EasyLanguage Mathematical Rules)**:
   * **Buy Signal Condition**:
     \\[\frac{\text{Close} - \text{Lowest Close}}{\text{Lowest Close}} \ge 0.04 \quad (4\%)\\]
   * **Sell Signal Condition**:
     \\[\frac{\text{Highest Close} - \text{Close}}{\text{Highest Close}} \ge 0.04 \quad (4\%)\\]
   * *Variables*: \\(\text{Close}\\) = current weekly close; \\(\text{Lowest Close}\\) = minimum weekly close recorded while short/out; \\(\text{Highest Close}\\) = maximum weekly close recorded while long [16-18].

---

### Confirmation Rules & Trading Rules
* **Exits Over Entries**: Always define stop-loss parameters and exit rules prior to entry [5, 6].
* **No Curve-Fitting**: Avoid heavy parameter optimization on past data, as it creates unrealistic return expectations that fail in live trading [6].
* **Independent Execution**: Never alter mechanical signals during live execution; allow automated execution to remove trader emotion [3, 4].

## Part 2: Continuous Futures Contracts

### Core Concepts & Definitions
* **Limited Life Span Problem**: Individual futures contracts expire after a short duration (typically 1.5 years or less), creating low volume near expiration and artificial price gaps when changing from expiring contracts to new contracts [21-24].
* **Continuous Futures Contract**: Synthetic long-term price series constructed by combining data from multiple contract months to eliminate price rollover gaps and liquidity distortions for multi-year charting and computer system testing [24, 25].

---

### Contract Types & Construction Methods

#### 1. Nearest Contract
* **Definition**: Merges consecutive active front-month futures contracts to form an uninterrupted historical record using actual price data [23, 26].
* **Rollover Mechanics**: Rollover occurs automatically at contract expiration or a set number of days (\\(N\\)) prior to expiration (e.g., `TRNE00`, `TRNE05`, `TRNE12`, `TRNE15`, `TRNE21` representing \\(0, 5, 12, 15, \text{ or } 21\\) days prior to expiration) [23, 27].

#### 2. Next Contract
* **Definition**: Tracks the contract month immediately trailing the active Nearest Contract [28].
* **Variants**:
  * *Next-1 (`TRNXT1`)*: Always uses data from the contract one cycle behind the Nearest Contract (e.g., March data when Nearest uses December) [28, 29].
  * *Next-2 (`TRNXT2`)*: Always uses data from the contract two cycles behind the Nearest Contract (e.g., June data when Nearest uses December) [29].

#### 3. Gann Contract
* **Definition**: Links data for a specific calendar month across consecutive years, rolling forward only to the identical contract month of the following year (favored by W.D. Gann) [24, 30].
* **Examples**: July Wheat (`W07GN`), April Gold (`GC04GN`), December Japanese Yen (`JY12GN`) [24].

#### 4. Spread-Adjusted Continuous Contracts
* **Definition**: Adjusts historical prices by adding or subtracting the price difference (spread) between expiring and new contracts at rollover dates to remove price jumps [10, 24].

#### 5. Constant Forward Continuous Contracts (Perpetual Contract™)
* **Definition**: Developed by Robert Pelletier (Commodity Systems, Inc.), this method constructs a continuous price series targeting a constant forward time horizon (e.g., 12 weeks or 6 months into the future) using a weighted average of two surrounding contracts [31-33].
* **Configuration Parameters**:
  * Commodity Ticker Symbol (e.g., `TR` for T-Bonds) [34, 35].
  * Number of contracts used in calculation (typically 2 to 4 contracts) [34, 35].
  * Number of weeks projected into the future (typically 3 to 40 weeks) [34, 35].

---

### Mathematical Formulas & Extrapolation Mechanics

For a **Constant Forward Continuous Contract** using the 2 nearest contracts (\\(P_1\\) expiring at date \\(T_1\\), and \\(P_2\\) expiring at date \\(T_2\\)) to project a price \\(P_{\text{target}}\\) at target date \\(T_{\text{target}}\\):

\\[\text{Slope } (m) = \frac{P_2 - P_1}{T_2 - T_1}\\]

\\[P_{\text{target}} = P_1 + m \times (T_{\text{target}} - T_1)\\]

* *Variables*:
  * \\(P_1\\) = Closing price of the nearest contract expiring at \\(T_1\\) [33].
  * \\(P_2\\) = Closing price of the second contract expiring at \\(T_2\\) [33].
  * \\(T_{\text{target}}\\) = Target date defined by adding specified forward weeks to current date \\(T_{\text{today}}\\) [33, 36].

---

### Confirmation Rules & Trading Rules
* **Volume-Based Rollover Rule**: Rollover from front-month to back-month contracts should be executed when open interest and daily volume in the expiring contract begin to decline/erode (typically 10 to 15 days prior to expiration) [23, 27, 35].
* **Application Rule**:
  * **Visual Charting**: Standard Nearest-month continuation charts published by commercial services are sufficient for visual technical analysis [25].
  * **System Testing**: System testing **must** use Constant Forward / Perpetual Contracts or spread-adjusted continuous contracts; unadjusted nearest contracts introduce false rollover price gaps that trigger erratic signals in computer back-tests [24, 25].

### Author Case Study: T-Bond Constant Forward Continuous Contract (`TRCF212`)
* **Setup**:
  * Commodity: Treasury Bonds (`TR`) [33].
  * Model Configuration: 2 nearest contracts (`CF2`), 12 weeks forward time horizon (`12`) [33].
  * Date: December 1 [33].
  * Data Points:
    * December Contract Price (\\(P_1\\)): \\(88.25\\) [33].
    * March Contract Price (\\(P_2\\)): \\(87.75\\) [33].
    * Target Date (\\(T_{\text{target}}\\)): February 25 (12 weeks forward from Dec 1) [33].
* **Execution & Result**:
  * Linear extrapolation between the December (\\(88.25\\)) and March (\\(87.75\\)) prices at the February 25 target intersection generates a calculated Continuous Contract closing price of **\\(87.91\\)** [33, 36].
  * Because Feb 25 is chronologically closer to the March expiration date than December, the March contract price receives greater weighting in the mathematical calculation [36].

For example, a November soybean continuation chart would combine only the historic data provided by each successive year's November soybean contract. (This technique of linking specific delivery months was favored by W.D. Gann.) Some chartists go even further by averaging the prices of several contracts, or constructing indices that attempt to smooth the changeover by making adjustments in the price premium or discount."
