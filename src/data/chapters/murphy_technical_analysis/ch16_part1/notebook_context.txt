### 1. The Three Elements of Successful Trading

Every successful trading program relies on three foundational components: **Price Forecasting**, **Trading Tactics (Timing)**, and **Money Management** [1, 2]. 

* **Price Forecasting (What to Do)**: Indicates the direction in which a market is expected to trend [2, 3]. It is the critical first step in determining whether a trader is bullish or bearish and whether to enter from the long or short side [2]. If the price forecast is incorrect, no subsequent tactic will produce profitable results [2].
* **Trading Tactics / Timing (When to Do It)**: Determines specific entry and exit points [2, 3]. Timing is almost entirely technical in nature [2]. Because futures contracts feature low margin requirements (typically less than 10%) and high leverage, a relatively small price move against a position can force liquidation [2, 4]. A trader can be correct on the overall direction of the market and still lose money if market entry timing is flawed [2, 4].
* **Money Management (How Much to Commit)**: Governs the allocation of capital across trades and portfolio assets [3]. It includes portfolio makeup, diversification, position sizing, protective stop placement, reward-to-risk calculations, and strategy adjustments following winning or losing streaks [3]. Money management directly addresses account survival, ensuring a trader survives losing periods to profit over the long run [5].

### 2. Capital Preservation Rules

Money management guidelines exist to protect capital during drawdowns and prevent over-exposure to correlated markets [6, 7].

#### Position Sizing Formulas & Account Allocation Limits
* **Total Invested Capital Limit (50% Rule)**:
  \\[\text{Total Invested Capital} \le 0.50 \times \text{Total Account Equity}\\]
  *At any given time, no more than 50% of total capital should be committed to active market positions [6]. The remaining 50% is held in risk-free reserves, such as Treasury Bills, to absorb drawdowns during adverse periods [6].*
  * **Author Example**: In a **\$100,000 account**, a maximum of **\$50,000** is available for active trading margin, while **\$50,000** remains in T-Bills as a protective buffer [6].
* **Single Market Commitment Limit (10–15% Rule)**:
  \\[\text{Margin Deposit per Market} \le (0.10 \text{ to } 0.15) \times \text{Total Account Equity}\\]
  *Prevents over-concentrating capital into a single asset [8].*
  * **Author Example**: In a **\$100,000 account**, only **\$10,000 to \$15,000** may be committed as margin deposit for any single market position [8].
* **Maximum Risk Limit per Trade (5% Rule)**:
  \\[\text{Maximum Risk per Trade} = (\text{Entry Price} - \text{Stop Price}) \times \text{Position Size} \le 0.05 \times \text{Total Account Equity}\\]
  *Defines the maximum allowable loss if the trade fails [8]. This rule dictates contract sizing and the maximum allowable distance for protective stop-loss orders [8, 9].*
  * **Author Example**: On a **\$100,000 account**, a trader must never risk more than **\$5,000** on a single trade [8, 9].
* **Market Group Commitment Limit (20–25% Rule)**:
  \\[\text{Total Margin in Correlated Group} \le (0.20 \text{ to } 0.25) \times \text{Total Account Equity}\\]
  *Controls exposure across correlated asset classes [7].*
  * **Author Example**: Markets within the same group move together—such as Gold and Silver in precious metals, or multiple foreign currency futures against the U.S. Dollar [7, 10]. Holding full positions across multiple correlated markets violates diversification principles [7, 10].

#### Rules for Protective Stops & Pyramiding
* **Support and Resistance Grounding**: Protective stops must always be anchored beyond valid technical support (for long positions) or technical resistance (for short positions), rather than placed arbitrarily [9, 11, 12]. 
* **Avoid Obvious Round Numbers**: Do not place stop orders on obvious round numbers (e.g., \$300, \$400, \$500, or 1000-point index benchmarks) [13, 14]. Place protective sell stops for long positions *just below* round numbers, and buy stops for short positions *just above* round numbers [13, 15].
* **Handling Margin Calls**: **Never meet a margin call**—do not throw good money after bad [16].
* **Position Liquidation**: Always close out losing positions before closing out winning positions [16].
* **Pyramiding Guidelines (Adding Positions)**:
  1. Each successive layer of positions added to a trade must be **smaller than the preceding layer** [16].
  2. Add units **only to winning positions**; never add to a losing position [16].
  3. Trail protective stop-loss orders to the **breakeven point** as new layers are added [16].

### 3. Reward-to-Risk Ratios (Minimum 3-to-1)

Because futures trading involves low margin requirements, minor adverse market moves can trigger stops [17]. Even the most successful futures traders make money on only **40% of their trades** [17]. To remain profitable when losing most trades, the average dollar magnitude of winning trades must substantially exceed the average magnitude of losing trades [17, 18].

#### Mathematical Formula & Minimum Ratio
\\[\text{Reward-to-Risk Ratio} = \frac{\text{Potential Profit Objective (Reward)}}{\text{Potential Dollar Risk (Risk)}} \ge 3:1\\]

\\[\text{Potential Reward} = |\text{Target Price Objective} - \text{Entry Price}|\\]

\\[\text{Potential Risk} = |\text{Entry Price} - \text{Protective Stop Price}|\\]

A potential trade must offer a minimum expected profit objective at least **three times** the potential risk before execution is considered [18, 19].

#### Chart Patterns & Price Objectives for Calculating Reward
Price objectives (the reward numerator) are established using technical measuring rules across chart patterns, trendlines, and retracements [18, 20]:
* **Head and Shoulders**: Measure the vertical distance from the Head to the Neckline, and project that exact distance vertically from the Neckline breakout point [21, 22].
* **Triangles (Symmetrical, Ascending, Descending)**: Measure the vertical height of the widest part of the base on the left, and project that vertical distance from the breakout point [23].
* **Flags and Pennants**: Measure the vertical height of the preceding steep advance or decline ("flagpole") from its initial breakout point, and project that distance from the breakout point of the flag/pennant [24].
* **Trendlines & Channels**: Project a price distance beyond a broken trendline equal to the vertical distance achieved on the opposite side prior to the trend reversal [25]. In trend channels, the upper channel line serves as a profit-taking objective for long positions [26].
* **Percentage Retracements**: Expect corrective targets or entry zones at **33–38%** (minimum retracement), **50%**, and **62–66%** (maximum retracement) of the prior trend [27-29].

#### Trading & Confirmation Rules
* **Filter Rule**: Reject any trade where the distance to the technical price target yields a reward-to-risk ratio lower than 3-to-1 [18, 19].
* **Execution Rule**: Fine-tune entry points near support levels or pullbacks (40–60% retracement zones) to keep the stop loss tight, thereby reducing the denominator (Risk) and expanding the overall ratio [9, 11, 29, 30].

### 4. Trending vs. Trading Units

Trading a single position unit creates an operational dilemma: taking profits early at initial technical resistance risks missing a prolonged major trend, whereas holding through corrections risks surrendering accumulated paper profits [31, 32]. 

To resolve this, traders should divide holdings into **Multiple Position Units**: **Trending Units** and **Trading Units** [32].

```
                     ┌────────────────────────────────────────┐
                     │       TOTAL POSITION COMMITMENT        │
                     └───────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                                         │
                   ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
                   │                                           │
         ┌─────────▼─────────┐                       ┌─────────▼─────────┐
         │  TRENDING UNITS   │                       │   TRADING UNITS   │
         └─────────┬─────────┘                       └─────────┬─────────┘
                   │                                           │
   • Held for the long pull                    • In-and-out short-term trading
   • Uses wide / loose stops                   • Uses tight stops / profit targets
   • Rides out major corrections               • Locks in profits at overbought levels
   • Generates largest profits                 • Reinstated when trend resumes
```

#### Operational Rules for Position Units
* **Trending Units**:
  * Earmarked for the long pull to capture major market moves [32].
  * Managed with **looser protective stops** to allow the market room to consolidate or undergo percentage retracements without getting stopped out [32].
  * Account for the largest accumulated profits over time [32].
* **Trading Units**:
  * Earmarked for short-term, in-and-out tactical trading [32].
  * Closed out or locked in with **tight protective stops** when prices reach initial resistance, hit chart objectives, or enter overbought oscillator conditions [32, 33].
  * Reinstated on corrective dips once the broader trend resumes [33].

#### Staged Breakout Tactics Using Multiple Units
When entering a new market breakout, multiple units enable a three-stage entry tactic that balances price and probability [34, 35]:
1. **Unit 1 (Anticipation)**: Initiate a small probe position near support prior to the breakout for a lower entry price [34, 35].
2. **Unit 2 (Breakout)**: Add a second unit on the actual closing breakout above resistance [34, 35].
3. **Unit 3 (Pullback)**: Add a final unit on the initial corrective dip/pullback following the breakout [34, 35].

### 5. Handling Winning and Losing Streaks

A trader's performance track record consists of a series of equity peaks and troughs, resembling a price chart [36]. Long-term success requires managing capital size relative to these equity fluctuations [36].

```
  Account Equity
        │
        │           Equity Peak (Do NOT increase size!)
        │              /\
        │             /  \    Drawdown / Equity Dip
        │   /\       /    \   (Increase commitments HERE)
        │  /  \     /      \  /\
        │ /    \   /        \/  \
        │/      \/               \
        └───────────────────────────► Time
```

#### Rules for Winning Streaks
* **The Overbought Equity Paradox**: Increasing position size immediately following a winning streak is the worst tactical error a trader can make—it is the direct equivalent of buying into an overbought market at a price peak [36].
* **Capital Destruction Mechanics**: If a trader doubles position sizing at an equity peak, the inevitable subsequent losing period will wipe out 100% of accumulated profits rather than a normal percentage drawdown [36].
* **Rule**: Maintain consistent position sizing during winning streaks; never scale up commitments at an equity peak [36].

#### Rules for Losing Streaks (Adversity & Drawdowns)
* **The Math of Recovery (50% Drawdown Rule)**:
  \\[\text{Equity Loss} = 50\% \implies \text{Required Recovery Gain} = 100\%\\]
  *If a trader loses 50% of trading equity, remaining funds must double (100% gain) just to return to the original breakeven level [33].*
* **The Equity Trough Strategy**: Counterintuitively, the optimal time to increase market commitments is **after a dip or drawdown in equity** (near an equity trough) [36]. This ensures heavier position commitments align with equity bottoms rather than equity peaks [36].
* **Rule During Adversity**: Do not overreact to drawdowns by recklessly over-leveraging or drastically altering proven strategies out of desperation [33]. Rely on the 50% T-Bill capital reserve to survive adverse streaks intact [6].

The Breaking of Trendlines This is one of the most useful early entry or exit signals. If the trader is looking to enter a new position on a technical sign of a trend change or a reason to exit an old position, the breaking of a tight trendline is often an excellent action signal. Other technical factors must, of course, always be considered. Trendlines can also be used for entry points when they act as support or resistance. Buying against a major up trendline or selling against a down trendline can be an effective timing strategy."
- "A more pleasant dilemma occurs after a winning streak. What do you do with your winnings? Suppose you've doubled your money. One alternative is to put your money to maximum use by doubling the size of your positions. If you do that, however, what will happen during the inevitable losing period that's sure to follow? Instead of giving back 50% of your winnings, you'll wind up giving it all back. So the answers to these two questions aren't as simple or obvious as they might first appear. Every trader's track record is a series of peaks and troughs, much like a price chart. The trend of the equity chart should be pointing upward if the trader is making money on balance. The worst time to increase the size of one's commitments is after a winning streak. That's much like buying into an overbought market in an uptrend. The wiser thing to do (which goes against basic human nature) is to begin increasing one's commitments after a dip in equity. This increases the odds that the heavier commitments will be made near the equity troughs instead of the peaks."
