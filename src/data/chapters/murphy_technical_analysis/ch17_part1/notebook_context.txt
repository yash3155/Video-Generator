### Intermarket Analysis Principles

Intermarket technical analysis is a branch of market analysis that evaluates price action across four interrelated financial asset classes: **currencies, commodities, bonds, and stocks**, alongside global international markets [1, 2]. The fundamental premise is that no individual market exists in isolation; all financial markets are linked and mutually dependent [1, 3].

#### Core Concepts & Sequential Linkage
* **The Intermarket Chain**: Financial movements follow a sequential ripple effect across asset classes: **U.S. Dollar \\(\rightarrow\\) Commodities \\(\rightarrow\\) Bonds \\(\rightarrow\\) Stocks** [4, 5]. 
* **Macro-to-Micro / Top-Down Market Approach**: Intermarket analysis provides a structured top-down framework for portfolio allocation and trading [6, 7]:
  1. **Overall Market Health**: Analyze major equity and fixed-income averages [7].
  2. **Sector & Group Selection**: Identify sectors and industry groups exhibiting superior relative strength driven by intermarket trends (e.g., inflation vs. disinflation environments) [7, 8].
  3. **Individual Asset Selection**: Select individual stocks or instruments showing the highest relative strength within top-performing groups [7].

#### Formulas & Quantitative Tools
* **Relative Strength (RS) Ratio Formula**:
  \\[\text{RS Ratio} = \frac{P_{\text{Asset A}}}{P_{\text{Asset B}}}\\]
  * **Variables**: \\(P_{\text{Asset A}}\\) = Price of Numerator asset/sector/stock; \\(P_{\text{Asset B}}\\) = Price of Denominator benchmark/asset [9, 10].
  * **Trading Rule**: A rising RS ratio line indicates Asset A is outperforming Asset B (buy Asset A / sell Asset B) [8-10]. A falling RS ratio line indicates Asset B is outperforming Asset A [9, 10].
* **Intermarket Correlation Indicator**:
  * Measures the degree of directional co-movement between two assets on a scale from \\(+1.0\\) (perfect positive correlation) to \\(-1.0\\) (perfect negative correlation), with \\(0.0\\) indicating no correlation [11].
  * **Rule**: Place greater technical weight on relationships displaying high positive or high negative correlation readings [11].
* **Program Trading Arbitrage (S&P 500 Cash vs. Futures)**:
  * **Fair Value Calculation**: S&P 500 futures normally trade at a premium to the S&P 500 cash index based on short-term interest rates, dividend yield on the S&P 500, and days to contract expiration [12].
  * **Arbitrage Buy Rule (Program Buying)**: When the futures premium moves significantly *above* Fair Value, automated systems sell futures and buy a basket of S&P 500 cash stocks, pushing stock prices higher [12].
  * **Arbitrage Sell Rule (Program Selling)**: When the futures premium falls significantly *below* Fair Value, systems buy futures and sell the basket of cash stocks, driving stock prices lower [12]. Sudden moves in S&P 500 futures triggering program trades are frequently initiated by movements in Treasury Bond futures [12, 13].

### Link Between Bonds and Stocks

#### Core Concepts & Traditional Relationship
* **Yield & Price Mechanics**: Bond prices move inversely to interest rates (yields); rising bond prices signify falling interest rates [14].
* **Non-Deflationary / Inflationary Regime**: Under standard economic conditions, bond prices and stock prices share a **positive correlation** [11, 14]. Rising bond prices (falling interest rates) reduce borrowing costs and raise equity valuation multiples, acting as a major bullish driver for stocks [14, 15]. Conversely, falling bond prices (rising interest rates) act as a bearish drag on equities [14].
* **Leading Indicator Rule**: Treasury Bond futures act as a leading indicator for stock market major trend reversals [14].

#### Leading Intermarket Signals & Sector Relationships
* **Utility Stocks as Bond Predictor**: Utility stocks (e.g., PHLX Utility Index) are highly interest-rate sensitive and closely tied to T-Bond prices [16, 17]. Historically, utility stock turning points often lead T-Bond price turns, making utilities a leading indicator for bonds [17, 18].
* **Sector Rotation Rules**:
  * **Strong Bonds / Weak Commodities**: Capital rotates into interest-rate-sensitive stock sectors (**utilities, financials, consumer staples**) [16, 19].
  * **Weak Bonds / Strong Commodities**: Capital rotates into inflation-sensitive and commodity-linked sectors (**gold miners, energy, cyclicals**) [16, 19].

#### Author Case Studies & Historical Examples
* **Major Turns (1981–1995)**: Major bottoms in Treasury Bonds in 1981, 1984, 1988, 1991, and 1995 directly preceded major bull market advances in the stock market [14, 15].
* **Major Warnings (1987–1994)**: Major tops in Treasury Bonds in 1987, 1990, and 1994 warned of upcoming stock market downturns or multi-month corrections [14, 15].

### Inverse Link Between Commodities and Bonds

#### Core Concepts & Inflation Mechanics
* **Inflation Driving Force**: Treasury Bond prices are heavily dictated by inflation expectations [15]. Broad commodity prices serve as primary leading indicators of inflationary pressures [15, 20].
* **Negative Correlation Rule**: Commodities and bond prices trend in **opposite directions** [11, 15]. 
  * **Rising Commodity Prices**: Signal accelerating price inflation \\(\rightarrow\\) causes bond prices to drop and interest rates to rise [15].
  * **Falling Commodity Prices**: Signal disinflation/deflation \\(\rightarrow\\) causes bond prices to rally and interest rates to drop [15, 21].

#### Technical Ratios & Chart Patterns
* **CRB / T-Bond Ratio**:
  \\[\text{CRB/Bond Ratio} = \frac{\text{CRB Futures Price Index}}{\text{Treasury Bond Futures Price}}\\]
  * **Breakout / Trendline Rules**: Applying trendline analysis and moving averages directly to the CRB/Bond ratio line signals key structural macro shifts [8-10]:
    * A uptrending CRB/Bond ratio signals an inflationary environment favoring commodities and cyclical equities over fixed income [9, 10].
    * A downtrending ratio signals a disinflationary/deflationary environment favoring Treasury bonds and defensive equities [9, 10, 19].

#### Author Case Studies & Historical Examples
* **Spring 1996 & Spring 1997 Peaks**: Major peaks in the Commodity Research Bureau (CRB) Index in the spring of 1996 and spring of 1997 coincided directly with major price bottoms in Treasury Bonds, confirming their strong negative correlation [15, 22].

### Link Between the Dollar and Commodities/Gold

#### Core Concepts & Currency Impact
* **Dollar Impact on Commodities**: A rising U.S. Dollar Index (UDX) exerts downward pressure on commodity prices, acting as a non-inflationary or disinflationary force [22, 23]. A declining U.S. Dollar boosts commodity prices [22, 24].
* **Gold-Dollar Inverse Relationship**: Gold prices and the U.S. Dollar move in opposite directions [22, 23]. 
* **Intermarket Sequence**: Gold acts as a leading indicator for the broader commodity market, while gold mining equities act as a leading indicator for gold bullion prices [4, 18, 22].

#### Dollar Impact on Equity Capitalization (Large vs. Small Caps)
* **Strong U.S. Dollar**: Disadvantages large multinational corporations (making export goods expensive overseas) while favoring domestically focused small-cap equities [25-27].
  * **Trading Rule**: In a strong dollar environment, favor small-cap indices (**Russell 2000**) over large-cap multinational indices (**Dow Jones Industrials / S&P 500**) [25-27].
* **Weak U.S. Dollar**: Benefits large multinational corporations by lowering foreign purchase prices, favoring large-cap equities [25-27].

#### Author Case Studies & Historical Examples
* **1980 & 1995 Turns**: A major low in the U.S. Dollar Index in 1980 coincided with a historical peak in gold and commodities [23]. Similarly, a dollar bottom in 1995 led to a sharp collapse in broad commodity prices a year later [23].

### Deflation Scenarios & Market Decoupling

#### Core Concepts & Macro Shifts
* **Disinflation vs. Deflation**: Disinflation refers to prices rising at a slower pace (beneficial for both bonds and stocks) [21, 28]. Deflation refers to absolute falling price levels, which fundamentally alters traditional intermarket correlations [21, 28].
* **Decoupling Rule (Bonds vs. Stocks)**: In a true deflationary scenario, the positive correlation between bonds and stocks breaks down (decouples) [15, 21, 28]:
  * **Commodities vs. Bonds**: The inverse relationship remains intact — commodity prices plunge while bond prices rally strongly [21, 28].
  * **Bonds vs. Stocks**: Stocks react **negatively** to deflation due to collapsing corporate earnings and systemic credit risks, decoupling from rising bond prices [21, 28].

```
Macro Environment Matrix:

  Asset Class        Inflation Trend        Disinflation Trend       Deflation Trend
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Commodities            Rising                   Falling                Plunging
  Treasury Bonds         Falling                  Rising                 Rising
  Stocks                 Mixed / Sector Specific  Rising (Bullish)       Falling (Bearish)
  Key Rotation           Oils, Gold, Cyclicals    Tech, Growth, Equities T-Bonds, Utilities, Cash
``` [16, 19, 21, 28]

#### Asset Allocation Rules in Deflation
* **Asset Shifts**: Capital moves out of commodities, equities, and cyclical stocks into Treasury bonds, high-quality cash equivalents, and consumer staples [19, 21, 28].
* **Intermarket Confirmation**: A severe, multi-month drop in the CRB/Bond ratio breaks support levels and confirms deflationary stress, signaling a defensive portfolio stance [9, 19, 29].

#### Author Case Studies & Historical Examples
* **1997–1998 Asian Currency & Global Contagion**: 
  * The collapse of Asian currencies in mid-1997 triggered global price deflation that spread to Russia and Latin America in 1998 [21, 30].
  * Key industrial and precious commodities (**copper, gold, crude oil**) experienced severe crashes [21, 31].
  * Commodity-exporting countries (Australia, Canada, Mexico, Russia) suffered severe equity bear markets [30].
  * **Decoupling Demonstration**: U.S. Producer Prices fell on an annual basis [21]. Global investors fled equities (causing stock drops and capital rotation out of cyclicals into consumer staples), while Treasury Bond prices surged to record highs [19, 21, 30, 32].
