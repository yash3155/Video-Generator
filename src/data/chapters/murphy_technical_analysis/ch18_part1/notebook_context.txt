### 1. Measuring Market Breadth

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Market breadth indicators measure the internal health of the overall stock market by analyzing the collective participation of individual stocks rather than relying solely on major price averages like the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) or S&P 500 **[1, 2]**. This forms part of a top-down market analysis approach, which begins with assessing overall market health before analyzing specific sectors and individual stocks **[1]**.
* **Data Components**: Breadth analysis relies on daily or weekly data published by exchanges (such as the NYSE, NASDAQ, or AMEX) comprising **[1, 3]**:
  1. Advancing vs. Declining issues **[1, 3]**.
  2. New 52-week Highs vs. New 52-week Lows **[1, 3]**.
  3. Upside (Advancing) Volume vs. Downside (Declining) Volume **[1, 3]**.
* **Author Case Study / Data Example**: A sample dataset from *The Wall Street Journal* shows a day where the DJIA gained 12.20 points, yet declining issues (1,559) outnumbered advancing issues (1,327), and downside volume (279.5 million shares) exceeded upside volume (248.2 million shares) **[3]**. This illustrated negative market breadth despite a positive closing price in the Dow **[3]**.

---

### 2. Advance-Decline (AD) Line and AD Divergence

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: The Advance-Decline (AD) line is the primary market breadth indicator **[4]**. It evaluates whether the broader market of listed stocks (e.g., ~3,500 NYSE stocks) is advancing in harmony with narrow capitalization-weighted market indices **[4, 5]**. In Wall Street terminology, it tests whether the "troops" are keeping up with the "generals" **[5]**.
* **Formulas & Variables**:
  * **Daily Net Advances**: 
    \\[\text{Net Advances} = A - D\\]
    *Where \\(A\\) = Number of Advancing Issues, \\(D\\) = Number of Declining Issues* **[4, 6]**.
  * **Cumulative AD Line**: 
    \\[\text{AD Line}_t = \text{AD Line}_{t-1} + (A - D)_t\\]
    *Where \\((A - D)_t\\) is added if positive or subtracted if negative* **[4, 6]**.
  * **AD Ratio Variation**: 
    \\[\text{AD Ratio} = \frac{A}{D}\\]
    *Used to prevent distortion caused by the total number of listed exchange stocks growing over time* **[7]**.
* **Trading & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Trend Confirmation**: In a healthy bull market, both the AD line and major averages move upward together in positive alignment **[4, 5]**.
  * **AD Divergence**: Occurs when major stock averages reach new highs while the AD line fails to confirm and makes a lower peak **[5]**. Historically, the AD line peaks well ahead of market averages, serving as an early warning of a market top **[5]**.
  * **Daily vs. Weekly AD Lines**: Daily AD lines measure short- to intermediate-term trends **[8]**. A weekly AD line (measuring weekly net advances) must confirm a divergence to signal a major multi-year trend reversal **[8]**.

### 3. McClellan Oscillator and Summation Index

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Developed by Sherman McClellan, the McClellan Oscillator is an overbought/oversold momentum indicator derived from exponential moving averages of daily net advances **[9]**. The McClellan Summation Index is its cumulative version used for long-term breadth analysis **[10]**.
* **Formulas & Variables**:
  * **McClellan Oscillator**: 
    \\[\text{McClellan Oscillator} = \text{EMA}_{19}(A - D) - \text{EMA}_{39}(A - D)\\]
    *Where \\(\text{EMA}_{19}\\) represents a 10% smoothing factor (19-day EMA) and \\(\text{EMA}_{39}\\) represents a 5% smoothing factor (39-day EMA) of daily net advances \\((A - D)\\)* **[9]**.
  * **McClellan Summation Index**: 
    \\[\text{Summation Index}_t = \text{Summation Index}_{t-1} + \text{McClellan Oscillator}_t\\]
    *Cumulative sum of daily McClellan Oscillator values* **[10]**.
* **Range & Extremes**:
  * The McClellan Oscillator fluctuates around a **zero line** **[9]**.
  * **Overbought Level**: Readings above \\(+100\\) **[9]**.
  * **Oversold Level**: Readings below \\(-100\\) **[9]**.
* **Trading & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Oscillator Signals**: Zero-line crossovers generate short- to intermediate-term buy (crossing above zero) and sell (crossing below zero) signals **[9]**.
  * **Summation Index Signals**: Zero-line crossovers on the Summation Index indicate major bull or bear market trend turns **[10, 11]**.
* **Author Case Study**: During the stock market pullbacks in October 1997, the McClellan Oscillator plunged well below \\(-100\\) (reaching near \\(-250\\)), identifying an extreme oversold condition that preceded a major bounce **[10]**.

### 4. New Highs vs. New Lows Index

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Tracks market expansion and leadership by comparing the number of stocks reaching new 52-week highs against those hitting new 52-week lows **[11]**. Dr. Alexander Elder describes the New High-New Low Index as "probably the best leading indicator of the stock market" **[12]**.
* **Calculations & Display Methods**:
  * **Smoother Dual Lines**: Plotted as separate 10-day moving averages of New Highs and New Lows **[11, 13]**.
  * **Net High-Low Line**: 
    \\[\text{Net High-Low} = \text{New Highs} - \text{New Lows}\\]
    *Plotted directly against major indices or as a histogram centered at zero* **[13-15]**.
* **Trading & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Bullish Market**: A healthy market requires New Highs to exceed New Lows **[11, 13]**.
  * **Crossover Sell Signal**: Generated when the 10-day MA of New Lows crosses above the 10-day MA of New Highs **[11, 13]**.
  * **Divergence Signal**: If a major stock index hits a new peak but the New High-New Low Index forms a lower peak, it signals underlying weakness in broader participation **[12]**.
* **Author Case Study**: In October 1997, the 10-day moving average of New Lows temporarily expanded to test the 10-day average of New Highs, resulting in a sharp drop in the High-Low line before bullish alignment reasserted itself **[13, 14]**.

### 5. Upside vs. Downside Volume

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Gauges volume flow by comparing the trading volume in advancing stocks against the trading volume in declining stocks **[15, 16]**.
* **Calculation & Interpretation**:
  * Calculated either by plotting two separate 10-day moving average lines (Up Volume vs. Down Volume) or by plotting the net differential (\\(\text{Up Volume} - \text{Down Volume}\\)) **[15, 16]**.
  * **Bullish Alignment**: Upside volume consistently exceeds downside volume **[15, 16]**.
  * **Bearish Alignment**: Downside volume consistently exceeds upside volume **[15, 16]**.

### 6. Arms Index (TRIN)

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Created by Richard W. Arms, Jr., the Arms Index (also known as TRIN, for TRading INdex) is a ratio of ratios that measures whether volume is concentrated in advancing or declining stocks **[16, 17]**. It operates as a **contrary indicator**, moving in the opposite direction of stock prices **[17, 18]**.
* **Mathematical Formula & Variables**:
  \\[\text{Arms Index (TRIN)} = \frac{\left( \frac{\text{Advancing Issues}}{\text{Declining Issues}} \right)}{\left( \frac{\text{Advancing Volume}}{\text{Declining Volume}} \right)} = \frac{A / D}{V_A / V_D}\\]
  *Variables*:
  * \\(A\\) = Number of Advancing Issues **[17]**.
  * \\(D\\) = Number of Declining Issues **[17]**.
  * \\(V_A\\) = Volume of Advancing Issues **[17]**.
  * \\(V_D\\) = Volume of Declining Issues **[17]**.
* **Value Interpretation & Benchmark Readings**:
  * **Below 1.0**: Indicates greater volume per advancing stock than per declining stock (Bullish / Positive) **[17]**.
  * **Above 1.0**: Indicates greater volume per declining stock than per advancing stock (Bearish / Negative) **[17]**.
  * **10-day Moving Average Overbought/Oversold**:
    * **Oversold (Bullish turn)**: 10-day MA \\(> 1.20\\) **[18]**.
    * **Overbought (Bearish turn)**: 10-day MA \\(< 0.70\\) **[18]**.
  * **Intraday Extremes**: Sharp upward spikes in TRIN (e.g., above 2.0) signal extreme panic selling and often mark short-term bottoms **[17, 18]**.
* **Variations & Complementary Indicators**:
  * **TRIN vs. TICK**: TICK measures net intraday uptick vs. downtick transactions **[19]**. A rising TICK combined with a falling TRIN indicates strong intraday buying pressure **[19]**.
  * **Open Arms Index**: Smooths each of the four inputs (\\(A\\), \\(D\\), \\(V_A\\), \\(V_D\\)) over 10, 21, or 55 days *before* calculating the Arms ratio, providing a smoother trendline **[20]**.
  * **Fibonacci Smoothings**: Arms recommends applying 21-day and 55-day moving average crossovers for intermediate-term entry and exit signals **[18, 20]**.

### 7. Equivolume and Candlepower Charting

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**:
  * **Equivolume Charting**: Developed by Richard W. Arms, Jr. (*Volume Cycles in the Stock Market*, 1983), Equivolume replaces individual price bars and lower volume histograms with single price-volume rectangles **[21]**.
  * **Candlepower Charting**: Developed by Greg Morris (1990; referred to as *Candlevolume* in MetaStock), Candlepower combines Japanese Candlesticks with Equivolume formatting **[22]**.
* **Construction & Display Rules**:
  * **Equivolume Rectangles**:
    * **Height**: Measures the daily price range (\\(\text{High} - \text{Low}\\)) **[21]**.
    * **Width**: Represents trading volume (heavy volume produces wider rectangles; light volume produces narrower rectangles) **[21]**.
  * **Candlepower / Candlevolume**: Displays open, high, low, and close price dynamics via candlestick real bodies and shadows, while altering the horizontal width of each candle based on volume **[22, 23]**.
* **Confirmation & Trading Rules**:
  * **Breakout Rule**: A valid price breakout must be confirmed by a noticeably **wider rectangle/candle**, reflecting high volume **[24, 25]**.
  * **Trend Rules**: In an uptrend, advancing days should display wide rectangles/candles, while pullbacks should display narrow rectangles/candles **[25]**.
  * **Warning Signal**: Exceptionally wide rectangles/candles on down days indicate selling pressure and distribution **[24]**.
* **Author Case Study**: Murphy demonstrates Equivolume and Candlepower charts using daily price data for **Intel** from late 1997 to early 1998, where a series of wide rectangles during down days clearly signaled major institutional distribution prior to a price drop **[24, 26]**.

On an Equivolume chart, therefore, a bullish price breakout should be accompanied by a noticeably wider rectangle. Equivolume charting combines price and volume analysis into one chart and makes for much easier comparisons between price and volume. In an uptrend, for example, up days should see wider rectangles while down days should see narrower rectangles. Equivolume charting can be applied to market averages as well as individual stocks and can be plotted for both daily and weekly charts. For more information, consult Volume Cycles in the Stock Market by Richard Arms (Dow Jones-Irwin, 1983)."
