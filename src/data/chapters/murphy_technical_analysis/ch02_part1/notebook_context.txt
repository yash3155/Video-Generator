### Charles Dow's Background & Historical Foundation

* **Founding & Origins**: Charles Dow and his partner Edward Jones founded **Dow Jones & Company in 1882** [1]. Dow set down his core principles regarding market behavior in a series of editorials for *The Wall Street Journal*, which he co-founded [1, 2].
* **Evolution of the Averages**:
  * **July 3, 1884**: Dow published the first stock market average consisting of **11 stocks** (9 railroad companies and 2 manufacturing firms) [3].
  * **1897**: Dow divided the market into two distinct indices: a **12-stock Industrial index** and a **20-stock Rail index** to better reflect economic health [3].
  * **1928**: The Industrial index was expanded to **30 stocks** [3].
  * **1929**: A **Utility index** was added to the averages [3].
* **Historical Codification**: Dow never wrote a formal book on his theory before his death in 1902 [2]. S.A. Nelson compiled Dow's editorials in **1903 (*The ABC of Stock Speculation*)**, coining the term **"Dow's Theory"** [2]. William Peter Hamilton expanded these concepts in **1922 (*The Stock Market Barometer*)**, followed by Robert Rhea's definitive work in **1932 (*The Dow Theory*)** [2].
* **Economic Purpose**: Dow designed stock market averages primarily as a **leading economic indicator** of general business conditions rather than a tool to forecast individual stock prices [4].

---

### The 6 Basic Tenets of Dow Theory

1. **The Averages Discount Everything**  
   The market action and index averages reflect the sum total of all Wall Street's knowledge regarding past, current, and anticipated events [5, 6]. Supply and demand forces automatically assimilate all fundamental factors, market sentiment, and even unforeseen **"acts of God"** (such as natural disasters) into the price action almost instantaneously [6].
2. **The Market Has Three Trends**  
   Market trends follow the law of action and reaction [7]. An **uptrend** is defined as a series of successively higher peaks and higher troughs [8]. A **downtrend** is defined as a series of lower peaks and lower troughs [8]. These trends are classified into three degrees: **Primary, Secondary, and Minor** [7].
3. **Major Trends Have Three Phases**  
   Primary bull and bear markets progress through three sequential phases: **Accumulation, Public Participation, and Distribution** [9].
4. **The Averages Must Confirm Each Other**  
   No major bull or bear market signal is valid unless both the **Dow Jones Industrial Average** and the **Dow Jones Transportation Average** generate matching signals by penetrating prior secondary peaks or troughs [10]. Signals do not need to occur simultaneously, but a shorter delay between them provides stronger confirmation [10].
5. **Volume Must Confirm the Trend**  
   Volume is a critical secondary indicator [11]. Trading activity should **expand in the direction of the primary trend**: in an uptrend, volume increases as prices rise and contracts on dips; in a downtrend, volume increases as prices drop and shrinks on rallies [11].
6. **A Trend Is Assumed to Be in Effect Until It Gives Definite Signals That It Has Reversed**  
   Based on physical laws of momentum, an existing market trend tends to remain in motion until an external force generates a clear reversal signal [12].

Dow illustrated market movements using the analogy of ocean dynamics [7]:

     ▼             ▼                       ▼             ▼

  < 3 Wks       < 3 Wks                 < 3 Wks       < 3 Wks

* **Primary Trend (The Tide)**:
  * **Definition**: The principal direction of the market representing major bull or bear movements [7, 9].
  * **Duration**: Lasts **more than one year** (often several years) in stock markets [13]. In futures markets, major trends are shortened to anything **over six months** [14].
* **Secondary / Intermediate Trend (The Waves)**:
  * **Definition**: Counter-trend corrections occurring within the primary move [7, 13].
  * **Duration**: Lasts **three weeks to three months** [13, 14].
  * **Retracement Depth**: Typically retraces between **\\(33.3\%\\) and \\(66.6\%\\)** (\\(\frac{1}{3}\\) to \\(\frac{2}{3}\\)) of the preceding primary advance or decline, most frequently reaching a **\\(50\%\\) retracement** [13].
* **Minor / Near-Term Trend (The Ripples)**:
  * **Definition**: Short-term fluctuations within the intermediate wave [7, 13].
  * **Duration**: Lasts **less than three weeks** [13, 14].
  * **Purpose**: Used primarily for **trade execution and timing** [15, 16].

---

### The 3 Phases of Major Trends

* **Accumulation Phase**:
  * **Participants**: Informed, astute investors ("smart money") [9].
  * **Market Context**: Occurs at the tail end of a bear market when market sentiment is exceedingly depressed and bad economic news has been fully discounted [9].
* **Public Participation Phase**:
  * **Participants**: Trend-following technical traders and institutional participants [9].
  * **Market Context**: Characterized by rapidly advancing prices, improving business indicators, and widespread public interest [9].
* **Distribution Phase**:
  * **Participants**: Astute early accumulators who distribute (sell) their holdings to the general public [9].
  * **Market Context**: Accompanied by glowing media reports, peak economic indicators, heavy speculative volume, and widespread public participation [9].

### Quantitative Retracement Rules & Mathematical Ratios

Dow Theory uses specific percentage boundaries to measure secondary corrections [13]:

\\[\text{Retracement Range} = P_{\text{peak}} - k \cdot (P_{\text{peak}} - P_{\text{trough}})\\]

* **Variable Definitions**:
  * \\(P_{\text{peak}}\\) = Highest closing price of the prior primary wave [8, 13].
  * \\(P_{\text{trough}}\\) = Lowest closing price of the prior primary wave [8, 13].
  * \\(k\\) = Retracement fraction, where \\(k \in \left[\frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}\right]\\) (or \\(33.3\%\\) to \\(66.6\%\\)) [13].
  * **Benchmark Benchmark (\\(k = 0.50\\))**: A \\(50\%\\) retracement of the prior move represents the single most common correction distance [13].

*Note: Dow Theory does not employ computerized algorithmic formulas; buy and sell signals are strictly governed by closing price penetrations of prior secondary highs and lows [11, 17].*

### Chart Patterns & Reversal Structures

#### 1. Failure Swing Top / Bottom
* **Definition**: A trend reversal pattern where the market attempts to resume the primary trend but fails to cross the previous reaction peak/trough [17-19].
* **Pattern Structure**:
  * **Top**: Rally peak \\(A \rightarrow\\) Reaction drop \\(B \rightarrow\\) Rally peak \\(C\\) (fails to reach \\(A\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Downward penetration of level \\(B\\) (Signal point \\(B1\\)) [18, 19].
  * **Bottom**: Decline trough \\(A \rightarrow\\) Bounce peak \\(B \rightarrow\\) Decline trough \\(C\\) (holds above \\(A\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Upward breakout over level \\(B\\) (Signal point \\(B1\\)) [19].

#### 2. Nonfailure Swing Top / Bottom
* **Definition**: A stronger structural pattern where price exceeds the previous peak/trough before reversing through reaction levels [17-19].
* **Pattern Structure**:
  * **Top**: Rally peak \\(A \rightarrow\\) Reaction low \\(B \rightarrow\\) New peak \\(C\\) (exceeds \\(A\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Decline below \\(B\\) (low \\(D\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Weak rally to \\(E\\) (fails to reach \\(C\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Downward break below \\(D\\) (Signal point \\(S2\\)) [18].
  * **Bottom**: Decline low \\(A \rightarrow\\) Peak \\(B \rightarrow\\) New low \\(C\\) (below \\(A\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Rally to \\(D\\) (above \\(B\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Dip to \\(E\\) (above \\(C\\)) \\(\rightarrow\\) Breakout above \\(D\\) (Signal point \\(B2\\)) [18, 19].

#### 3. Lines (Horizontal Consolidations)
* **Definition**: Sideways trading ranges where price fluctuations stay within a narrow horizontal band [17, 19, 20].
* **Function**: Represents a state of equilibrium between supply and demand [20]. A breakout above the upper line signals trend continuation or upside reversal; a breakdown below the lower line signals a downside break [19].

---

### Confirmation Rules & Volume Rules

* **Intermarket Confirmation Rule**: A primary trend change is valid **only when both the Industrial and Transportation (Rail) Averages confirm each other** by closing beyond their respective secondary peaks or reaction lows [10]. If one index sets a new high while the other fails to do so, a **divergence** exists, warning that the prior trend remains intact or is losing momentum [10].
* **Volume Confirmation Rule**: Volume acts as a secondary confirmation tool [11]. In a healthy uptrend, volume must increase as prices advance and contract during downward corrections [11]. In a downtrend, volume increases on price drops and shrinks during rallies [11].
* **The Closing Price Rule**: Dow relied **exclusively on daily closing prices** [11, 17]. Intraday penetrations beyond support or resistance levels are considered invalid noise [17].

---

### Trading Rules & Market Applications

* **Execution Focus by Asset Class**:
  * **Stock Investors**: Focus primarily on the **primary trend** and ignore short-term fluctuations [15].
  * **Futures Traders**: Focus on the **secondary (intermediate) trend** for directional bias and use **minor trends** for timing short-term entry and exit points (e.g., buying minor dips in intermediate uptrends) [15, 16].
* **Trend-Following Objective**: Dow Theory does not attempt to anticipate market turns or catch tops and bottoms [21, 22]. Its goal is to identify major market changes and capture the **large middle portion** of primary trends [22].
* **Primary Limitation (Signal Lag)**: Because Dow Theory requires confirmation of secondary highs/lows, signals typically lag, causing traders to miss **\\(20\%\\) to \\(25\%\\)** of a market move before entering a position [21].
