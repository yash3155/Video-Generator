### The Three Premises of Technical Analysis

1. **Market Action Discounts Everything**  
   This statement forms the cornerstone of technical analysis [1]. Technicians believe that anything that can affect a market’s price—whether fundamental, political, psychological, or otherwise—is already reflected in the market price [1]. Because price reflects the underlying balance of supply and demand, studying price action itself is all that is required to forecast market direction [1, 2]. Technicians view chart reading as a shortcut form of fundamental analysis, as market action indirectly reflects all economic forces [3, 4].

2. **Prices Move in Trends**  
   The entire technical approach is predicated on identifying trends in their early stages to trade in the direction of those trends [5]. Adapted from Newton's first law of motion, a key corollary to this premise states that **a trend in motion is more likely to continue than to reverse** until it gives definite signals of a turnaround [6, 7].

3. **History Repeats Itself**  
   Much of technical analysis is rooted in the study of human psychology [8]. Price patterns that have appeared on charts over the past century reflect bullish or bearish market psychology [8]. Because human psychology tends not to change over time, patterns that worked well in the past are assumed to remain valid in the future [8].

---

### Core Philosophical Debate: Technical vs. Fundamental Analysis

* **Cause vs. Effect:** Fundamental analysis examines the economic forces of supply and demand to determine an asset's **intrinsic value** [9]. Technicians study the **effect** (price action), while fundamentalists focus on the **cause** of market movements [10]. Technicians maintain that understanding *why* prices move is unnecessary for accurate forecasting [10, 11].
* **Inclusivity of the Approaches:** Technicians argue that the technical approach implicitly includes fundamental analysis because all fundamentals are discounted into market price [4]. Conversely, fundamental analysis does not incorporate price action, making it difficult to rely on fundamental analysis alone for active trading [4].
* **Price as a Leading Indicator:** Market prices act as leading indicators of known fundamentals [12]. Prices often begin new trends before conventional economic data or news confirms the shift, as the market reacts to unknown or future fundamental changes [12].
* **Analysis vs. Timing:** Fundamental analysis can assist in long-term market forecasting, but it provides little guidance for **timing** (pinpointing specific entry and exit points) [13, 14]. Technical analysis is indispensable for timing, which is critical for managing risk and leverage [13, 14].

---

### Key Definitions (Chapter 1, Part 1)

* **Technical Analysis:** The study of market action, primarily through the use of charts, for the purpose of forecasting future price trends [15].
* **Market Action:** The three principal sources of information available to the technician: **price, volume, and open interest** (open interest applies specifically to futures and options) [15, 16].
* **Price Action:** The movement of market prices over time [15, 16]. While often used interchangeably with market action, price action strictly refers to price data alone [15, 16].
* **Intrinsic Value:** The theoretical true worth of an asset as determined by fundamental analysis based on the law of supply and demand [9].
* **Traditional Chartist (Visual Analyst):** A technician who relies primarily on price charts and visual indicators, treating market analysis as an art form that involves subjective interpretation [17].
* **Statistical Technician (Quantitative Analyst):** A technician who quantifies, tests, and optimizes technical rules to build automated, mechanical computer trading models, minimizing human subjectivity [18].
