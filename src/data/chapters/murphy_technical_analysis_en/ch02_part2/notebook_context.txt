### 1. Averages Confirming Each Other

* **Core Concept & Definition**: A fundamental principle of Dow Theory is that no major bull or bear market signal can occur unless both key market averages—specifically the **Dow Jones Industrial Average** and the **Dow Jones Transportation Average** (historically the Rail Average)—confirm each other by generating the same signal [1, 2].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Bull Market Signal**: Both the Industrial and Transportation averages must exceed a previous secondary peak to confirm the inception or continuation of an uptrend [2].
  * **Bear Market Signal**: Both averages must close below a previous secondary trough to confirm a major downtrend [2, 3].
  * **Timing**: Signals do not have to occur simultaneously; however, a shorter length of time between the two signals provides stronger confirmation [2].
* **Divergence Rule**: If one average breaks out to a new high or low while the other fails to do so, a **divergence** occurs [2, 4]. Dow Theory assumes that the prior trend remains intact as long as the averages diverge [2].
* **Author Case Studies & Historical Examples**:
  * **Long-Term Trend Alignment**: Figure 2.1 demonstrates that for a major bull market to persist over multi-year periods, both the Industrials and Transports must advance together [5].
  * **1997 Confirmation Case Study (Figure 2.2)**: 
    1. *Point 1 (Early 1997)*: The Dow Transports broke out above a prior resistance line, confirming an earlier breakout in the Dow Industrials [6].
    2. *Point 2 (May 1997)*: The Dow Industrials surged to a new high, confirming an earlier new high registered by the Transports [6].

### 2. Volume Confirmation

* **Core Concept & Definition**: Volume is a secondary but vital confirming indicator that measures the degree of intensity or pressure behind a price move [7-10].
* **Core Rule of Volume**: **Volume should expand in the direction of the prevailing trend** [7, 11].
  * **In an Uptrend**: Trading volume expands as prices advance and contracts (diminishes) on price pullbacks [7, 11].
  * **In a Downtrend**: Trading volume expands as prices decline and contracts during price rallies [7, 12].
* **"Volume Precedes Price"**: Technicians observe that changes in buying or selling pressure show up in volume statistics before manifesting as trend reversals on price charts [13].
* **Chart Pattern Breakout Rules**:
  * **Upside Breakouts**: An upside breakout from a reversal or continuation pattern (e.g., Head and Shoulders bottom, Triangle, Double Bottom) **must be accompanied by a heavy surge in volume** to be valid [12, 14-17]. At market bottoms, a volume pickup is **absolutely essential** [14, 15].
  * **Downside Breakouts**: Markets can "fall of their own weight" at tops without heavy volume, though increased downside volume confirms genuine selling pressure [12, 14].
* **Mathematical Formula / Volume Indicator**:
  * **On-Balance Volume (OBV)** (Joseph Granville): A cumulative running total of volume where volume is added on up days and subtracted on down days [18, 19]:
    \\[\text{OBV}_t = \begin{cases} \text{OBV}_{t-1} + \text{Volume}_t & \text{if } \text{Close}_t > \text{Close}_{t-1} \\ \text{OBV}_{t-1} - \text{Volume}_t & \text{if } \text{Close}_t < \text{Close}_{t-1} \\ \text{OBV}_{t-1} & \text{if } \text{Close}_t = \text{Close}_{t-1} \end{cases}\\]
* **Author Case Studies & Examples**:
  * **McDonald's (Figure 7.3 & 7.8)**: An upside price breakout above its November 1997 peak was validated by a sharp burst in volume bars, followed by the OBV line leading prices higher [11, 20, 21].
  * **Intel Corp (Figure 7.4 & 7.7)**: Figure 7.4 illustrates volume expanding on up days and contracting on dips [20, 22]. Figure 7.7 shows a **bearish divergence** between OBV and price, correctly warning of an impending major decline [23].

### 3. Failure Swings vs. Nonfailure Swings

* **Core Concept & Definition**: Specific price pattern structures used in Dow Theory and oscillator analysis (such as Welles Wilder's RSI) to distinguish between normal secondary corrections and major trend reversals [24-26].

```
FAILURE SWING (TOP)                 NONFAILURE SWING (TOP)
     Peak A                              Peak C
       /\                                  /\
      /  \    Peak C                      /  \
     /    \    /\                        /    \    Peak E
    /      \  /  \                      /      \    /\
   /        \/    \                    /        \/    \
  /          B     \                  /          B     \
 /                  \ S              /                  \ S1    \ S2
/                    \              /                    \_______\
 (Sell at S when B is broken)        (Sell at S1 or S2 after lower high E)
```

#### A. Top Patterns (Figures 2.3a & 2.3b)
* **Failure Swing (Top)**:
  * **Structure**: In an uptrend, price rallies to Peak A, declines to Trough B, and rallies to Peak C. **Peak C fails to reach or exceed Peak A** [24].
  * **Sell Signal**: Occurs at **Point S** when price recedes from Peak C and breaks below support at Trough B [24].
  * **Pattern Strength**: Classified as a **weaker pattern** because the buying effort at C lacks the momentum to reach the prior high [24, 27].
* **Nonfailure Swing (Top)**:
  * **Structure**: Price rallies to Peak A, drops to Trough B, and rallies to Peak C, which **surpasses Peak A** [25]. Price then drops sharply below Trough B to Point D [25].
  * **Sell Signals**: Conservative Dow theorists wait for a secondary rally to Peak E (which stays below C) followed by a breakdown below Trough D (**Point S2**) to confirm two lower highs and two lower lows [25]. Some aggressive traders enter at **Point S1** when Trough B is first broken [25].
  * **Pattern Strength**: Represents a **stronger pattern** because Peak C registered a new high before failing [25, 27].

#### B. Bottom Patterns (Figures 2.4a & 2.4b)
* **Failure Swing (Bottom)**: Trough C **fails to fall below Trough A**. A buy signal is triggered when price breaks above resistance at Peak B (**Point B1**) [28].
* **Nonfailure Swing (Bottom)**: Trough C falls below Trough A, rallies above Peak B (**Point B1**), pulls back to a higher trough E, and breaks above Peak D (**Point B2**) to complete the turn [28].

#### C. Oscillator Application (Relative Strength Index / RSI)
* **RSI Top Failure Swing**: Occurs when RSI > 70, Peak 2 fails to exceed Peak 1, and the RSI line drops below the intervening trough (Point 3) [26, 29].
* **RSI Bottom Failure Swing**: Occurs when RSI < 30, Trough 2 holds above Trough 1, and the RSI line crosses above the intervening peak (Point 3) [26, 29].

### 4. Closing Prices

* **Core Concept & Definition**: Charles Dow relied **exclusively on closing prices** to formulate signals and evaluate market trends [7, 27, 30].
* **Trading Rules & Application**:
  * **Filter Rule**: Intraday penetrations above a peak or below a trough are considered invalid "whipsaws" or noise [27, 31]. 
  * **Breakout Requirement**: To establish a valid breakout or trend signal, the market average or price must **close** beyond the prior peak or trough [27, 31].
  * **Line Charts**: Line charts connect consecutive closing prices. Many technicians regard line charts as a superior measure of price activity because the closing price represents the final consensus of value for the trading session [30, 32].

### 5. Criticisms of Dow Theory (Capturing the Middle 68% of Moves)

* **Primary Criticism**: **Lag and Delayed Signals**. Critics argue that Dow Theory misses the first 20% to 25% of a price move before generating a signal [33]. A buy signal typically occurs well into Phase 2 of an uptrend after price penetrates a prior intermediate peak [33].
* **Author's Defense & Intended Purpose**:
  * **Not Intended for Anticipation**: Charles Dow never intended his theory to forecast exact tops and bottoms [34, 35].
  * **Core Objective**: The explicit goal of Dow Theory is to identify the emergence of major bull and bear markets and **capture the large middle portion of important market moves** [34].
  * **Historical Performance Statistics**: From 1920 to 1975, Dow Theory signals successfully captured **68% of all major moves** in the Dow Industrial and Transportation Averages, and **67% of moves** in the S&P 500 Composite Index (Barron's study) [34].
  * **Misunderstanding Trend-Following**: Murphy stresses that criticizing Dow Theory for failing to catch exact market tops and bottoms reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of trend-following philosophy [34].
