### 1. Point and Figure vs. Bar Charts

#### **Historical Background**
* **Origins**: Point and figure (P&F) charting predates standard bar charting by at least 10 years [1]. Originating around 1886 as the **"book method"** (a term referenced by Charles Dow in 1901), it was later called **"figure charts"** in the 1920s before Victor deVilliers popularized the term **"point and figure"** in 1933 [1, 2].

#### **Core Conceptual Differences**
* **Pure Price Movement vs. Time & Price**: 
  * **Bar Charts**: Combine price (vertical axis) and time (horizontal axis) [3]. Every period adds a new bar regardless of whether price changed [3].
  * **P&F Charts**: Record **pure price movement** only [3]. Time is ignored on the horizontal axis [3]. If prices do not change, the chart remains untouched; during active periods, multiple plots occur [3].
* **Treatment of Volume**: 
  * **Bar Charts**: Display volume as separate vertical histogram bars at the bottom [4].
  * **P&F Charts**: Do not record volume numbers as a separate entity [4]. Instead, volume is reflected in intraday price change activity (the density of price oscillations recorded in columns) [4, 5]. This highlights specific price levels where maximum trading activity occurred, pinpointing strong support and resistance [5].
* **Visual Structure**: 
  * Consist of alternating vertical columns of **\\(\mathbf{X}\\)'s** (rising prices) and **\\(\mathbf{O}\\)'s** (falling prices) [5, 6].
* **Breakout Precision**: 
  * An **upside breakout** occurs when an \\(\text{X}\\) column rises **1 box higher** than the top of a previous \\(\text{X}\\) column [5-7].
  * A **downside breakout** occurs when an \\(\text{O}\\) column drops **1 box lower** than the bottom of a previous \\(\text{O}\\) column [6-8].
  * Signals are far more precise and less subjective on P&F charts than on bar charts [5, 8, 9].
* **Flexibility & Sensitivity**: 
  * Sensitivity is controlled by varying **box size** (price value per box) and **reversal criteria** (number of boxes required to start a new column) [8, 10].
  * Larger box/reversal sizes eliminate short-term market noise for long-term investors; smaller box sizes increase sensitivity for short-term traders, generating more signals and tighter stop placements [10-12].

#### **Summary of P&F Advantages**
1. Highly adaptable to any time frame or trading need by altering box size and reversal parameters [9].
2. Eliminates time distortion and delivers precise, non-ambiguous buy/sell signals [8, 9].
3. Promotes strict trading discipline through objective entry, exit, and stop-loss rules [9].

### 2. Constructing Intraday P&F Charts (X and O Columns)

#### **Core Rules & Definitions**
* **Box Size**: The fixed price increment assigned to each vertical square/box on the grid (e.g., 1 point for stocks, or 5 points for currency futures) [13, 14].
* **Reversal Size**: The minimum number of boxes price must move in the opposite direction to justify starting a new column [8, 15].

#### **Step-by-Step Construction Algorithm (1-Box Reversal Intraday)**
1. **Grid Setup**: Scale the vertical axis using the chosen box size [16].
2. **Column Plotting**:
   * Plot **\\(\mathbf{X}\\)'s** in a single vertical column while prices advance [5, 16].
   * When price drops, move 1 column to the right and plot **\\(\mathbf{O}\\)'s** downward [17].
3. **Minimum 2-Box Entry Requirement (1-Box Reversal Only)**:
   * On a 1-box reversal chart, every column **must contain at least 2 entries** [17, 18].
   * If a 1-box price movement occurs followed by an immediate reversal, **\\(\mathbf{X}\\)'s and \\(\mathbf{O}\\)'s are combined within the same column** to satisfy the 2-box minimum before moving to the next column [15, 17, 18].
4. **Time Tracking**: Black out the final price box of each trading day to establish a time marker across the chart [18, 19].

#### **Reversal Criteria Comparison**
* **1-Box Reversal (\\(1 \times 1\\) or \\(5 \times 1\\))**: Tracks detailed intraday tick activity; used for short-term analysis; serves as the base chart from which condensed charts are built [15, 20].
* **3-Box Reversal (\\(1 \times 3\\) or \\(5 \times 3\\))**: Condenses data by requiring a 3-box price retracement to start a new column; ideal for intermediate trend analysis [10, 15].
* **5-Box Reversal (\\(1 \times 5\\) or \\(5 \times 5\\))**: Severely compresses data to track primary long-term trends [11, 15].

#### **Author Case Example**
* **Swiss Franc / Deutsche Mark Futures (9 Trading Days, 5-Point Box Size)**: Murphy illustrates plotting intraday ticks ranging from \\(4850\\) to \\(4970\\) across 1-box, 3-box, and 5-box reversal formats, demonstrating how noise compresses into clean trend columns as reversal requirements increase [14-16, 18].

### 3. The Horizontal & Vertical Count for Price Targets

#### **The Horizontal Count Premise**
Unlike bar charts (which rely on vertical pattern height), P&F charting utilizes the **horizontal count** [20, 21]. This is based on the premise that there is a **direct relationship between the horizontal width of a congestion area (base or top) and the distance price will travel** after a breakout [21].

#### **Horizontal Count Rules (Intraday 1-Box Chart)**
1. Identify a completed basing or topping congestion area [22].
2. Locate the horizontal reference line across the congestion area that has the **least number of empty boxes** (highest number of filled \\(\text{X}\\)'s and \\(\text{O}\\)'s) [23].
3. Count the total number of columns across the congestion area along that line (including empty spaces within the pattern boundaries) [22, 23].
4. Project that total column count vertically upward from the base line (for upside targets) or downward from the top line (for downside targets) [23].

---

#### **Mathematical Price Target Formulas**

##### **1. 3-Box Reversal Horizontal Count Formula**
\\[\text{Price Target} = \text{Base/Top Price Level} \pm \Big( \text{Column Count} \times \text{Box Size} \times 3 \Big)\\]

* **Variables**:
  * \\(\text{Column Count}\\): Total number of horizontal columns across the consolidation base or top [24, 25].
  * \\(\text{Box Size}\\): Monetary/point value assigned to each box [24, 25].
  * \\(3\\): Reversal multiplier for a 3-box chart [24, 25].
  * \\(\text{Base/Top Price Level}\\): The reference price line from which the count is projected [24, 25].
* **Example**: For a chart with a \\(\$1.00\\) box size (\\(3\text{-box reversal value} = \$3.00\\)) and a \\(10\text{-column}\\) wide base:
  \\[\text{Target} = \text{Base Level} + (10 \times \$3.00) = \text{Base Level} + \$30.00 \quad [24, 25]\\]

##### **2. 3-Box Reversal Vertical Count Formula**
\\[\text{Price Target} = \text{Column Starting Level} \pm \Big( \text{Box Count in 1st Column} \times \text{Box Size} \times 3 \Big)\\]

* **Variables**:
  * \\(\text{Box Count in 1st Column}\\): Number of \\(\text{X}\\)'s (or \\(\text{O}\\)'s) filled in the first impulse column of a new trend [26, 27].
  * \\(\text{Box Size}\\): Point value per box [24, 26].
  * \\(3\\): Multiplier that effectively triples the vertical height of the initial impulse leg [26, 27].
* **Rule**: If a double top or double bottom occurs, the count is taken from the second column of \\(\text{X}\\)'s or \\(\text{O}\\)'s [26, 27].

---

#### **Logarithmic P&F Method (Kenneth Tower)**
Developed by Kenneth Tower (UST Securities), this approach scales box sizes as **percentages based on 3-year historical volatility** rather than fixed dollar points [28]. 
* **Calculation**: If box size \\(= 3.6\%\\), a 2-box reversal requires a \\(7.2\%\\) price retracement to open a new column [28].

#### **Author Case Studies & Examples**
* **British Telecommunications PLC**: Demonstrates a horizontal target to \\(92\\) (tripling a base count and adding to \\(50\\)) and a vertical target to \\(102\\) (tripling the \\(\text{X}\\) column box count and adding to \\(63\\)) [29].
* **Intel Corp**: Shows a short-term logarithmic horizontal target to \\(33\\) (measured halfway across the base) and a long-term target to \\(87.6\\) (measured across the full width of the base) [30].
* **America Online (AOL)**: Logarithmic percentage chart (\\(3.6\%\\) box size) projecting horizontal arc targets to \\(69.7\\) and \\(136.5\\) [28, 31].

### 4. P&F Price Patterns

#### **The Fulcrum Definition**
A **fulcrum** is a well-defined horizontal congestion area occurring after a major advance or decline [32]. It represents an accumulation base or distribution top where price repeatedly tests support or resistance levels, interrupted by minor rallies/declines [32]. It frequently takes the shape of a double or triple top/bottom and is completed when a breakout (**catapult**) clears the boundaries of the congestion range [32].

#### **General Patterns & Limitations**
* Standard reversal formations (Double Tops/Bottoms, Triple Tops/Bottoms, Head & Shoulders, Saucers) appear regularly on P&F charts [32].
* **V-Bases / Spikes**: Because V-bottoms lack horizontal width, they **cannot be measured using horizontal count techniques** [33].

### 5. 3-Box Reversal Charting & 45-Degree Trendlines

#### **Daily High/Low Updating Algorithm (A.W. Cohen Method)**
Constructed using only daily **High** and **Low** prices, eliminating the need for intraday tick logs [40, 41]:

1. **If current column is an \\(\mathbf{X}\\) column (rising)**:
   * Look at the daily **High** first [41, 42].
   * If the High allows plotting \\(\ge 1\\) new \\(\text{X}\\), plot the \\(\text{X}\\)'s and **stop** (ignore the daily Low) [41, 42].
   * If **no** new \\(\text{X}\\) can be plotted, inspect the daily **Low** to see if a \\(\ge 3\\)-box reversal occurred [42, 43].
   * If a reversal occurred, move 1 column right, move down 1 box, and plot 3 \\(\text{O}\\)'s [42, 43].
   * If no reversal occurred, make **no entries** for that day [42, 43].
2. **If current column is an \\(\mathbf{O}\\) column (falling)**:
   * Look at the daily **Low** first [42, 43].
   * If the Low allows plotting \\(\ge 1\\) new \\(\text{O}\\), plot the \\(\text{O}\\)'s and **stop** (ignore the daily High) [42, 43].
   * If **no** new \\(\text{O}\\) can be plotted, inspect the daily **High** to see if a \\(\ge 3\\)-box reversal occurred [42-44].
   * If a reversal occurred, move 1 column right, move up 1 box, and plot 3 \\(\text{X}\\)'s [43, 44].
   * If no reversal occurred, make **no entries** for that day [43, 44].
3. **Iron Rule**: \\(\text{X}\\)'s and \\(\text{O}\\)'s can **never be drawn on the same day** [44].

---

#### **45-Degree Trendline Rules**
Unlike bar charts, trendlines on 3-box P&F charts do not connect relative peaks or troughs; they are drawn strictly at **45-degree geometric angles** [45].

```
 BULLISH SUPPORT LINE (45°)              BEARISH RESISTANCE LINE (45°)
      X                                       O (Peak Column Top)
     X X                                       \
    X   X                                       \  O
   X     X (Bullish Zone)                        \  O
  O       X                                       \  O (Bearish Zone)
 --- 45° line ---                                  --- 45° line ---
(Lowest O Column Base)
```

* **Bullish Support Line**: Drawn upward to the right at a **45-degree angle** starting from **directly beneath the lowest column of \\(\mathbf{O}\\)'s** in a base [45]. As long as price remains above this line, the market is bullish [45].
* **Bearish Resistance Line**: Drawn downward to the right at a **45-degree angle** starting from **directly above the top of the highest column of \\(\mathbf{X}\\)'s** at a peak [45]. As long as price remains below this line, the market is bearish [45].
* **Trendline Adjustments**: If a corrective move breaks a 45-degree support line but the uptrend subsequently resumes, a new 45-degree support line is projected from the bottom of the new reaction low [46].

---

#### **Trading & Entry Tactics**
* **Primary Execution Signals**:
  * **Buy Signal**: Triggered when an \\(\text{X}\\) column moves **1 box higher** than the prior \\(\text{X}\\) column peak [7, 47].
  * **Sell Signal**: Triggered when an \\(\text{O}\\) column falls **1 box lower** than the prior \\(\text{O}\\) column trough [7, 47].
* **Trendline Filter**: Only take long positions when price is above the Bullish Support Line; only take short positions when price is below the Bearish Resistance Line [47, 48].
* **Four Specific Entry Strategies**:
  1. *Breakout Entry*: Buy immediately upon the initial 1-box breakout [49, 50].
  2. *Reversal Entry*: Wait for a 3-box reversal pullback after a breakout to obtain a lower risk entry price [49, 50].
  3. *Correction Reversal Entry*: Buy the first 3-box reversal back in the direction of the trend following a corrective move (provides a tight stop location) [49-51].
  4. *Second Breakout Entry*: Add to positions on a secondary breakout in the direction of the original signal [52, 53].

#### **Protective Stop-Loss & Trailing Stop Rules**
* **Initial Stop Loss**:
  * **Long Positions**: Place protective sell stop **just below the lowest box of the latest column of \\(\mathbf{O}\\)'s** [12, 49, 50].
  * **Short Positions**: Place protective buy stop **just above the highest box of the latest column of \\(\mathbf{X}\\)'s** [50, 52].
* **Trailing Stop Management**: As the trend progresses, continually trail protective stops upward beneath each newly formed \\(\text{O}\\) column (or downward above each \\(\text{X}\\) column in a downtrend) to lock in accumulated profits [12, 52, 53].

---

#### **P&F Moving Averages (Kenneth Tower Method)**
* Plotted directly on P&F charts using **10-column** and **20-column** moving averages [54].
* **Calculation**:
  1. Calculate the column average: \\(\text{Column Average} = \frac{\sum \text{Prices in Column}}{\text{Number of X's or O's in Column}}\\) [54].
  2. Average these column values across 10 and 20 consecutive columns [54].
* **Interpretation**: Operates like standard moving average crossovers (faster 10-column average above 20-column average signals an uptrend) [55].

#### **Author Case Studies & Examples**
* **Royal Dutch Petroleum**: Multi-year logarithmic \\(2.7\%\\) reversal chart featuring 10-column and 20-column moving averages, tracking a 4-year uptrend from 1993–1997 and a 1998 linear chart breakout [55-57].
* **Semiconductor Index**: Highlights how a 3-box reversal chart produced 4 timely buy and sell signals over a 2.5-year span, catching major trend legs while avoiding whipsaws [58].

MEASURING TECHNIQUES Three point reversal charts allow the use of two different measuring techniques—the horizontal and the vertical. For the horizontal, count the number of columns in a bottom or topping pattern. That number of columns must then be multiplied by the value of the reversal or the number of boxes needed for a reversal. For example, let's assign a $1.00 box value to a chart with a 3 box reversal. We count the number of boxes across a base and come up with 10. Because we're using a 3 box reversal, the value of that reversal is $3.00 (3x$1.00). Multiply the 10 columns across the base by $3 for a total of $30. That number is then added to the bottom of the basing pattern or subtracted from the top of a topping pattern to arrive at the price objective."

- "The original 1 box reversal method of plotting markets required intraday prices. The 3 box reversal was a condensation of the 1 box and was meant for intermediate trend analysis. Cohen reasoned that because so few 3 box reversals occurred in stocks during the day that it was not necessary to use intraday prices to 278 Chapter 11 construct the 3 box reversal chart. Hence the decision to use only the high and low prices, which were readily available in most financial newspapers. This modified technique, which is the basis of the Chartcraft service, greatly simplified point and figure charting and made it accessible to the average trader."
- "CONSTRUCTION OF THE 3 POINT REVERSAL CHART The construction of the chart is relatively simple. First, the chart must be scaled in the same way as the intraday chart. A value must be assigned to each box. These tasks are performed for subscribers to the Chartcraft service because the charts are already constructed and the box values assigned. The chart shows a series of alternating columns with x's representing rising prices and the o columns showing falling prices. (See Figure 11.8.) The actual plotting of the x's and o's requires only the high and low prices for the day. If the last column is an x column (showing rising prices), then look at the high price for the day. If the daily high permits the filling in of 1 or more x's, then fill in those boxes and stop. That's all you do for that day. Remember that the entire value of the box must be filled. Fractions or partial filling of the box don't count. Repeat the same process the next day, looking only at the high price. As long as prices continue to rise, permitting the plotting of at least one x, continue to fill in the boxes with x's, ignoring the low price."

- "THE DRAWING OF TRENDLINES In our discussion of intraday charts, it was pointed out that trend-lines were drawn in the conventional way. This is not the case on these 3 point reversal charts. Trendlines on these charts are drawn at 45 degree angles. Also, trendlines do not necessarily have to connect previous tops or bottoms. The Basic Bullish Support Line and Bearish Resistance Line These are your basic up and down trendlines. Because of the severe condensation on these charts, it would be impractical to try to connect rally tops or reaction lows. The 45 degree line is, therefore, used. In an uptrend, the bullish support line is drawn at a 45 degree angle upward to the right from under the lowest column of o's. As long as prices remain above that line, the major trend is considered to be bullish. In a downtrend, the bearish resistance line is drawn at a 45 degree angle downward to the right from the top of the highest column of x's. As long as prices remain below that down trendline, the trend is bearish. (See Figures 11.10-11.12.)"
