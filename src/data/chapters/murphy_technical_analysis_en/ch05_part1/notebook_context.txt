### 1. Reversal vs. Continuation Patterns

* **Reversal Patterns**: Price formations that indicate a major change or turnaround in the prevailing trend is underway [1, 2]. They mark key transition periods where the forces of supply and demand shift [3-5]. Reversal patterns typically require longer time durations to build and signal major, long-term trend changes [6].
* **Continuation Patterns**: Formations that indicate the market is merely pausing or consolidating to digest a prior move or correct a near-term overbought/oversold condition before resuming the original trend [1, 6, 7]. Continuation patterns are generally shorter-term in duration (intermediate or minor) [6, 8].

---

### 2. Six Preliminary Rules Common to All Reversal Patterns

Before identifying any specific major reversal pattern, Murphy outlines six fundamental preliminary rules [9]:

1. **Existence of a Prior Trend**: A prerequisite for any reversal pattern is a well-established prior trend [9, 10]. A market must have an existing trend to reverse; if a pattern appears without a prior trend, it is suspect [10].
2. **Breaking of an Important Trendline**: The first signal of an impending trend reversal is often the violation of a major trendline [9, 11]. This signals a change in trend, though not necessarily an immediate reversal—prices may enter a sideways consolidation first [11].
3. **Pattern Size Correlates with Subsequent Move**: The larger the price pattern (measured in vertical height and horizontal width/duration), the greater the subsequent move [9, 12]. Vertical height reflects price volatility, while horizontal width reflects time duration [12].
4. **Tops Are Shorter and More Volatile**: Topping patterns generally take less time to form, feature wider and more violent price swings, and exhibit greater volatility than bottoms [9, 12, 13]. Because prices tend to fall faster than they rise, bear markets offer rapid profit opportunities, albeit with higher short-term risk [13].
5. **Bottoms Have Smaller Price Ranges and Take Longer**: Bottom formations feature narrower price ranges but require longer time periods to build [9, 10, 12, 13]. Consequently, identifying and trading bottoms is generally less treacherous and less costly than catching tops [13].
6. **Volume Is More Important on the Upside**: Volume must confirm price action upon pattern completion [14]. While market tops can "fall of their own weight" due to a lack of buying interest (inertia) without heavy downside volume [14, 15], upside breakouts at bottoms **must** be accompanied by a significant expansion in volume to validate the breakout [14, 16, 17].

### 3. Head and Shoulders Top (H&S Top)

The Head and Shoulders Top is recognized as the best known and most reliable of all major reversal patterns [18]. It consists of three distinct peaks, where the middle peak (the head) is higher than the two surrounding peaks (the shoulders) [5, 19, 20].

```
                Head (C)
                 /\
  Left          /  \          Right
Shoulder (A)   /    \      Shoulder (E)
    /\        /      \        /\
   /  \      /        \      /  \
--/----\----/----------\----/----\------------ Neckline
       (B)              (D)       \ (G) Return Move
                                   \
                                    V Downside Break
```

#### Structural Sequence:
1. **Left Shoulder (Peak A)**: Heavy volume accompanies a rally to a new high, followed by a corrective dip to point B on lighter volume [5, 20].
2. **Head (Peak C)**: Prices rally to a new high (Peak C), but volume is noticeably lighter than during the left shoulder rally [20-22]. The subsequent decline (Point D) penetrates below Peak A, approaching the previous reaction low at B—a strong warning of trend weakening [20, 23].
3. **Right Shoulder (Peak E)**: A third rally occurs on noticeably light volume but fails to reach the height of Peak C [20, 23, 24]. This failure establishes descending peaks [24, 25].

---

### 4. Neckline Breaks & Confirmation Rules

* **Neckline Definition**: A trendline drawn underneath the two intervening reaction lows (troughs B and D) [25]. 
* **Neckline Slope**:
  * **Upward Slanting**: Most common at tops; reflects ongoing market strength [25, 26].
  * **Horizontal**: Neutral baseline [25, 26].
  * **Downward Slanting**: Indicates severe market weakness (often accompanied by a weak right shoulder), but provides a later sell signal after much of the price drop has occurred [26].
* **Confirmation Criteria**:
  * The Head and Shoulders pattern is **not complete** until the neckline is decisively broken on a closing price basis [25, 27].
  * **Price Filters**: Technicians apply price filters such as a 1% to 3% closing penetration beyond the neckline [27-29].
  * **Time Filters**: The "two-day rule" requires two consecutive daily closes below the broken neckline to confirm a valid breakdown [27, 29].

---

### 5. Return Moves

* **Mechanism**: After the initial downside break of the neckline, prices frequently execute a temporary bounce (return move) back toward the underside of the broken neckline or previous reaction low (Point D) [19, 30].
* **Role Reversal**: The broken neckline and reaction low D reverse roles from prior support to overhead resistance [30].
* **Volume Dynamics**:
  * If the initial neckline breakdown occurs on **very heavy volume**, the likelihood of a return move is diminished due to heavy downside momentum [30].
  * If the initial breakdown occurs on **lighter volume**, a return move is much more likely [30].
  * The return move itself **must** take place on light volume, followed by expanding volume as the downtrend resumes [30].
* **Failed Head and Shoulders**: Once broken, prices should not close back above the neckline [31, 32]. A decisive close back inside the neckline signals a pattern failure ("Failed Head and Shoulders"), indicating that the initial breakdown was a false signal and the original uptrend is resuming [31, 32].

### 7. Price Objectives (Mathematical Formulas & Variables)

Vertical measuring techniques project minimum price targets based on pattern height [33, 34].

#### Vertical Height Formula:
\\[H = P_{\text{Head}} - P_{\text{Neckline}}\\]

#### Downside Target Formula:
\\[\text{Target}_{\text{Down}} = P_{\text{Breakout}} - H\\]

#### Variable Definitions:
* \\(P_{\text{Head}}\\): Price level at the peak of the head (Point C) [33].
* \\(P_{\text{Neckline}}\\): Price level of the neckline directly below the head [33].
* \\(H\\): Vertical height of the pattern [33].
* \\(P_{\text{Breakout}}\\): Price level where the neckline is penetrated [33].

* **Alternative Calculation**: Measure the vertical distance of the first downleg from the Head to reaction low D (\\(P_{\text{Head}} - P_{\text{Low D}}\\)) and project double that distance downward from Point D [34].
* **Minimum vs. Maximum Objectives**:
  * **Minimum Target**: The projected vertical height \\(H\\) below the breakout price [33, 35].
  * **Maximum Target**: A 100% retracement of the prior bull trend (returning to where the entire advance began) [35-37].
* **Objective Adjustments**: Projected targets must be adjusted to account for existing technical factors: prominent historical support levels from prior reaction lows, major percentage retracements (38%, 50%, 62/66%), open price gaps, or long-term trendlines [37, 38]. If a projected target is 30 but prominent support lies at 32, the objective should be adjusted upward to 32 [38].

### 8. Inverse Head and Shoulders Bottom

The Head and Shoulders Bottom (or Inverse Head and Shoulders) is a mirror image of the topping pattern, consisting of three troughs with the middle trough (the head) lower than the two shoulders [16, 39].

```
--\----/----/----------\----/----\------------ Neckline
   \  /      \        /      \  /
    \/        \      /        \/
  Left         \    /        Right
Shoulder (A)    \  /      Shoulder (E)
                 \/
              Head (C)
```

#### Key Differences from the H&S Top:
1. **Critical Role of Volume on Breakout**: While tops can fall without high volume, a bottom **requires** a sharp burst of heavy trading volume on the neckline breakout [14, 16, 17]. Prices do not rise on inertia; demand must decisively exceed supply [15].
2. **Volume Sequence in First Half**: Volume at the head is slightly lighter than at the left shoulder [17]. However, the rally off the head shows noticeable expansion in volume (often exceeding the rally off the left shoulder) [16, 17]. The dip to the right shoulder occurs on very light volume [17].
3. **Frequency of Return Moves**: Return moves back to the broken neckline after an upside breakout are **more common** at bottoms than at tops and take place on light volume [16, 26, 39].
4. **Neckline Slope**: Most bottom necklines have a slight downward slant [15, 26]. A rising neckline reflects greater underlying strength but provides a later entry signal [26].

#### Author Case Studies & Tactical Entry Rules for Bottoms [31, 40]:
Murphy highlights three tactical methods for aggressive traders looking to position ahead of the neckline breakout [40]:
* **Right Shoulder Probing**: Initiate long positions during the formation of the right shoulder when prices retrace 50% to 66% of the rally off the head (from Point C to D) [31, 40].
* **Symmetry Entry**: Buy into the right shoulder as prices drop to the same price level as the trough of the left shoulder [31, 40].
* **Minor Trendline Break**: Draw a short-term down trendline along the corrective decline from Point D to E and buy the first upside break of that trendline [31, 40].
* **Pyramiding Positions**: Aggressive initial positions taken during right shoulder development are added to when the neckline is decisively broken on heavy volume, or during the subsequent light-volume return move back to the neckline [31, 40].

FINDING A PRICE OBJECTIVE The method of arriving at a price objective is based on the height of the pattern. Take the vertical distance from the head (point C) to the neckline. Then project that distance from the point where the neckline is broken. Assume, for example, that the top of the head is at 100 and the neckline is at 80. The vertical distance, therefore, would be the difference, which is 20. That 20 points would be measured downward from the level at which the neckline is broken. If the neckline in Figure 5.1a is at 82 when broken, a downside objective would be projected to the 62 level (82 - 20 = 62)."

The theme of measuring the height of the pattern and then projecting that distance from a breakout point will be constantly repeated. It's important to remember that the objective arrived at is only a minimum target. Prices will often move well beyond the objective. Having a minimum target to work with, however, is very helpful in determining beforehand whether there is enough potential in a market move to warrant taking a position. If the market exceeds the price objective, that's just icing on the cake. The maximum objective is the size of the prior move. If the previous bull market went from 30 to 100, then the maximum downside objective from a topping pattern would be a complete retracement of the entire upmove all the way down to 30. Reversal patterns can only be expected to reverse or retrace what has gone before them."
- "102 Chapter 5 A corollary to this point of having a prior trend to reverse is the matter of measuring implications. It was stated earlier that most of the measuring techniques give only minimum price objectives. The maximum objective would be the total extent of the prior move. If a major bull market has occurred and a major topping pattern is being formed, the maximum implication for the potential move to the downside would be a 100% retracement of the bull market, or the point at which it all began."
- "Adjusting Price Objectives A number of other factors should be considered while trying to arrive at a price objective. The measuring techniques from price patterns, such as the one just mentioned for the head and shoulders top, are only the first step. There are other technical factors to take into consideration. For example, where are the prominent support levels left by the reaction lows during the previous bull move? Bear markets often pause at these levels. What about percentage retracements? The maximum objective would be a 100% retracement of the previous bull market. But where are the 50% and 66% retracement levels? Those levels often provide significant support under the market. What about any prominent gaps underneath? They often function as support areas. Are there any long term trendlines visible below the market?"
- "The technician must consider other technical data in trying to pinpoint price targets taken from price patterns. If a downside price measurement, for example, projects a target to 30, and there is a prominent support level at 32, then the chartist would be wise to adjust the downside measurement to 32 instead of 30. As a general rule, when a slight discrepancy exists between a projected price target and a clearcut support or resistance level, it's 770 Chapter 5 usually safe to adjust the price target to that support or resistance level. It is often necessary to adjust the measured targets from price patterns to take into account additional technical information. The analyst has many different tools at his or her disposal. The most skillful technical analysts are those who learn to blend all of those tools together properly."

- "Tactics Market tactics play an important role in all trading. Not all technical traders like to wait for the breaking of the neckline before initiating a new position. As Figure 5.3 shows, more aggressive traders, believing that they have correctly identified a head and shoulders bottom, will begin to probe the long side during the formation of the right shoulder. Or they will buy the first technical signal that the decline into the right shoulder has ended. Some will measure the distance of the rally from the bottom of the head (points C to D) and then buy a 50% or 66% retracement of that rally. Still others would draw a tight down trendline along the decline from points D to E and buy the first upside break of that trendline. Because these patterns are reasonably symmetrical, some will buy into the right shoulder as it approaches the same level as the bottom of the left shoulder. A lot of anticipatory buying takes place during the formation of the right shoulder. If the initial long probe proves to be profitable, additional positions can be added on the actual penetration of the neckline or on the return move back to the neckline after the breakout."
