### 1. Trading Tactics: Anticipating vs. Waiting for Breakouts

#### **Core Concepts & Definitions**
* **Forecasting vs. Timing**: Price forecasting answers **what** to do (buy or sell) based on major trend direction [1, 2]. Timing (trading tactics) covers the short-term time frame (days, hours, minutes) and determines **when** and **where** to enter or exit [1, 3].
* **The Breakout Dilemma**: When a market approaches a breakout from a chart pattern or trading range, the trader must choose between three tactical entries [4, 5]:
  1. **Anticipating the breakout**: Taking a position *before* the market breaks out [4, 5].
  2. **Trading the breakout**: Buying or selling at the exact moment the breakout occurs [4, 5].
  3. **Waiting for the pullback**: Waiting for a corrective reaction/dip back to the breakout area [4, 5].

---

#### **Comparison & Trade-offs of the 3 Tactics**

| Tactic | Advantages | Disadvantages / Risks |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Anticipating Breakouts** | Yields the most favorable (lowest buy / highest sell) entry price if the breakout succeeds [5]. | Higher probability of a bad trade or false breakout (bull/bear trap) [5, 6]. |
| **Trading the Breakout** | Increases the probability of success by confirming price momentum and pattern completion [5]. | Incurs a worse (higher buy / lower sell) entry price [5]. |
| **Waiting for Pullback** | Offers a sensible compromise with confirmed direction and a better entry price near support/resistance [5]. | High risk of missing dynamic, fast-moving trends that do not offer a pullback [5]. |

---

#### **Trading Rules & Multiple-Units Strategy**
* **The Multiple-Units Solution**: Murphy advocates dividing positions into multiple units to execute a staged entry [5, 7]:
  * **Unit 1**: Take a small probe position in **anticipation** of the breakout [5, 7].
  * **Unit 2**: Add position upon the actual **breakout** [5, 7].
  * **Unit 3**: Add final position on the **corrective dip/pullback** following the breakout [5, 7].
* **Other Tactical Entry Rules**:
  * **Trendlines**: Enter on a violation of a tight trendline sloping counter to the main trend, or buy/sell against major trendlines acting as support or resistance [7].
  * **Support & Resistance**: Buy breaks of resistance or dips to support; sell breaks of support or rallies to resistance [8].
  * **Percentage Retracements**: Initiate long positions on **40%–60% pullbacks** in an uptrend, or short positions on **40%–60% bounces** in a downtrend [9].
  * **Price Gaps**: Buy dips to the upper boundary or inside a bullish support gap; place stops below the gap [10].

---

#### **Author Case Studies & Application Examples**
* **Head & Shoulders Bottom/Top**: Aggressive traders probe long positions during the formation of the **right shoulder** (or buy a 50%–66% retracement of the rally from the head) rather than waiting for the neckline penetration [11]. If profitable, additional units are added on the neckline breakout and subsequent return move [11].
* **Point & Figure 3-Box Reversal Tactics**: Traders can buy the initial breakout, buy a 3-box reversal immediately following the breakout, or buy the first 3-box reversal in the breakout direction after a correction occurs [12, 13].

### 2. Placing Protective Stops

#### **Core Concepts & Definitions**
* **Protective Stop**: A resting order placed to limit capital loss if a trade fails or to lock in paper profits on a winning position [14, 15].
* **Placement Art**: Effective stop placement balances **technical chart levels** with strict **money management parameters** [14, 16, 17].

---

#### **Technical Rules & Placement Principles**
* **Placement Relative to Structure**:
  * Long positions: Place stops **below valid support levels** [8, 17].
  * Short positions: Place stops **above valid resistance levels** [8, 17].
* **Avoiding Round Numbers**: Do not place protective stops directly at obvious round numbers (e.g., \$10, \$20, \$50, \$100, \$1000) [18, 19]. Place stops for long positions **below** round numbers and short stops **above** round numbers, as market orders tend to cluster at round numbers [18, 19].
* **Volatility & Market Noise Trade-Off**:
  * *Stops placed too close (tight stops)*: Lead to premature liquidation caused by random intraday market noise [14].
  * *Stops placed too far (loose stops)*: Avoid noise but generate unacceptably large dollar losses when trades fail [14].
* **Trailing Stops**: As a market advances, raise protective stops below newly established higher reaction lows (support) to protect accumulated profits [15, 20].
* **Point & Figure "Pole" Rule**: After an uninterrupted price run of 10 or more boxes without a correction (a "pole"), place a trailing stop at the price level where a 3-box reversal would occur [21].

---

#### **Mathematical Sizing & Risk Formula**
* **5% Account Risk Rule**: The maximum capital risked on any single trade must not exceed **5% of total account equity** [22].
\\[\text{Max Dollar Risk} = \text{Total Equity} \times 0.05\\]

* **Position Sizing Formula**:
\\[\text{Position Size (Contracts/Shares)} = \frac{\text{Total Equity} \times 0.05}{\text{Entry Price} - \text{Stop-Loss Price}}\\]
*(Where entry price minus stop-loss price equals dollar risk per unit)* [16, 17, 22].

### 3. Types of Trading Orders

Murphy defines 5 primary order types used in tactical execution [15, 23-25]:

1. **Market Order**
   * **Definition**: Instructs the broker to buy or sell immediately at the best current market price [23].
   * **Rules & Trade-offs**: Ensures immediate position entry, protecting against missing fast markets [23, 25]. However, it risks bad fills or "chasing" extended markets [25].

2. **Limit Order**
   * **Definition**: Specifies an exact price (or better) that the trader is willing to pay or accept [15]. A **buy limit** is placed *below* current market price; a **sell limit** is placed *above* [15].
   * **Rules & Trade-offs**: Provides precise price control (e.g., buying a pullback near support) [15, 25]. However, execution is not guaranteed; if prices bounce before touching the limit, the trade is missed [15, 25].

3. **Stop Order (Stop-Loss / Buy Stop / Sell Stop)**
   * **Definition**: Specifies a price trigger at which the order automatically converts into a **market order** and executes at the best available price [15]. A **buy stop** is placed *above* the market; a **sell stop** is placed *below* [15].
   * **Rules & Trade-offs**: Used to enter breakouts (buy stop above resistance), limit losses (sell stop below support), or protect profits (trailing stop) [15]. Because it converts to a market order upon activation, the actual fill price may suffer slippage in fast markets [15, 25].

4. **Stop-Limit Order**
   * **Definition**: Combines a stop order with a limit order [24]. It specifies a **stop price** (which activates the order) and a **limit price** (the maximum/minimum acceptable execution price) [24].
   * **Rules & Trade-offs**: Allows a trader to buy or sell a breakout while strictly capping price slippage [24]. However, if prices gap past the limit price after hitting the stop, the order remains unfilled [24, 25].

5. **Market-If-Touched (M.I.T.) Order**
   * **Definition**: Placed below the market to buy or above to sell (like a limit order), but converts into a **market order** as soon as the specified limit price is touched [24].
   * **Rules & Trade-offs**: Solves the limit order problem where prices touch a level without filling the order, ensuring entry on dips [24, 25]. However, M.I.T. orders are not permitted on all exchanges [25].

### 4. Intraday Pivot Points

#### **Core Concepts & Definitions**
* **Intraday Pivot Points**: A short-term timing technique that combines key daily price levels with specific intraday time windows to anticipate daily turning points, execute early entries, and maintain tighter protective stops [26, 27].

---

#### **Key Variables & Time Framework**
* **7 Key Price Levels** [26]:
  1. \\(H_{\text{prev}}\\) = Previous Day's High
  2. \\(L_{\text{prev}}\\) = Previous Day's Low
  3. \\(C_{\text{prev}}\\) = Previous Day's Close
  4. \\(O_{\text{curr}}\\) = Current Day's Open
  5. \\(H_{\text{curr}}\\) = Current Day's High
  6. \\(L_{\text{curr}}\\) = Current Day's Low
  7. \\(C_{\text{curr}}\\) = Current Day's Close

* **4 Intraday Time Periods** [26]:
  1. Market Open
  2. 30 minutes after the Open
  3. Midday (~12:30 PM New York Time)
  4. 35 minutes before the Close

---

#### **Trading Rules & Execution Mechanics (Bullish Buy Example)**
1. **Initial Filter**: Do not initiate positions during the **first 30 minutes** of trading [27].
2. **Morning Setup**: If the market opens above the previous close (\\(C_{\text{prev}}\\)) but below the previous high (\\(H_{\text{prev}}\\)), place a **buy stop** just above \\(H_{\text{prev}}\\) [27].
3. **Stop Loss**: If filled, place a protective **sell stop** below the current day's low (\\(L_{\text{curr}}\\)) [27].
4. **Late-Day Setup**: If no trade has triggered by **35 minutes before the close**, place a **buy stop** above today's current high (\\(H_{\text{curr}}\\)) with a protective stop below today's open (\\(O_{\text{curr}}\\)) [27].
5. **Dynamic Adjustment**: As the session progresses, narrow pivot entry points and protective stops [27].
6. **Confirmation Requirement**: For a valid buy signal, price **must close above both** \\(C_{\text{prev}}\\) and \\(O_{\text{curr}}\\) [27]. Signals given later in the day carry greater technical weight [27].

### 5. Summary of Money Management Guidelines

#### **Quantitative Capital Allocation Rules**
* **50% Capital Allocation Rule**: Limit total invested trading funds to **50% of total capital** [22, 28]. The remaining 50% is placed in Treasury Bills as a cash reserve for drawdowns [22, 28].
* **10%–15% Commitment Rule**: Total margin committed to any **single market** must not exceed **10%–15% of total equity** [22].
* **5% Risk Rule**: The maximum dollar loss permitted on any **single trade** is **5% of total equity** [22].
* **20%–25% Market Group Rule**: Total margin committed to any single **correlated market group** (e.g., precious metals or foreign currencies) must be limited to **20%–25% of total equity** [29, 30].

---

#### **Risk-to-Reward & Pyramiding Rules**
* **3-to-1 Reward-to-Risk Ratio**: Potential profit objective must be at least **3 times** the potential loss before taking a trade [31, 32].
* **Pyramiding (Adding Units)** Rules [33]:
  1. Each successive position layer must be **smaller** than the previous layer [33].
  2. Add **only to winning positions**; never add to a losing position [33].
  3. Adjust protective stops to the **breakeven point** as new units are added [33].

---

#### **Murphy's 13 Summary Rules for Money Management & Trading** [32, 33]
1. Trade in the direction of the intermediate trend [32].
2. In uptrends, buy dips; in downtrends, sell bounces [32].
3. Let profits run; cut losses short [32].
4. Use protective stops to limit losses [32].
5. Don't trade impulsively; have a plan [32].
6. Plan your work and work your plan [32].
7. Use strict money management principles [32].
8. Diversify, but don't over-diversify [30, 32].
9. Employ at least a **3 to 1** reward-to-risk ratio [32].
10. Follow pyramiding rules (smaller layers, add only to winners, never add to losers, raise stops to breakeven) [33].
11. **Never meet a margin call**; don't throw good money after bad [33].
12. Close out losing positions before winning ones [33].
13. Make trading decisions away from the market (preferably when markets are closed) [33].

- "Some General Money Management Guidelines Admittedly, the question of portfolio management can get very complicated, requiring the use of advanced statistical measures. We'll approach it here on a relatively simple level. The following are some general guidelines that can be helpful in allocating one's funds and in determining the size of one's trading commitments. These guidelines refer primarily to futures trading. 1. Total invested funds should be limited to 50% of total capital. The balance is placed in Treasury Bills. This means that at any one time, no more than half of the trader's capital should be committed to the markets. The other half acts as a reserve during periods of adversity and drawdown. If, for example, the size of the account is $100,000, only $50,000 would be available for trading purposes."
- "4. Total margin in any market group should be limited to 20-25% of total equity. The purpose of this criteria is to protect against getting too heavily involved in any one market group. Markets within groups tend to move together. Gold and silver are part of the precious metals group and usually trend in the same direction. Putting on full positions in each market in the same group would frustrate the principle of diversification. Market commitments in the same group should be controlled. These guidelines are fairly standard in the futures industry, but can be modified to the trader's needs. Some traders are more aggressive than others and take bigger positions. Others are more conservative. The important consideration is that some form of diversification be employed that allows for preservation of capital and some measure of protection during losing periods. (Although these guidelines relate to futures trading, the general principles of money management and asset allocation can be applied to all forms of investing.)"
- "Diversification Versus Concentration While diversification is one way to limit risk exposure, it can be overdone. If a trader has trading commitments in too many markets at the same time, a few profitable trades may be diluted by a larger number of losing trades. A tradeoff exists and the proper Money Management and Trading Tactics 397 balance must be found. Some successful traders concentrate their trading in a handful of markets. That's fine as long as those markets are the ones that are trending at that time. The more negative correlation between the markets, the more diversification is achieved. Holding long positions in four foreign currency markets at the same time would not be a good example of diversification, since foreign currencies usually trend in the same direction against the U.S. dollar."

That profit objective (the reward) is then balanced against the potential loss if the trade goes wrong (the risk). A com-monly used yardstick is a 3 to 1 reward-to-risk ratio. The profit potential must be at least three times the possible loss if a trade is to be considered. "Letting profits run and cutting losses short" is one of the oldest maxims of trading. Large profits in trading are achieved by staying with persistent trends. Because only a relative handful of trades during the course of a year will generate large profits, it's necessary to maximize those few big winners. Letting profits run is the way that is done. The other side of the coin is to keep losing trades as small as possible. You'd be surprised how many traders do just the opposite."
- "SUMMARY OF MONEY MANAGEMENT AND TRADING GUIDELINES The following list pulls together most of the more important elements of money management and trading. 1. Trade in the direction of the intermediate trend. 2. In uptrends, buy the dips; in downtrends, sell bounces. 3. Let profits run, cut losses short. 4. Use protective stops to limit losses. 5. Don't trade impulsively; have a plan. 6. Plan your work and work your plan. 7. Use money management principles. 8. Diversify, but don't overdo it. 9. Employ at least a 3 to 1 reward-to-risk ratio."
