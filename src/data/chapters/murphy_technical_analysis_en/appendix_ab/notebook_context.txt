### 1. Demand Index (DI)

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Developed in the 1970s by James Sibbet [1, 2], the Demand Index (DI) is an advanced technical oscillator that combines price action and volume into a leading market indicator [3, 4]. It calculates the ratio of buying pressure to selling pressure and fluctuates around a central zero line [1, 4, 5]. Values above zero indicate that buying pressure exceeds selling pressure (positive/bullish), while values below zero indicate that selling pressure exceeds buying pressure (negative/bearish) [4, 5]. DI can be plotted as a histogram or as a continuous line [4, 6].
* **Mathematical Formulas & Variable Definitions**:
  * **Core Ratio**:
    \\[DI = \frac{BP}{SP}\\]
    *(Note: If \\(BP > SP\\), the ratio is inverted as \\(DI = \frac{SP}{BP}\\) in standard software implementations)* [7, 8].
  * **Price Change (\\(P\\)) and Volatility Modifier (\\(K\\))**:
    * \\(P = \text{Percentage change in price}\\) [8].
    * Modified price change: \\(P = P \times K\\) [8].
    * Constant \\(K = \frac{3 \times C}{VA}\\) [8], where:
      * \\(C\\) = Closing price [8].
      * \\(VA\\) (Volatility Average) = \\(10\text{-day average of a 2-day price range } (Highest\ High - Lowest\ Low)\\) [8].
  * **Buying Pressure (\\(BP\\)) & Selling Pressure (\\(SP\\))**:
    * **If prices rise**:
      * \\(BP = V \text{ (Volume)}\\) [8]
      * \\(SP = \frac{V}{P}\\) [8]
    * **If prices decline**:
      * \\(BP = \frac{V}{P}\\) [8]
      * \\(SP = V \text{ (Volume)}\\) [8]
* **Chart Patterns & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Zero-Line Crossings**: A move above zero confirms that buyers have seized control, whereas a drop below zero confirms seller control [4, 5].
  * **Divergence Patterns**:
    * *Bullish (Positive) Divergence*: Price establishes lower reaction lows while DI forms higher lows [4, 5]. This signals that downside volume pressure is exhausting and a price bottom is forming [5].
    * *Bearish (Negative) Divergence*: Price establishes higher reaction highs while DI forms lower peaks [4]. This warns that upside buying volume is weakening ahead of a price top [4].
  * **Indicator Trendline Analysis**: Trendlines drawn directly across the peaks or troughs of a continuous DI line are frequently broken **prior** to the breaking of corresponding price trendlines [6].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Initiate long positions when DI crosses above the zero line or when a bullish divergence is confirmed by a zero-line crossover [4, 5].
  * Initiate short positions or exit longs when DI drops below zero or when a bearish divergence emerges while prices make new highs [4, 5].
  * Use trendline breaks on the DI line as early warning triggers to exit or enter ahead of price trendline breaks [6].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Weekly Treasury Bond Futures (1994–1997)**: Between April and November 1994, T-Bonds declined from 104 to 96 while DI formed higher lows (bullish divergence), confirmed when DI crossed above zero at Point 1 [5]. DI peaked in May 1995 (Point 2) and dropped below zero (Point 3) [4, 5]. In late November 1995, T-Bonds rallied 6 points higher while DI formed a significantly lower peak (Point 4), warning of an impending market top [4].
  * **Weekly General Motors (GM) (1995–1997)**: Plotted as a line, the DI's downward trendline broke a week ahead of GM's price trendline in late 1995, offering an earlier buying entry [6, 9]. In April 1996, GM hit new price highs while DI made lower highs, giving an early signal before a severe June–July price drop [9].

### 2. Herrick Payoff Index (HPI)

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Developed by John Herrick [2, 9], the Herrick Payoff Index (HPI) is designed specifically for commodity futures to measure capital flow into or out of a contract by combining price, volume, and open interest [9, 10]. It helps traders detect whether price trends are supported by genuine capital commitment or driven by liquidation panic [9, 10].
* **Mathematical Formula & Variables**:
  * The HPI mathematically integrates three primary market variables [9, 10]:
    * \\(P\\) = Price change / direction [10].
    * \\(V\\) = Daily Volume [10].
    * \\(OI\\) = Daily Open Interest [9, 10].
  * **Interpretation of Values**:
    * **Positive HPI (\\(>0\\))**: Indicates that open interest is rising alongside advancing prices, projecting higher price targets as new money flows into the market [10].
    * **Negative HPI (\\(<0\\))**: Indicates that funds are flowing out of the market, projecting lower prices [10].
* **Chart Patterns & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Zero-Line Crossings**: A crossing above zero generates a Buy signal ('B'), confirming positive money flow [10, 11]. A crossing below zero generates a Sell signal ('S'), confirming negative money flow [11].
  * **Weekly Confirmation**: HPI is most reliable on weekly charts, where noisy false signals are filtered out [11].
  * **Divergence Patterns**:
    * *Bullish Divergence*: Prices make lower reaction lows while HPI forms higher lows, showing that capital outflow has ceased and money flow is turning positive [11, 12].
    * *Bearish Divergence*: Prices hit new highs while HPI forms lower peaks, warning that open interest is failing to support the rally [11, 12].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Buy when HPI crosses above zero or when a bullish divergence develops in an oversold price area [10, 11].
  * Sell or short when HPI crosses below zero or when a bearish divergence signals capital exit during a price rally [10, 11].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Daily Coffee Futures (1997–1998)**: Displayed as a histogram, zero-line upward crosses generated precise Buy ('B') signals and downward crosses generated Sell ('S') signals [11].
  * **Weekly Treasury Bond Futures (1991–1998)**:
    * In early 1993, T-Bonds rallied nearly 10 points higher while HPI formed a lower peak (bearish divergence), warning of the major 1994 price collapse [11]. HPI violated zero in October 1993 [11].
    * During early-to-mid 1994, T-Bonds made lower lows while HPI created higher lows (bullish divergence), turning positive in December 1994 right at the market bottom [12].
    * In late 1995, a negative divergence formed after bonds rallied over 25 points, signaling the end of the advance [12].

### 3. STARC Bands (Stoller Average Range Channels)

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Created by Manning Stoller [13, 14], STARC Bands utilize Welles Wilder's Average True Range (ATR) to construct volatility bands around a simple moving average [13, 15]. Unlike breakout bands, STARC Bands are used as **trading filters** and risk parameters to identify overextended price extremes [13].
* **Mathematical Formulas & Variable Definitions**:
  * **Center Line (\\(MA\\))**: \\(6\text{-period Simple Moving Average}\\) [13].
  * **Average True Range (\\(ATR\\))**: \\(15\text{-period Average True Range}\\) [13].
    * *True Range* is the greatest of: \\((High_t - Low_t)\\), \\(|High_t - Close_{t-1}|\\), or \\(|Low_t - Close_{t-1}|\\) [15].
  * **Upper Band (\\(STARC+\\))**: 
    \\[STARC+ = 6\text{-period } MA + (2 \times 15\text{-period } ATR)\\] [13]
  * **Lower Band (\\(STARC-\\))**: 
    \\[STARC- = 6\text{-period } MA - (2 \times 15\text{-period } ATR)\\] [13]
* **Chart Patterns & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Extreme Zone Penetration**: Price movement outside the STARC bands is rare and represents an extreme, high-risk situation [13].
  * **Risk Classifications**:
    * *At or above STARC+*: High-risk buying zone / Low-risk selling or profit-taking zone [13].
    * *At or below STARC-*: High-risk selling zone / Low-risk buying zone [13].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Do **not** buy when price reaches or exceeds STARC+; use STARC+ to take profits or establish short positions [13, 16].
  * Do **not** sell or short when price reaches or drops below STARC-; use STARC- as an advantageous buying area [13, 16].
  * Applies effectively across all timeframes, from 5-minute intraday charts to weekly bar charts, preventing traders from "chasing" extended markets [16].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Weekly Gold Futures (1997)**:
    * In February 1997 (Point 1), gold prices overshot the STARC- band [16]. The bands indicated a high-risk selling zone; waiting paid off as gold rallied \$22 over three weeks to reach the STARC+ band (Point 2), creating a low-risk shorting opportunity [16].
    * In July 1997 (Point 3), gold dropped below STARC- and stabilized into a 12-week sideways range rather than continuing downward [16].
    * In November–December 1997 (Point 4), gold touched STARC- three times; in each instance, prices stabilized or bounced higher for 1–2 weeks [16].

### 4. Keltner Channels

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Originally developed by Chester Keltner in 1960 and re-introduced with modifications by Linda Raschke [17], Keltner Channels are volatility-based bands derived from Average True Range (ATR) [17]. Unlike STARC Bands, Keltner Channels are designed as **volatility breakout indicators** (a graphical representation of a channel breakout system) [17].
* **Mathematical Formulas & Variable Definitions** *(Linda Raschke's Modification)*:
  * **Center Line (\\(EMA\\))**: \\(20\text{-period Exponential Moving Average of closes}\\) [17].
  * **Average True Range (\\(ATR\\))**: \\(10\text{-period Average True Range}\\) [17].
  * **Upper Band (\\(Plus\ Channel\\))**: 
    \\[\text{Plus Band} = 20\text{-period } EMA + (2 \times 10\text{-period } ATR)\\] [17]
  * **Lower Band (\\(Minus\ Channel\\))**: 
    \\[\text{Minus Band} = 20\text{-period } EMA - (2 \times 10\text{-period } ATR)\\] [17]
* **Chart Patterns & Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Volatility Breakout Confirmation**:
    * A close **above** the upper (Plus) band signals an upward volatility breakout and confirms an advancing trend [17].
    * A close **below** the lower (Minus) band signals a downward volatility breakout and confirms a declining trend [17].
  * **Moving Average Support**: Following a bullish breakout, pullbacks to the middle line (20-period EMA) act as primary support in an ongoing uptrend [18, 19].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Buy when price closes above the Plus band [17, 18]. Add to positions or enter on pullbacks that touch the 20-period EMA [18, 19].
  * Sell / short when price closes below the Minus band [17, 20]. Maintain short positions until a close above the Plus band occurs [18, 20].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Daily March 1998 Copper Futures (1997)**: In late October 1997 (Point 1), copper closed below the Minus band, signaling a bearish volatility breakout [20]. Copper dropped 16 cents over the next two months as the negative signal remained active [18, 20].
  * **Daily March 1998 Coffee Futures (1997)**: Point 1 registered two consecutive closes above the Plus band, triggering a bullish buy signal [18]. Prices then pulled back to test support at the 20-period EMA (Point 2) before launching a 30-cent rally over a few weeks [18, 19].

### 5. Market Profile

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Developed by J. Peter Steidlmayer [21-23], Market Profile is a statistical approach to price analysis that displays price frequency distribution over time as a horizontal price histogram [24, 25].
  * **Normal Curve / Bell Curve**: Market activity distributes around a modal (fairest) price [25, 26]. The market's purpose is to facilitate trade via a dual auction mode [23, 27].
  * **Equilibrium vs. Disequilibrium**: A symmetric profile represents market balance (equilibrium) between buyers and sellers [28-30]. A skewed or asymmetric profile represents market imbalance (disequilibrium / trend mode) [29, 30].
  * **Time Price Opportunity (TPO)**: The fundamental unit of Market Profile [31]. Each TPO letter identifies a specific price level at which the market traded during a specific 30-minute time period (A–X for midnight to noon, a–x for noon to midnight) [31, 32].
  * **Value Area (VA)**: The price range surrounding the fairest price containing **70%** (specifically 68.3%, or 1 standard deviation) of the session's total TPOs [26, 33]. It represents fair value for short-term traders [34, 35].
  * **Price vs. Value**:
    * *Accepted Price*: Price zone where the market spends substantial time (fair value) [35].
    * *Rejected Price*: Price zone where the market spends very little time (unfair high or unfair low) [35].
    * Short-term traders trade where \\(\text{Price} = \text{Value}\\) [35]. Longer-term traders seek advantageous prices where \\(\text{Price} \neq \text{Value}\\) [34, 35].
  * **Trader Timeframes & Roles**:
    * *Short-Term Trader (Day Timeframe / Locals)*: Limited time, forced to trade today, seeks fair price, establishes the **Initial Balance** in the first hour [34, 36].
    * *Longer-Term Trader (Commercials, Swing / Position Traders)*: Time is an ally, seeks advantageous prices (buyers seek lower, sellers seek higher), drives **Range Extension** and directional trends [34, 36, 37].
  * **Responsive vs. Initiating Behavior**:
    * *Responsive*: Longer-term trader buys below fair value (after price drops) or sells above fair value (after price rises) [38].
    * *Initiating*: Longer-term trader buys above fair value (after price rises) or sells below fair value (after price drops), signaling high conviction [38].
* **Profile Patterns & Daily Range Development**:
  * **Normal Day**: Longer-term traders inactive; range set in first hour (initial balance / pioneer range); prices rotate symmetrically all day [39, 40].
  * **Normal Variation Day**: Short-term traders set initial balance; longer-term traders enter and extend the range (up to double the initial balance) before balance re-establishes [40].
  * **Trend Day**: Longer-term traders dominate early, driving range extension continuously in one direction; market closes near the extreme and value area expands directionally [40, 41].
  * **Neutral Day**: Both longer-term buyers and sellers enter at opposite extremes (range extension up and down), indicating two-sided competition [40].
* **Structural Elements & Tracking Rules**:
  * **Single Print Extremes**: Require a **minimum of 2 single prints** to confirm valid longer-term trader competition at range highs or lows [42, 43]. A single print alone represents a local top/bottom where no competition occurred [42].
  * **Range Extension**: Occurs when longer-term traders enter with sufficient volume to tip the initial balance and extend the range [33, 43, 44].
  * **Value Area TPO Buying/Selling Count**:
    1. Draw a horizontal line through the modal (fairest) price [33].
    2. Count TPOs above and below the fairest price up to single prints [33].
    3. The side with the **smaller number of TPOs** represents the longer-term trader imbalance (since longer-term trading accounts for a smaller percentage of volume in the fair value area) [33]. E.g., 22 TPOs above vs. 12 below indicates net TPO selling (bearish bias) [33, 41].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Intel Corporation (Panel #1, Figure B.7)**: Demonstrates single print extremes [43]. Selling extreme formed from \\(77\ \frac{11}{32}\\) to \\(77\ \frac{5}{32}\\); Buying extreme formed from \\(73\ \frac{31}{32}\\) to \\(75\\) (\\(\ge 2\\) TPOs each) [43].
  * **Coffee (Panel #2, Figure B.7)**: Demonstrates range extension [43]. Initial balance set in periods A & B; longer-term sellers drove range extension down in periods C, H, and I [43, 44].
  * **S&P 500 Index (Panel #3, Figure B.7)**: Value Area TPO count at fairest price \\(1039.20\\) showed TPO selling count = \\(59\\) vs TPO buying count = \\(100\\) [43]. Smaller count on the sell side indicates net TPO buying imbalance, implying prices need to move higher for balance [33, 43].
  * **Sugar (Figure B.5)**: Comparing 4 consecutive daily profiles (Feb 10–13) showing an apparent uptrend versus a combined cumulative profile revealing a 4-day balanced distribution, signaling an impending directional move [38, 45, 46].

- "3. The Value Area is determined each trading session by price rotations around the modal price (i.e., the price with the highest TPO count or the fairest price). The value area is computed by counting 70% of all TPOs surrounding the fairest price. In other words, the value area is an estimate of fair value which is approximated by one standard deviation of the session's trading volume (recall the student example earlier). When a longer term trader makes a trade in the value area, he is buying low or selling high in relation to a longer term view, not in relation to today's value. This behavior creates an imbalance in today's value area. Longer term trader activity is measured by counting TPOs. The following procedure can be used to determine which side contains the longer term imbalance, 1) a line is drawn through the fairest price, and 2) TPOs are counted on either side of the fairest price until a single print is encountered. The imbalance is assigned to the side with the smaller number of TPOs because the longer term trader activity represents the smaller percentage of total trade in the value area. As their price objectives differ, longer term buyers and sellers generally do not trade with each other at the same price and at the same time. It is the behavioral interaction between these two distinct timeframe types of activity that causes the profile to develop as it does. The Short Term Trader and Longer Term Trader Play Different Roles: Short term and longer term traders play key, but different, roles in facilitating trade. A market's initial balance (i.e., a place where two-sided trade can occur) is usually established in the first hour of trade by short term buyers and sellers (day timeframe activity) in their pursuit of a fair price. Most of the day's activity occurs in the fair price or value area. Prices above and below this developed fair value area offer opportunity and are advantageous to longer term traders. With time on their side, longer term traders can either accept or reject prices away from fair value. By entering the market with large enough volume, longer term buyers and sellers can upset the initial balance, thereby extending the price range higher or lower. The longer term trader is responsible for the way the day's range develops and for the duration of the longer term auction. In other words, the role of the longer term trader is to move the market directionally."
- "Price and Value: The distinction between price and value defines a market-generated opportunity. There are two kinds of prices: 1) those that are accepted—defined as a price area where the market trades over time and 2) those that are rejected— defined as a price area where the market spends very little time. A rejected price is considered excessive in the market—defined as an unfair high or unfair low. Price and value are all but synonymous for short term traders as they ordinarily trade in the fair value area. For longer term traders, however, the concept that price equals value is often inaccurate. Price is observable and objective while value is perceived and subjective, depending upon the particular needs of longer term traders. For example, a price at the top of today's range, while excessive or unfair for today, is cheap to the longer term trader who believes that prices next week will be much higher (i.e., today's price is below next week's anticipated value)."

Time Frames and Trader Behavior: The concept of different time frames was introduced to help explain the behavioral patterns of market participants. Market activity is divided into two timeframe categories, short term and longer term. The short term activity is defined as day time frame activity where traders are forced to trade today (e.g., locals, day traders and options traders on expiration day fall into this category). With limited time to act, the short term trader is seeking a fair price. Short term buyers and sellers do trade with each other at the same time and at the same price. Longer term activity is defined by all other timeframe activity (e.g., commercials, swing traders, and all other position traders fall into this category). - "Market Profile, on the other hand, offers an alternative approach to traditional trend analysis by evaluating market activity over different time periods. In its simplest form, an evaluation of the profile graphic on consecutive days can help define the start or continuation of the short term price trend. For example, if today's value area is higher than yesterday's value area, then the current market price trend is up. Moreover, if tomorrow's value area is higher than today's, then the current market uptrend has continued. By monitoring market activity in this fashion, the trader is able to readily identify trend continuation or change. CONCLUSION The Market Profile method can be used to analyze any price data series for which continuous transaction activity is available. This includes listed and unlisted equities, U.S. government notes and bonds (prices or yields), commodity futures and options, where applicable. The profile graphic presents the movement of prices, per unit of time, in two dimensions—vertically (i.e., directionally) and horizontally (i.e., frequency of occurrence). When price action is viewed in this way, a picture of price discovery unfolds which is unavailable in the traditional one dimensional (vertical) bar chart."
