### 1. Moving Averages as a Smoothing Device with Time Lag

#### Core Concepts & Definitions
* **Definition:** A **moving average** is a trend-following device whose primary function is to identify or signal that a new trend has begun or that an old trend has ended or reversed [1]. It tracks the progress of a trend and can be conceptualized as a curving trendline [1].
* **Trend Follower (Not a Leader):** Moving averages never anticipate market action; they only react [1]. A moving average is a follower that confirms trend direction after price action has already taken place [1].
* **Smoothing Function:** By averaging price data over a specific time window, random daily price fluctuations ("noise") are smoothed out, making the underlying primary or intermediate trend much clearer to see [2].

```
  Price Bar Chart with Moving Average Curve:
  
  Price
    |       /\    /\      /  <-- Price Bars (High / Low / Close)
    |  /\  /  \  /  \    /
    | /  \/    \/    \  /
    |/________________\/____  <-- Curving Moving Average Line (Smoothed Trend)
    +------------------------ Time
```

#### Time Lag Mechanics & Sensitivity Trade-Off
* **Inheritance of Lag:** By its mathematical nature, averaging creates an unavoidable **time lag** behind market action [2].
* **Shorter Moving Averages (e.g., 5, 10, or 20 days):**
  * Hug the price action closely and significantly reduce time lag [2].
  * **Advantage:** Provide trend signals much earlier in the move [3].
  * **Disadvantage:** Highly sensitive to short-term random noise, generating more false signals (**whipsaws**) and increasing commission costs [3, 4].
* **Longer Moving Averages (e.g., 50 or 200 days):**
  * Trai price at a greater distance, smoothing out minor market fluctuations [2, 5].
  * **Advantage:** Highly reliable during established trend phases; keep the trader locked into the major trend without getting shaken out during lateral consolidations [2, 5, 6].
  * **Disadvantage:** Higher time lag; when a major trend reversal actually occurs, longer averages give back a substantial portion of accumulated profits before generating a exit signal [6].
* **Murphy's Rule of Sensitivity:** Shorter averages perform better when a trend is in the process of reversing (providing fast exits), whereas longer averages perform better as long as an established trend remains in force [6].

#### Prices Averaged & Centering
* **Which Prices to Average:**
  * **Closing Price:** The standard and most widely used price point [5, 7].
  * **Midpoint Value:** \\(\frac{\text{High} + \text{Low}}{2}\\) [5].
  * **Typical / Average Price:** \\(\frac{\text{High} + \text{Low} + \text{Close}}{3}\\) [8].
  * **Price Bands:** Calculating separate moving averages for daily Highs and daily Lows to form a volatility buffer or neutral zone [8].
* **Centering the Average:** Statistically, a moving average should be centered in the middle of its time span (e.g., plotting a 10-day average 5 days back) [9]. However, centering introduces severe signal lag, making it unusable for real-time trading; thus, technical traders plot moving averages at the latest time period [9].

### 2. Simple vs. Exponential Moving Averages (and Linearly Weighted)

#### Simple Moving Average (SMA)
* **Definition:** The arithmetic mean of prices over a set number of periods [10-12].
* **Mathematical Formula:**

\\[\text{SMA} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} P_i}{N}\\]

* **Calculation Variables:**
  * \\(P_i\\): Closing price (or selected price) for period \\(i\\).
  * \\(N\\): Total number of periods in the average (e.g., 10 days).
  * *Daily Calculation Shortcut:* 

\\[\text{SMA}_{\text{today}} = \text{SMA}_{\text{yesterday}} + \frac{P_{\text{today}} - P_{\text{today}-N}}{N}\\]

  [7].
* **Two Major Criticisms:**
  1. **Limited Window:** It only takes into account price action within the specified \\(N\\)-day period and completely drops prior history [11].
  2. **Equal Weighting:** It assigns equal weight to every day in the calculation (e.g., in a 10-day SMA, Day 1 and Day 10 each carry an identical 10% weight; in a 5-day SMA, each day carries 20%) [11].

#### Linearly Weighted Moving Average (LWMA)
* **Definition:** An average that attempts to solve the equal weighting problem by giving heavier weight to recent price action [13].
* **Mathematical Formula:**

\\[\text{LWMA} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} (i \times P_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} i}\\]

* **Calculation Variables:**
  * \\(i\\): Multiplier weight assigned to each day (from \\(1\\) for the oldest day up to \\(N\\) for the newest day).
  * \\(P_i\\): Price on day \\(i\\).
  * *Example:* For a 10-day LWMA, Day 10 close is multiplied by 10, Day 9 by 9, ..., down to Day 1 multiplied by 1 [13]. The sum is divided by the sum of multipliers (\\(10 + 9 + 8 + \dots + 1 = 55\\)) [13].
* **Limitation:** Solves the weighting issue but still only includes prices within the \\(N\\)-day window [13].

#### Exponentially Smoothed Moving Average (EMA)
* **Definition:** A weighted average that solves both criticisms of the SMA by assigning greater weight to recent data while retaining all historical data in the instrument's life span [14].
* **Mathematical Formula:**

\\[\text{EMA}_{\text{today}} = \left( P_{\text{today}} \times K \right) + \left( \text{EMA}_{\text{yesterday}} \times (1 - K) \right)\\]

* **Calculation Variables & Constants:**
  * \\(P_{\text{today}}\\): Current closing price.
  * \\(\text{EMA}_{\text{yesterday}}\\): Previous day's EMA value.
  * \\(K\\): Weighting percentage factor applied to the most recent price:

\\[K = \frac{2}{N + 1}\\]

* **Weighting Examples:**
  * A 10% (\\(0.10\\)) weight on current close + 90% (\\(0.90\\)) on the previous EMA equals a 20-day EMA equivalent [15].
  * A 5% (\\(0.05\\)) weight on current close + 95% (\\(0.95\\)) on the previous EMA equals a 40-day EMA equivalent [15].

#### Comparative Evaluation (SMA vs. EMA)
* **Sensitivity:** EMAs react faster to recent price moves than SMAs of the same length [16].
* **Practical Effectiveness:** While EMAs are popular in computerized trading systems, Murphy notes that SMAs remain the most widely used by technicians and that empirical evidence does not prove EMAs consistently outperform simple averages in actual trading [17, 18].

### 3. Single, Double, and Triple Crossover Systems

```
  Single Crossover           Double Crossover            Triple Crossover (4-9-18)
 Price vs. Moving Avg     Fast MA vs. Slow MA          Fast (4d), Med (9d), Slow (18d)
 
    Price      MA            Fast MA    Slow MA          4d     9d     18d
      \       /                \       /               \      |      /
       \     /                  \     /                 \     |     /
        \   /                    \   /                   \    |    /
       [ Buy ]                  [ Buy ]                 [ Confirmed Buy ]
```

#### Single Moving Average System
* **Trading Rules:**
  * **Buy Signal:** Generated when the closing price moves above the moving average line [12, 17].
  * **Sell Signal:** Generated when the closing price moves below the moving average line [12, 17].
* **Confirmation Rule:** A signal is stronger when the slope of the moving average line itself turns in the direction of the price crossing (e.g., the average line curves upward as prices cross above it) [4, 17].

#### Double Crossover Method
* **Concept:** Uses two moving averages (one fast/short, one slow/long) to reduce whipsaws [19].
* **Trading Rules:**
  * **Buy Signal:** Generated when the shorter moving average crosses above the longer moving average [19, 20].
  * **Sell Signal:** Generated when the shorter moving average crosses below the longer moving average [19].
* **Popular Combinations:**
  * **Futures:** 5-day and 20-day averages [19, 21].
  * **Futures / Stocks:** 9-day and 18-day, or 10-day and 50-day averages [19, 22, 23].

#### Triple Crossover Method (4-9-18 Day System)
* **Concept:** Developed by R.C. Allen for commodities trading, using three moving averages to stagger alerts and confirmations [21, 24].
* **Proper Alignment Rules:**
  * **Bullish Trend Alignment:** 4-day MA > 9-day MA > 18-day MA [24].
  * **Bearish Trend Alignment:** 4-day MA < 9-day MA < 18-day MA [24].
* **Trading Rules & Sequential Signals:**
  * **Buying Alert:** In a downtrend, the 4-day MA turns up and crosses above both the 9-day and 18-day MAs [23].
  * **Confirmed Buy Signal:** Triggered when the 9-day MA subsequently crosses above the 18-day MA, establishing full bullish alignment [23].
  * **Selling Alert (Profit Taking):** In an uptrend, the 4-day MA dips below both the 9-day and 18-day MAs [25].
  * **Confirmed Sell Short Signal:** Triggered when the 9-day MA crosses below the 18-day MA [25].

### 4. The 50 and 200-Day Moving Averages

* **Roles in Market Trend Hierarchy:**
  * **50-Day Moving Average (10-Week Average):** The primary benchmark used by stock traders to measure the **intermediate-term trend** [18, 22].
  * **200-Day Moving Average (40-Week Average):** The classic benchmark used to track the **major or primary long-term trend** on daily and weekly stock charts [2, 18, 26].
* **Support & Resistance Dynamics:**
  * In ongoing bull markets, intermediate pullbacks frequently find significant dynamic support at or near the 50-day or 200-day moving averages [2, 26, 27].
  * During sideways or consolidation phases, short-term averages move back and forth repeatedly, while prices remain above the 200-day moving average, preserving the long-term trend orientation [2, 5].
* **Crossover Interactions:**
  * **Intermediate Signal:** 10-day MA crossing above/below the 50-day MA generates intermediate trend buy/sell signals [19, 22].
  * **Major Bull/Bear Signal:** The interaction between the 50-day and 200-day moving averages defines major market health (e.g., a 50-day crossing above a 200-day marks major bull market conditions) [2, 5, 18].

### 5. Price and Time Filters

Filters are criteria used to verify valid trendline or moving average breakouts and eliminate bad signals or whipsaws during choppy, trendless markets [28, 29].

```
                  +-----------------------------------+
                  |         BREAKOUT FILTERS          |
                  +-----------------------------------+
                                    |
            +-----------------------+-----------------------+
            |                                               |
  [ PRICE FILTERS ]                               [ TIME FILTERS ]
  • 3% Penetration Rule (Major Trends)            • Two-Day Rule (2 Consecutive Closes)
  • 1% Penetration Rule (Short-Term)              • Friday Close Rule (Weekly Confirmation)
  • Full Point Rule (Stock Charts)
```

#### Price Filters
* **3% Penetration Rule:** Requires that prices close beyond a trendline, support/resistance level, or moving average by at least 3% of the price level where the line was broken [28-30].
  * *Example:* If gold breaks a major trendline at \$400, prices must close 3% (\$12) below the trendline at \$388 to confirm a valid breakdown [30].
* **1% Penetration Rule:** A smaller percentage filter applied for shorter-term trading signals [29, 30].
* **Full Point Penetration:** A criterion commonly used by stock chartists requiring a full \$1.00 move beyond a resistance or support level rather than fractional price penetrations [30].

#### Time Filters
* **Two-Day Rule:** Requires that prices close beyond a trendline or moving average for **two consecutive trading days** [29, 31]. A single-day penetration is ignored as a potential whipsaw [31].
* **Friday Close Rule:** Requires a close beyond the breakout point on Friday to confirm a valid weekly signal [29, 31].

#### Band & Channel Filters
* **Percentage Envelopes:** Placing boundaries at fixed percentages above and below a moving average (e.g., 3% envelope around a 21-day SMA for short term; 5% envelope around a 10-week MA for long term) to filter overextended market conditions [32].
* **Weekly Channel Rules:** Using channel breakout signals (such as 1-week or 2-week price channel breakouts) to confirm moving average crossover signals before taking market positions [33].

3. **Light Crude Oil Futures (Daily Bar Chart):**
   * *5-Day and 20-Day Crossover:* Demonstrates how a 5-day MA crossing below a 20-day MA in October captured a major, multi-month downtrend in crude oil [19, 21].

   * *4-9-18 Day Triple Crossover:* Shows a bottom formation where the 4-day MA turned up first across the 9-day and 18-day MAs, followed by the 9-day crossing above the 18-day to confirm a major buy signal [23, 25, 32, 35].
5. **Dow Jones Industrial Average (Daily & Weekly Charts):**
   * *Moving Average Envelopes:* Demonstrates 3% envelopes around a 21-day SMA on daily charts and 5% envelopes around a 10-week SMA on weekly charts to identify overextended market extremes [32, 36, 37].

- "What Constitutes a Valid Breaking of a Trendline? As a general rule, a close beyond the trendline is more significant than just an intraday penetration. To go a step further, sometimes even a closing penetration is not enough. Most technicians employ a variety of time and price filters in an attempt to isolate valid trendline penetrations and eliminate bad signals or "whipsaws." One example of a price filter is the 3% penetration criteria. This price filter is used mainly for the breaking of longer term trend-lines, but requires that the trendline be broken, on a closing basis, by at least 3%. (The 3% rule doesn't apply to some financial futures, such as the interest rate markets.)"

Filters Most chartists require a close beyond a previous resistance peak instead of just an intraday penetration. Second, a price filter of some type might be used. One such example is a percentage penetration criterion (such as 1% or 3%). Third, the two day penetration rule could be used as an example of a time filter. Another time filter could be a Friday close beyond the previous peak. The volume on the upside breakout might also provide a clue to its reliability."
- "If, for example, gold prices broke a major up trendline at $400, prices would have to close below that line by 3% of the price level where the line was broken (in this case, prices would have to close $12 below the trendline, or at $388). Obviously, a $12 penetration criteria would not be appropriate for shorter term trading. Perhaps a 1% criterion would serve better in such cases. The % rule represents just one type of price filter. Stock chartists, for example, might require a full point penetration and ignore fractional moves. There is tradeoff involved in the use of any type of filter. If the filter is too small, it won't be very useful in reducing the impact of whipsaws. If it's too big, then much of the initial move will be missed before a valid signal is given. Here again, the trader must determine what type of filter is best suited to the degree of trend being followed, always making allowances for the differences in the individuals markets."
- "An alternative to a price filter (requiring that a trendline be broken by some predetermined price increment or percentage amount) is a time filter. A common time filter is the two day rule. In other words, to have a valid breaking of a trendline, prices must close beyond the trendline for two successive days. To break 72 Chapter 4 an up trendline, therefore, prices must close under the trendline two days in a row. A one day violation would not count. The 1-3% rule and the two day rule are also applied to the breaking of important support and resistance levels, not just to major trend-lines. Another filter would require a Friday close beyond a major breakout point to ensure a weekly signal."

Percentage envelopes can be used to help determine when a market has gotten overextended in either direction. In other words, they tell us when prices have strayed too far from their moving average line. In order to do this, the envelopes are placed at fixed percentages above and below the average. Shorter term traders, for example, often use 3% envelopes around a simple 21 day moving average. When prices reach one of the envelopes (3% from the average), the short term trend is considered to be overextended. For long range analysis, some possible combinations include 5% envelopes around a 10 week average or a 10% envelope around a 40 week average. (See Figures 9.8a-b.)"
- "The main criticism of the weekly rule is the same one leveled against all trend-following approaches, namely, that it does not catch tops or bottoms. But what trend-following system does? The important point to keep in mind is that the four week rule performs at least as well as most other trend-following systems and better than many, but has the added benefit of incredible simplicity. Adjustments to the 4 Week Rule Although we're treating the four week rule in its original form, there are many adjustments and refinements that can be employed. For one thing, the rule does not have to be used as a trading system. Weekly signals can be employed simply as another technical indicator to identify breakouts and trend reversals. Weekly breakouts can be used as a confirming filter for other techniques, such as moving average crossovers. One or 2 week rules function as excellent filters. A moving average crossover signal could be confirmed by a two week breakout in the same direction in order for a market position to be taken."
