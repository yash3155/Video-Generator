### 1. The Importance of Longer Range Perspective

* **Core Concepts & Definitions**:
  * **Time Frame Compression**: While daily bar charts typically cover 6 to 9 months and represent the primary working tool for short-term traders [1], weekly and monthly bar charts compress price data over longer horizons [2, 3]. A **weekly bar** compresses five trading days into a single bar (spanning up to 5 years of history), while a **monthly bar** compresses an entire month's price action into a single bar (spanning up to 20 years) [2, 4].
  * **Universal Technical Applicability**: Technical analysis principles—including trendlines, support/resistance, percentage retracements, and chart patterns—apply across all time dimensions [5, 6].

* **Key Trading Rules & Principles**:
  * **Disputing Random Walk Theory**: The persistence of well-defined long-term trends lasting for years provides strong evidence against the Random Walk Theory (which claims price changes are serially independent) [7, 8]. Randomness in market action is primarily a short-term phenomenon [8].
  * **Analysis vs. Timing**: Long-term charts are designed strictly for **market analysis and trend forecasting**, not for short-term execution or trade timing [9]. Daily and intraday charts must be used to execute precise entry and exit points [9, 10].
  * **Logarithmic Scaling**: For multi-year long-range charts, logarithmic (semi-log) scaling is often preferred over arithmetic scaling because percentage movements and long-term trendlines align more accurately [11, 12].

### 2. Constructing Continuation Charts for Futures

* **Core Concepts & Limited Lifespan Problem**:
  * Unlike individual stocks, commodity futures contracts have fixed expiration dates and limited trading lifespans (typically 1.5 years) [6, 13]. 
  * Because an expiring contract loses liquidity and ceases to exist, constructing a continuous multi-year price record requires linking sequential futures contracts together [6, 14].

* **Methods of Construction & Formulas**:
  1. **Nearest Contract Method**:
     * **Definition**: Links individual contracts by always plotting the price of the contract nearest to expiration [14, 15]. When the nearest contract expires, plotting automatically rolls over to the next nearest contract [14].
     * **Limitations**: Produces sudden price gaps or jumps on charts when switching contracts if a premium or discount exists between delivery months, and can suffer from extreme spot-month volatility [16].
  2. **Alternative Continuous Methods**:
     * **Next Contract**: Plots the contract immediately following the nearest contract [17].
     * **Highest Open Interest**: Charts whichever delivery month currently holds the highest open interest, reflecting maximum liquidity and truest market value [18].
     * **Gann Contract**: Links data from the same specific calendar month year after year (e.g., plotting only successive November Soybean contracts) [18, 19].

  3. **Perpetual Contract™ (Constant Forward Continuous Contract)**:
     * **Definition**: Invented by Robert Pelletier (CSI), this method creates a continuous, gap-free historical price series looking a fixed time distance into the future [20, 21].
     * **Mathematical Concept & Linear Extrapolation Formula**:
       The contract value is calculated as a weighted average / linear interpolation between the two futures contracts that bracket the chosen forward target date [20-22].
       \\[\text{Price}_{\text{continuous}} = P_{\text{nearest}} + \left( P_{\text{next}} - P_{\text{nearest}} \right) \times \frac{D_{\text{target}} - D_{\text{nearest}}}{D_{\text{next}} - D_{\text{nearest}}}\\]
       * **Variables**:
         * \\(P_{\text{nearest}}\\): Closing price of the nearer futures contract [22].
         * \\(P_{\text{next}}\\): Closing price of the second nearest futures contract [22].
         * \\(D_{\text{nearest}}\\): Expiration / reference date of the nearer contract [22].
         * \\(D_{\text{next}}\\): Expiration / reference date of the second nearest contract [22].
         * \\(D_{\text{target}}\\): Selected target date looking a fixed number of weeks/months forward (e.g., 12 weeks or 3 months ahead of the current date) [20, 22].
     * **Purpose**: While nearest-month charts are sufficient for standard visual analysis, Perpetual Contracts smooth out rollover price distortions and are preferred for computerized back-testing of technical trading systems [19, 23].

### 3. Weekly and Monthly Reversals

* **Definitions**:
  * **Weekly / Monthly Reversals** represent the long-term equivalents of daily key reversal days, carrying much greater technical significance [24, 25].
  * **Upside Weekly Reversal**: During the week, prices move lower to form a new low for the current move, but by Friday's close, the market recovers and closes above the previous Friday's close [24, 26].
  * **Downside Weekly Reversal**: During the week, prices rally to set a new high for the current move, but by Friday's close, the market weakens and closes below the previous Friday's close [24, 26].
  * **Monthly Reversal**: Follows the identical mechanism over a calendar month, where the market sets a new move high/low during the month but closes past the previous month's close [25].

* **Confirmation & Trading Rules**:
  * **Volume & Range**: Reversal significance increases directly with a wider price range for the period and heavier trading volume [27, 28].
  * **Structural Location**: Weekly and monthly reversals carry maximum forecasting weight when they occur at or near major historical support or resistance levels [25].

### 4. Top-Down Chart Analysis (Long Term to Short Term)

* **Core Philosophy**:
  * Analysts must adopt a **"Macro to Micro"** approach, starting with long-term charts and systematically narrowing the focus to short-term charts [29].
  * **Rule of Order**: Never begin analysis with short-term charts [30]. Starting short-term forces frequent revision of conclusions whenever price hits an unobserved long-term trendline or support/resistance level [29, 30].

* **Step-by-Step Top-Down Workflow**:
  1. **Step 1: 20-Year Monthly Bar Chart**: Analyze the long-term trend structure, major chart patterns, primary trendlines, and historical support/resistance levels [29].
  2. **Step 2: 5-Year Weekly Bar Chart**: Review the intermediate trend, identify major chart patterns, check for weekly reversals, and refine key levels [29].
  3. **Step 3: 6–9 Month Daily Bar Chart**: Determine near-term trend direction and form the primary position/trading decision [10, 29].
  4. **Step 4: Intraday Charts (e.g., 5-min or 10-min)**: Inspect detailed short-term action for fine-tuning trade entry and exit timing [10, 29].

* **Intermarket Top-Down Integration**:
  * Extend top-down logic to asset selection: Broad Market Index Trends \\(\rightarrow\\) Strongest Sectors / Industry Groups \\(\rightarrow\\) Individual Leader Stocks \\(\rightarrow\\) Intraday Timing [31].

### 5. Inflation Adjustments

* **Core Question**: Should long-term historical price charts be adjusted for inflation to account for changes in currency purchasing power? [32]
* **Author's Stance**: John Murphy asserts that **no inflation adjustments should be made to long-term price charts** [32].
* **Core Arguments & Technical Rationale**:
  1. **Price Discounts Everything**: A foundational premise of technical analysis is that price action discounts all fundamental factors, including inflation and currency value shifts [32, 33].
  2. **Built-in Market Adjustments**: Currency fluctuations are naturally reflected in price trends—a falling dollar drives commodity prices higher (e.g., 1970s inflation), whereas disinflation causes prices to fall (e.g., 1980s–1990s commodity drops) [32, 34].
  3. **Historical Level Validity**: Visual evidence shows that markets repeatedly turn at unadjusted historical support and resistance levels established decades prior [33]. Adjusting charts for inflation distorts these clear, unadjusted price barriers that market participants actively track [33].
