### 1. Percentage Retracements (50%, 33%, 66% & Fibonacci 38%/62%)

* **Core Concepts**: Following a market move, prices consistently retrace a portion of the previous trend before resuming their move in the original direction [1]. These countertrend moves occur across all trend timeframes (major, secondary, and near-term) and fall into predictable percentage zones based on Dow Theory (which divides trends into thirds) and Fibonacci ratio analysis [1-3].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Percentage Retracement**: A corrective price move in the direction opposite to the prevailing trend, measured as a percentage of the prior impulse move [1].
  * **Minimum Retracement Zone (33%–38%)**: The lower boundary of a normal market correction, combining Dow’s one-third (\\(33\%\\)) with the Fibonacci \\(38.2\%\\) ratio [2, 3].
  * **Halfway Retracement (50%)**: The most widely recognized market tendency where price retraces approximately half of the prior move before regaining momentum [1, 4].
  * **Maximum Retracement Zone (62%–66%)**: The critical boundary of a trend correction, combining the Fibonacci \\(61.8\%\\) (\\(62\%\\)) ratio with Dow’s two-thirds (\\(66\%\\)) mark [2, 3].
* **Mathematical Formulas**:
  * **Uptrend Retracement Price (\\(P_{\text{retrace}}\\))**:
    \\[P_{\text{retrace}} = P_{\text{high}} - \left[ R \times (P_{\text{high}} - P_{\text{low}}) \right]\\]
  * **Downtrend Retracement Price (\\(P_{\text{retrace}}\\))**:
    \\[P_{\text{retrace}} = P_{\text{low}} + \left[ R \times (P_{\text{high}} - P_{\text{low}}) \right]\\]
    *Where*:
    * \\(P_{\text{high}}\\) = Peak price of the prior trend [1].
    * \\(P_{\text{low}}\\) = Trough price of the prior trend [1].
    * \\(R\\) = Retracement ratio multiplier (\\(0.33, 0.382, 0.50, 0.618, 0.667\\)) [2, 3, 5].
  * **Fibonacci Ratio Derivations**:
    * \\(38.2\% = \text{Ratio of alternate Fibonacci numbers (e.g., } 13/34 \approx 0.382 \text{ or } 1 - 0.618)\\) [5].
    * \\(61.8\% = \text{Ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers (e.g., } 8/13 \approx 0.615, 13/21 \approx 0.619)\\) [5, 6].
* **Chart Patterns**: Displayed on bar charts as horizontal support levels below the market during uptrend corrections or horizontal resistance levels above the market during downtrend rallies [3, 7]. Combining Dow and Fibonacci methodologies establishes two primary bands: a **Minimum Retracement Zone (33%–38%)** and a **Maximum Retracement Zone (62%–66%)** [3].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * A healthy trend correction must hold above the \\(62\% \text{--} 66\%\\) maximum zone [3].
  * **Trend Reversal Criterion**: If price moves decisively beyond the two-thirds (\\(66\%\\)) or \\(62\%\\) retracement point, the move is no longer considered a correction; the odds favor a full trend reversal that typically retraces \\(100\%\\) of the prior trend [3, 8].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Entry Zones**: Establish buying positions in an uptrend when prices dip into the \\(33\% \text{--} 50\%\\) or \\(38\% \text{--} 50\%\\) retracement zone [2, 7].
  * **Low-Risk Execution**: The \\(62\% \text{--} 66\%\\) level represents a low-risk buying area in an uptrend (or short-selling area in a downtrend) because risk can be tightly controlled [3].
  * **Stop Placement**: Place protective stops just beyond the \\(66\%\\) retracement line or just past the origin of the prior move (\\(100\%\\) level) [3, 8].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Dow Jones Industrial Average (Daily Chart, Apr 1997 – Aug 1997)**: Murphy illustrates how the Dow measured from its April low to August high respected Fibonacci levels: the first decline held at the \\(38\%\\) line, the second hit the \\(62\%\\) line, and the third stopped near \\(50\%\\), demonstrating that most corrections find support between \\(38\%\\) and \\(50\%\\) [7].
  * **U.S. Treasury Bond Futures (Monthly/Weekly Charts)**: The 1994 price collapse in T-Bonds halted exactly at the \\(38\%\\) Fibonacci retracement of the entire 1981–1993 bull market [9]. In a subsequent cycle, the 1996 decline corrected precisely to the \\(62\%\\) retracement line measured from the 1994 bottom to the early 1996 peak [9].

### 2. Speed Resistance Lines (Speedlines)

* **Core Concepts**: Developed by Edson Gould, speed resistance lines combine trendlines with percentage retracements by dividing the vertical height of a move into thirds [10]. Unlike traditional trendlines that connect price extremes, speedlines measure the **rate of ascent or descent (speed)** of a trend and are drawn directly through price action [10, 11].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Speed Resistance Lines (Speedlines)**: Two angular lines drawn from the origin of a trend through vertical one-third (\\(1/3\\)) and two-thirds (\\(2/3\\)) points of the move to identify dynamic support and resistance levels [10, 12, 13].
* **Mathematical Formulas**:
  * **Bullish Speedlines**:
    1. Identify trend origin \\((T_1, P_{\text{start}})\\) and peak \\((T_2, P_{\text{high}})\\) [12].
    2. Calculate vertical range: \\(V = P_{\text{high}} - P_{\text{start}}\\) [12].
    3. Calculate two-thirds point: \\(P_{2/3} = P_{\text{start}} + \frac{2}{3}V\\) [12].
    4. Calculate one-third point: \\(P_{1/3} = P_{\text{start}} + \frac{1}{3}V\\) [12].
    5. Draw the **2/3 Speedline** from \\((T_1, P_{\text{start}})\\) through \\((T_2, P_{2/3})\\) and the **1/3 Speedline** from \\((T_1, P_{\text{start}})\\) through \\((T_2, P_{1/3})\\) [12, 13].
  * **Bearish Speedlines**:
    1. Identify trend peak origin \\((T_1, P_{\text{high\_start}})\\) and trough \\((T_2, P_{\text{low\_trough}})\\) [12].
    2. Vertical range: \\(V = P_{\text{high\_start}} - P_{\text{low\_trough}}\\) [12].
    3. \\(P_{2/3} = P_{\text{high\_start}} - \frac{2}{3}V\\) and \\(P_{1/3} = P_{\text{high\_start}} - \frac{1}{3}V\\) [12, 13].
* **Chart Patterns**: Displays a fan-shaped structure of two trendlines extending from the beginning of a move through the price action to provide structural support/resistance [10, 11, 13].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Redrawing Requirement**: Every time price sets a new high in an uptrend (or a new low in a downtrend), all speedlines must be redrawn using the new peak or trough [11].
  * **Support/Resistance Interception**:
    * Downward corrections in an uptrend should hold at the upper (\\(2/3\\)) speedline [11].
    * If the \\(2/3\\) line breaks, price typically falls to test the lower (\\(1/3\\)) speedline [11].
    * If the lower (\\(1/3\\)) line breaks, prices will likely decline all the way to the beginning of the prior trend [11].
  * **Role Reversal**: Once broken, a speedline reverses its role (e.g., a broken \\(2/3\\) support line becomes an overhead resistance line on subsequent bounces) [11, 13, 14].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Buy reaction pullbacks near the higher \\(2/3\\) speedline in an uptrend [11, 13].
  * If the \\(2/3\\) line breaks, exit long positions and target the \\(1/3\\) speedline [11].
  * When price bounces off the \\(1/3\\) line, treat the broken \\(2/3\\) line as overhead resistance; a breakout above the \\(2/3\\) line is required to signal a challenge of the old highs [14].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * Murphy provides schematic daily bar chart examples (Figures 4.21a & 4.21b) demonstrating how speedlines act as dynamic support on corrections and convert into overhead resistance barriers once broken [10, 13].

### 3. Gann and Fibonacci Fan Lines

* **Core Concepts**: Geometric techniques that project radial lines from major tops or bottoms. Fibonacci fan lines project trendlines through Fibonacci percentage points (\\(38\%\\) and \\(62\%\\)), whereas Gann fan lines project lines at geometric angles based on specific price-to-time ratios [14, 15].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Fibonacci Fan Lines**: Radial lines drawn from a major price extreme through vertical \\(38\%\\) and \\(62\%\\) retracement levels measured at a subsequent peak or trough [14].
  * **Gann Fan Lines**: Trendlines drawn from prominent high or low pivot points at fixed geometric angles (most importantly the \\(45^\circ\\) line) to track price and time balance [14, 15].
* **Mathematical Formulas**:
  * **Fibonacci Fan Coordinates**:
    * \\(P_{38\%} = P_{\text{start}} + \left[ 0.382 \times (P_{\text{high}} - P_{\text{low}}) \right]\\) [14].
    * \\(P_{62\%} = P_{\text{start}} + \left[ 0.618 \times (P_{\text{high}} - P_{\text{low}}) \right]\\) [14].
  * **Gann Line Geometric Angles**:
    * Angle \\(\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Price Units}}{\text{Time Units}}\right)\\) [14, 15].
    * **\\(1 \times 1\\) Line (\\(45^\circ\\))**: Represents 1 unit of price per 1 unit of time (perfect price-time balance) [14, 15].
    * **Steeper Lines (Uptrend Acceleration)**: \\(2 \times 1\\) (\\(63\frac{3}{4}^\circ\\) or \\(63.75^\circ\\)) and \\(4 \times 1\\) (\\(75^\circ\\)) [14].
    * **Flatter Lines (Slower Advance)**: \\(1 \times 2\\) (\\(26\frac{1}{4}^\circ\\) or \\(26.25^\circ\\)) and \\(1 \times 4\\) (\\(15^\circ\\)) [14]. Up to nine different Gann lines can be constructed [14].
* **Chart Patterns**: Radial rays emanating from a primary pivot point, forming an angular grid across future price action [14].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * The \\(45^\circ\\) Gann line serves as the primary benchmark for a sustainable trend [14, 15].
  * If an uptrend line is steeper than \\(45^\circ\\), the advance is advancing too rapidly and is unsustainable; a line breaking below a steep slope usually signals an adjustment back toward the \\(45^\circ\\) line [15, 16].
  * When one fan line is penetrated, prices typically move toward the next fan line in the sequence [14, 17].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Use fan lines as progressive dynamic support levels during pullbacks in uptrends or resistance during rallies in downtrends [14, 17].
  * Trade breakouts from one fan line toward the objective set by the next fan line [17].
  * *Author's Caveat*: Gann lines can be controversial because it is difficult to determine in advance which specific geometric angle line will hold [14, 17].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **S&P 500 Stock Index (Weekly Bar Chart, 1994–1998)**: Murphy illustrates how an accelerating bull market required redrawing flat trendlines into progressively steeper angles [18]. He notes that when trends accelerate so rapidly that multiple steep lines are required, technicians should switch to moving averages (curvilinear trendlines) [16, 18].

### 4. Key Reversal Days (Selling Climaxes)

* **Core Concepts**: Short-term bar chart patterns occurring at trend extremes that indicate an immediate loss of momentum and a potential trend reversal [19, 20]. When accompanied by an outside price range and extreme volume, their reliability increases significantly [20, 21].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Top Reversal Day**: A day in an uptrend where prices set a new high (usually near the open) but weaken to close below the previous day's closing price [20].
  * **Bottom Reversal Day**: A day in a downtrend where prices drop to a new low but rally to close above the previous day's close [20, 21].
  * **Key Reversal Day**: A top or bottom reversal day featuring an exceptionally wide price range and heavy volume [20-22].
  * **Outside Day**: A day where both the high and low exceed the entire price range of the preceding day [21].
  * **Selling Climax**: A dramatic bottom reversal where panicked long traders are forced out of the market on heavy volume, creating a supply vacuum that prices rapidly rally to fill [21, 23].
* **Mathematical Formulas / Conditions**:
  * **Top Reversal Day**: \\(\text{High}_t > \text{High}_{t-1}\\) AND \\(\text{Close}_t < \text{Close}_{t-1}\\) [20].
  * **Bottom Reversal Day**: \\(\text{Low}_t < \text{Low}_{t-1}\\) AND \\(\text{Close}_t > \text{Close}_{t-1}\\) [20].
  * **Outside Day Condition**: \\(\text{High}_t > \text{High}_{t-1}\\) AND \\(\text{Low}_t < \text{Low}_{t-1}\\) [21].
  * **Key Reversal Intensity**: Reversal Condition + \\((\text{High}_t - \text{Low}_t) \gg \text{Average Range}\\) + \\(\text{Volume}_t \gg \text{Average Volume}\\) [20, 21].
* **Chart Patterns**: Displayed as an elongated price bar at the peak or trough of a trend with heavy volume spikes at the bottom of the chart [21, 24].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Volume & Range**: The wider the daily trading range and the heavier the trading volume, the higher the significance of the reversal [20, 21].
  * **Timeframe Scaling**: Weekly and monthly reversals carry far greater technical significance than daily reversal days [25, 26]. A weekly upside reversal occurs when price trades lower during the week (making a new low) but closes on Friday above the previous Friday's close [25, 27].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * Treat a key reversal day as an early warning signal to tighten stops or take profits [20, 21].
  * During a selling climax, avoid selling into the panic; look to buy as the price recovers into the volume vacuum [21].
  * Place protective stops just past the extreme spike high of a top reversal or the extreme spike low of a selling climax [20, 21].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Dow Jones Industrial Average (October 28, 1997)**: Murphy highlights this date as a classic selling climax / upside key reversal [24]. Prices opened sharply lower, plunged to an extreme intraday low, and reversed sharply to close higher on record-high volume bars [24].

### 5. Price Gaps & Island Reversals

* **Core Concepts**: Gaps are blank spaces on a bar chart where no trading takes place [28, 29]. An **up gap** forms when the daily low is higher than the previous day's high; a **down gap** forms when the daily high is lower than the previous day's low [29]. Gaps fall into three distinct functional categories plus the composite Island Reversal structure [28, 29].
* **Definitions**:
  * **Breakaway Gap**: A gap that occurs upon the completion of a major price pattern (or breakout from a basing/topping structure), signaling the start of a major move [28, 30, 31].
  * **Runaway (Measuring) Gap**: A gap that appears near the midpoint of an established trend on moderate volume, reflecting rapid, effortless momentum [28, 32, 33].
  * **Exhaustion Gap**: A gap that appears near the end of a trend, representing a final speculative leap before prices reverse [28, 34, 35].
  * **Island Reversal**: A pattern where an upside exhaustion gap is followed within a few days or weeks by a downside breakaway gap (or vice versa at market bottoms), leaving a cluster of price bars isolated like an island [22, 28, 36].
* **Mathematical Formulas & Measuring Rules**:
  * **Up Gap**: \\(\text{Low}_t > \text{High}_{t-1}\\); \\(\text{Gap Size} = \text{Low}_t - \text{High}_{t-1}\\) [29].
  * **Down Gap**: \\(\text{High}_t < \text{Low}_{t-1}\\); \\(\text{Gap Size} = \text{Low}_{t-1} - \text{High}_t\\) [29].
  * **Runaway Gap Price Objective (\\(P_{\text{target}}\\))**:
    \\[D = P_{\text{gap\_start}} - P_{\text{breakout}}\\]
    \\[P_{\text{target}} = P_{\text{gap\_start}} + D = P_{\text{breakout}} + (2 \times D)\\]
    *Where*:
    * \\(P_{\text{breakout}}\\) = Price at the original trend breakout point [26, 37].
    * \\(P_{\text{gap\_start}}\\) = Price at the occurrence of the runaway gap [37].
    * \\(D\\) = Vertical distance traveled prior to the gap [37].
* **Chart Patterns**: Visual gaps in bar continuity. Breakaway gaps occur at base boundaries; Runaway gaps occur mid-trend; Exhaustion gaps occur at trend extremes; Island reversals appear as isolated price islands bounded by gaps on both sides [28, 36, 38].
* **Confirmation Rules**:
  * **Volume Behavior**:
    * **Breakaway Gap**: Accompanied by **heavy volume**; the heavier the volume after the gap, the less likely it is to be filled [30].
    * **Runaway Gap**: Moves on **moderate volume** [32].
    * **Exhaustion Gap**: Occurs on heavy volume or sudden panic, but buying/selling rapidly dries up [34].
  * **Gap Invalidation Criterion**:
    * Breakaway and runaway gaps are typically **not filled** [28, 30, 32].
    * Up gaps function as support levels on corrections [30, 32, 39].
    * **Decisive Sign**: A close **below an upward gap** in an uptrend invalidates the move and signals serious market weakness or trend failure [30, 32, 36].
* **Trading Rules**:
  * **Gap Support/Resistance Entry**: In an uptrend, buy pullbacks to the upper edge or inside of a breakaway or runaway gap, placing protective stops right below the bottom of the gap [30, 39]. In a downtrend, sell rallies into down gaps [39].
  * **Measuring Target Projection**: Use runaway gaps to calculate target projections by doubling the vertical distance from the initial breakout to the gap [37].
  * **Island Reversal Execution**: When an island reversal forms, immediately liquidate existing positions and trade in the direction of the secondary breakaway gap [36, 38].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * **Applied Materials (AMAT Daily Chart, 1997–1998)**: Features an exhaustion gap near the rally top (closing below it confirmed the top), followed by a measuring gap halfway down the subsequent bear trend, and an exhaustion gap at the bottom [37].
  * **Placer Dome Inc. (PDG Daily Chart, Aug 1997 – Dec 1997)**: Displays a classic Island Reversal Top formed by an up gap in August followed three weeks later by a down gap, marking a major market top [36, 38].

- "Weekly and Monthly Reversals This type of reversal pattern shows up on weekly and monthly bar charts, and with much greater significance. On a weekly chart, each bar represents the entire week's range with the close registered on Friday. An upside weekly reversal, therefore, would occur when the market trades lower during the week, makes a new low for the move, but on Friday closes above the previous Friday's close. Weekly reversals are much more significant than daily reversals for obvious reasons and are watched closely by chartists as signaling important turning points. By the same token, monthly reversals are even more important."

- "Using Price Gaps Price gaps on bar charts can be used effectively in the timing of purchases or sales. After an upmove, for example, underlying gaps usually function as support levels. Buy a dip to the upper end of the gap or a dip into the gap itself. A protective stop can be placed below the gap. In a bear move, sell a rally to the lower end of the gap or into the gap itself. A protective stop can be kept over the gap. Combining Technical Concepts The most effective way to use these technical concepts is to combine them. Remember that when we're discussing timing, the basic decision to buy or sell has already been made. All we're doing here is fine tuning the entry or exit point. If a buy signal has been given, the trader wants to get the best price possible. Suppose prices dip into the 40-60% buying zone, show a prominent support level in that zone, and/or have a potential support gap. Suppose further that a significant up trendline is nearby."
