### 1. Chart Types: Concepts, Formulas, Patterns & Rules

#### A. Bar Charts
* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: The **daily bar chart** is the primary working tool of technical analysis [1, 2]. It is both a price and a time chart: the vertical axis (\\(y\\)-axis) displays price levels, while the horizontal axis (\\(x\\)-axis) tracks the passage of time (typically structured on a 5-day trading week) [3, 4]. A single vertical bar represents the entire price range between the period's high and low [1, 3]. 
* **Construction Mechanics**: 
  * **High & Low**: Top and bottom of the vertical bar [1, 3].
  * **Open Price**: Marked by a small horizontal tic extending to the **left** of the vertical bar [1, 3].
  * **Close Price**: Marked by a small horizontal tic extending to the **right** of the vertical bar [1, 3].
* **Trading & Interpretation Rules**: The closing price is considered the most critical price of the trading day [5]. Price gaps (spaces where no trading occurs) act as markers of urgency: upside gaps indicate market strength, while downside gaps indicate weakness [6].

---

#### B. Line Charts
* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: A **line chart** (or close-only chart) connects successive closing prices over time with a continuous line [5, 7, 8].
* **Rationale**: Many technicians consider intraday highs and lows to be temporary noise and regard the closing price as the most valid measure of true market value and price activity [5].

---

#### C. Point & Figure (P&F) Charts
* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: A Point & Figure chart measures **pure price movement** while ignoring time and volume as separate entities on the \\(x\\)-axis [9, 10]. If prices do not move by the required threshold, the chart remains untouched regardless of how much time passes [9].
* **Visual Elements**:
  * **Column of X’s**: Represents rising prices [7, 11].
  * **Column of O’s**: Represents falling prices [7, 11].
* **Construction Variations**:
  * **Box Size**: The minimum price increment assigned to each box (e.g., \$1.00, 5 points, or a percentage) [12-14].
  * **Reversal Thresholds**: 
    * *1-Box Reversal*: Used for intraday / very short-term activity [12, 15].
    * *3-Box Reversal*: Standard for intermediate-term trends (e.g., Chartcraft method) [15-17].
    * *5-Box Reversal*: Highly compressed format used for long-term trend analysis [15].
* **Mathematical Formulas & Measuring Techniques**:
  1. **Horizontal Count Target (1-Box Intraday P&F)** [18, 19]:
     \\[\text{Price Target} = \text{Measuring Line Base Price} \pm (\text{Number of Congestion Columns})\\]
  2. **Horizontal Count Target (3-Box Reversal P&F)** [13, 20]:
     \\[\text{Reversal Value} = \text{Box Size} \times \text{Reversal Criteria (e.g., 3)}\\]
     \\[\text{Target Objective} = \text{Pattern Base/Top Price} \pm (\text{Number of Columns across Congestion Area} \times \text{Reversal Value})\\]
     *Example*: A 10-column base on a \$1.00 box, 3-box reversal chart yields a \$3.00 reversal value (\\(1 \times 3\\)) and a target projection of \$30.00 (\\(10 \times 3\\)) above the base [13, 20].
  3. **P&F Moving Average Calculation** [21]:
     \\[\text{Column Average Price} = \frac{\sum \text{Prices of X's or O's in Column}}{\text{Number of X's or O's in Column}}\\]
     The resulting column averages are then smoothed over 10 or 20 columns [21].
* **P&F Chart Patterns & Signal Rules**:
  * **Buy Signal**: Triggered when a column of X’s rises **one box above** the peak of the prior X column [11, 22, 23].
  * **Sell Signal**: Triggered when a column of O’s falls **one box below** the low of the prior O column [11, 22, 23].
  * **Fulcrum**: A congestion pattern formed after a major move (resembling a double/triple top or bottom); a breakout from a fulcrum base is called a *catapult* [24].
* **Trendline Rules**:
  * **3-Box Reversal Trendlines**: Drawn strictly at **\\(45^\circ\\) angles** rather than connecting highs or lows [25].
  * **Bullish Support Line**: Drawn at a \\(45^\circ\\) angle slanting upward to the right starting **under the lowest O box** of a major bottom [25].
  * **Bearish Resistance Line**: Drawn at a \\(45^\circ\\) angle slanting downward to the right starting **above the highest X box** of a major top [25].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * *Intel Corp & America Online (AOL)*: Demonstrates Kenneth Tower's logarithmic P&F method using percentage box sizes (3.6% box for AOL, 3.2% for Intel), yielding horizontal count price targets of 69.7 and 136.5 for AOL and 33 / 87.6 for Intel [14, 26, 27].
  * *Royal Dutch Petroleum*: Shows 10-column and 20-column moving averages overlaid on a multi-year 2.7% logarithmic P&F chart, keeping the trader long throughout a 4-year uptrend (1993–1997) [28, 29].

#### D. Japanese Candlestick Charts
* **Core Concepts & Definitions**: Developed in Japan, candlestick charts display open, high, low, and close prices in a visually distinct format [30-32].
* **Anatomy & Color Coding**:
  * **Real Body**: The rectangular section between the Open price and Close price [30, 33].
  * **White (Open) Body**: Indicates the Close was **higher** than the Open (bullish/positive) [30, 33].
  * **Black Body**: Indicates the Close was **lower** than the Open (bearish/negative) [30, 33].
  * **Shadows (Wicks/Hairs)**: Thin lines extending above and below the real body representing the session high and low [30, 33].
* **Primary Candlestick Lines**:
  * **Long Days vs. Short Days**: Defined by large vs. small distances between Open and Close [34].
  * **Spinning Tops**: Small real bodies with long upper and lower shadows, indicating market indecision [34, 35].
  * **Doji**: Formed when the Open and Close prices are identical (or nearly equal) [35]. Variants include *Long-legged Doji* (extreme indecision), *Gravestone Doji* (long upper shadow, no lower shadow; bearish), and *Dragonfly Doji* (long lower shadow, no upper shadow; bullish) [36].
* **Key Candle Patterns**:
  * **Dark Cloud Cover (Bearish Reversal)**: Two-day pattern in an uptrend. Day 1 is a long white candle; Day 2 opens above the prior high but closes well below the midpoint of Day 1's white body [37].
  * **Piercing Line (Bullish Reversal)**: Two-day pattern in a downtrend. Day 1 is a long black candle; Day 2 opens lower but closes above the midpoint of Day 1's black body [37].
  * **Morning Star (Bullish Reversal)** / **Evening Star (Bearish Reversal)**: Three-candle turning patterns involving a long body, a small gapping body (star), and a strong counter-directional third candle [38-40].
  * **Rising Three Methods (Bullish Continuation)**: Five-candle pattern featuring a long white body, followed by three small retrogressing bodies contained within the first day's range, completed by a strong white candle closing at a new high [40, 41].
* **Confirmation Rules & Filtered Patterns**: Candlestick patterns should be combined with traditional technical oscillators (such as Stochastics %D, RSI, or CCI) to filter out false signals [42, 43]. For example, a bullish *Piercing Line* pattern is validated when the Stochastic %D indicator is in an oversold condition (below 20) [42].
* **Hybrid Formats (CandlePower / Candlevolume)**: Pioneer Richard Arms and author Greg Morris combined Equivolume with Candlesticks: the height of the candle represents the price range, while the **width of the candle body is determined by volume** [44, 45].

### 2. Scaling: Arithmetic vs. Logarithmic (Semi-Logarithmic)

| Feature | Arithmetic (Linear) Scale | Logarithmic (Semi-Log / Ratio) Scale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Distance between units represents an **equal dollar/point amount** across the entire axis [46, 47]. | Distance between units represents **equal percentage changes** [46, 47]. |
| **Visual Behavior** | A move from \$5 to \$10 covers the same vertical distance as a move from \$50 to \$55 (a \$5 increase) [47]. | A move from \$10 to \$20 (a 100% gain) covers the same vertical distance as a move from \$20 to \$40 or \$40 to \$80 [47]. |
| **Primary Use Case** | Standard for short-term daily charts and most commodity futures charts [47]. | Standard for long-term multi-year stock charts, inflation adjustments, and exponential trendlines [46-48]. |

* **Trendline Confirmation Rules**: Up trendlines on logarithmic charts are broken **sooner** than linear up trendlines during major market tops [49].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * *Intel Corp (3-Year Chart)*: An arithmetic scale distorted a 3-year rally into a near-vertical rise, whereas a semi-log scale produced a smoother, more reliable up trendline [4, 47].
  * *Tokyo Nikkei Dow (1979–1998)*: On a linear chart, the long-term up trendline (Line 1) was not broken until early 1992 at 22,000—two years after the actual peak [50]. On a logarithmic scale, a steeper trendline (Line 2) signaled the trend breakdown in mid-1990 at 30,000 [49].

### 3. Timeframes: Daily, Weekly, and Monthly Bar Charts

* **Time Compression Concepts**: 
  * **Daily Bar Chart**: Covers 6 to 9 months of price action; primary tool for short-term timing [2, 51].
  * **Weekly Bar Chart**: Each bar represents one full week's price action (Monday open to Friday close), covering up to 5 years [52, 53].
  * **Monthly Bar Chart**: Each bar represents one full month's price action, covering up to 20 years [53, 54].
* **Top-Down (Macro-to-Micro) Analysis Protocol**: Technical analysis must begin with long-range charts to identify the major trend before consulting short-term charts [55]:
  1. Examine the **20-year monthly chart** to locate major support/resistance, long-term patterns, and \\(20\\)-year trendlines [55].
  2. Examine the **5-year weekly chart** to analyze intermediate trends and indicators [55].
  3. Examine the **6-to-9 month daily chart** to pinpoint chart setups [55].
  4. Consult **intraday charts** (e.g., 5-min, 15-min, 60-min) for execution timing and entry/exit points [55-57].
* **Weekly & Monthly Price Reversals**:
  * **Upside Weekly Reversal**: Prices trade lower during the week, set a new low for the move, but close on Friday **above the previous Friday's close** [58, 59].
  * **Downside Weekly Reversal**: Prices trade higher during the week, set a new high, but close on Friday **below the previous Friday's close** [58, 59].
  * *Rule*: Reversals on weekly and monthly charts carry far greater significance than daily key reversal days [58, 59].
* **Author Case Studies**:
  * *General Motors (Weekly Chart)*: An early 1998 bottom began exactly at a multi-year support line drawn across the 1995–1996 lows [60].
  * *Burlington Resources (Monthly Chart)*: The 1997 rally stopped precisely at the horizontal resistance level formed by the 1989 and 1993 peaks, while the 1995 bottom matched the 1991 low [60].
  * *Southwest Airlines (Monthly Chart)*: Formed a 3.5-year bullish symmetrical triangle that was clearly visible on the monthly chart but invisible on daily bar charts [61].
  * *Dow Utilities (20-Year Monthly Chart)*: The 1994 market bottom bounced directly off a 20-year rising trendline [62].

### 4. Volume and Futures Open Interest

#### A. Definitions & Basic Principles
* **Volume**: The total amount of trading activity (contracts traded in futures, or shares traded in stocks) during a given time period [63, 64]. It measures the intensity or urgency behind a price move [65].
* **Open Interest**: The total number of outstanding or unliquidated futures/options contracts held by market participants at the end of the day [66-68]. It represents the number of contracts held by either the longs OR the shorts, not the sum of both [66, 69].

---

#### B. Reporting Mechanics & Time Lags
* **Stocks**: Volume figures are reported in real time [70, 71]. Open interest does not apply to stocks [72].
* **Futures**: Official volume and open interest figures are **reported a day late** [67, 70]. Traders must use estimated volume figures for the current day while plotting official volume and open interest with a 1-day lag [67, 70].
* **Total vs. Individual Month Figures**: Total volume and open interest across all delivery months must be used for technical forecasting in futures, because individual contract months show artificial volume expansion near launch and contraction near expiration regardless of market trend [72, 73].

---

#### C. Open Interest Trade Matrix
Every trade completed on an exchange floor alters open interest in one of three ways [74]:

| Buyer Action | Seller Action | Change in Total Open Interest |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Buys new long | Sells new short | **Increases (+1)** [74] |
| Buys new long | Sells old long (liquidating) | **No Change (0)** [75] |
| Buys old short (covering) | Sells new short | **No Change (0)** [75] |
| Buys old short (covering) | Sells old long (liquidating) | **Decreases (-1)** [75] |

---

#### D. General Rules & Interpretation Matrix

| Price Trend | Volume | Open Interest (Futures) | Technical Market Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rising (\\(\uparrow\\))** | **Increasing (\\(\uparrow\\))** | **Increasing (\\(\uparrow\\))** | **Strong / Bullish**: Aggressive new buying entering the market [76, 77]. |
| **Rising (\\(\uparrow\\))** | **Decreasing (\\(\downarrow\\))** | **Decreasing (\\(\downarrow\\))** | **Weak / Bearish**: Rally driven by short covering; upside buying drying up [76, 78, 79]. |
| **Declining (\\(\downarrow\\))** | **Increasing (\\(\uparrow\\))** | **Increasing (\\(\uparrow\\))** | **Weak / Bearish**: Aggressive short selling entering the market [76, 79]. |
| **Declining (\\(\downarrow\\))** | **Decreasing (\\(\downarrow\\))** | **Decreasing (\\(\downarrow\\))** | **Strong / Bullish**: Decline caused by long liquidation; downtrend nearing completion [76, 78, 79]. |

#### E. Mathematical Formulas & Volume Indicators

1. **On Balance Volume (OBV)** [80]:
   \\[\text{OBV}_t = \begin{cases} \text{OBV}_{t-1} + \text{Volume}_t & \text{if } \text{Close}_t > \text{Close}_{t-1} \\ \text{OBV}_{t-1} - \text{Volume}_t & \text{if } \text{Close}_t < \text{Close}_{t-1} \\ \text{OBV}_{t-1} & \text{if } \text{Close}_t = \text{Close}_{t-1} \end{cases}\\]
   *Rule*: The direction of the OBV line (its trend) is what matters [81]. Non-confirmation between price peaks and OBV peaks constitutes a divergence warning of a trend reversal [81, 82].

2. **Demand Index (DI) (James Sibbet)** [83, 84]:
   \\[\text{Buying Pressure (BP)} = \begin{cases} V & \text{if price rises} \\ \frac{V}{P} & \text{if price declines} \end{cases}\\]
   \\[\text{Selling Pressure (SP)} = \begin{cases} \frac{V}{P} & \text{if price rises} \\ V & \text{if price declines} \end{cases}\\]
   where \\(V\\) is volume and \\(P\\) is the percentage price change multiplied by constant \\(K = \frac{3 \times \text{Close}}{\text{Volatility Average}}\\) [84].

3. **Arms Index (TRIN) (Richard Arms)** [85]:
   \\[\text{Arms Index} = \frac{\left( \frac{\text{Number of Advancing Issues}}{\text{Number of Declining Issues}} \right)}{\left( \frac{\text{Advancing Volume}}{\text{Declining Volume}} \right)}\\]
   *Rule*: A reading below 1.0 is bullish (heavier volume in advancing stocks); a reading above 1.0 is bearish [85].

4. **Put/Call Option Volume Ratio** [86]:
   \\[\text{Put/Call Ratio} = \frac{\text{Put Volume}}{\text{Call Volume}}\\]
   *Rule*: Used as a contrary indicator. Extremely high ratios signal an oversold market; extremely low ratios warn of an overbought market [86].

#### F. Special Rules & Case Studies
* **Consolidation Range Buildups**: A substantial increase in open interest during a horizontal trading range intensifies the ultimate breakout, as traders on the wrong side of the breakout are forced into panic liquidation [87, 88].
* **Blowoff Tops & Selling Climaxes**:
  * *Blowoff Top*: Sharp upward price acceleration on extreme volume accompanied by a **drop in open interest**, signaling long liquidation at the top [89, 90].
  * *Case Study (Coffee Futures)*: In late 1993 and early 1997, coffee futures suffered major blowoff tops marked by sharp price spikes on heavy volume, where declining open interest lines signaled major trend collapses [90].
* **Commitments of Traders (COT) Report Rules**:
  * Published twice monthly by the CFTC, breaking open interest down into **Commercials** (large hedgers), **Large Speculators** (funds), and **Small Traders** [90, 91].
  * *Rule*: Commercial hedgers are usually right, while speculators are usually wrong at market extremes [91, 92].
  * *Bullish Signal*: Commercials are heavily net long while Large and Small Speculators are heavily net short [92].
  * *Bearish Signal*: Commercials are heavily net short while Speculators are heavily net long [92].
  * *Case Studies*: S&P 500 futures weekly charts (1995–1996) showed three major buy signals when Commercials turned net long [86, 92]; Copper futures weekly charts (1995–1997) showed three major sell signals when Speculators were heavily long and Commercials were heavily short [92, 93].

MEASURING TECHNIQUES Three point reversal charts allow the use of two different measuring techniques—the horizontal and the vertical. For the horizontal, count the number of columns in a bottom or topping pattern. That number of columns must then be multiplied by the value of the reversal or the number of boxes needed for a reversal. For example, let's assign a $1.00 box value to a chart with a 3 box reversal. We count the number of boxes across a base and come up with 10. Because we're using a 3 box reversal, the value of that reversal is $3.00 (3x$1.00). Multiply the 10 columns across the base by $3 for a total of $30. That number is then added to the bottom of the basing pattern or subtracted from the top of a topping pattern to arrive at the price objective."

Stock Market Indicators 447 EQUIVOLUME CHARTING Although Arms is best known for creating the Arms Index, he has also pioneered other ways of combining price and volume analysis. In doing so, he created an entirely new form of charting called Equivolume. In the traditional bar chart, the day's trading range is shown on the price bar with the volume bar plotted at the bottom of the chart. Since technical analysts combine price and volume analysis, they have to look at both parts of the chart at the same time. On the Equivolume chart, each price bar is shown as a rectangle. The height of the rectangle measures the day's trading range. The width of the rectangle is determined by that day's volume. Heavier volume days produce a wider rectangle. Lighter volume days are reflected in a narrower rectangle. (See Figure 18.9.)"

- "Volume and Open Interest Reported a Day Late in Futures Futures volume and open interest numbers are reported a day late. - "THE ARMS INDEX The Arms Index, named after its creator Richard Arms, is a ratio of a ratio. The numerator is the ratio of the number of advancing issues divided by the number of declining issues. On an intraday basis, a very high Arms Index reading is positive, while a very low reading is negative. PUT/CALL RATIOS Volume figures for the options markets are used essentially the same way as in futures and stocks— that is, they tell us the degree of buying or selling pressure in a given market. Volume figures in options are broken down into call volume (bullish) and put volume (bearish). By monitoring the volume in calls versus puts, we are able to determine the degree of bullishness or bearishness in a market. One of the primary uses of volume data in options trading is the construction of put/call volume ratios. When options traders are bullish, call volume exceeds put volume and the put/call ratio falls. A bearish attitude is reflected in heavier put volume and a higher put/call ratio. The put/call ratio is usually viewed as a contrary indicator. A very high ratio signals an oversold market. A very low ratio is a negative warning of an overbought market."
