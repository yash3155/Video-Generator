Investment vs. Consumption Assets**\n\nIn Chapter 5, derivatives valuation distinguishes between two major categories of underlying assets [1, 2]:\n\n* **Investment Assets**: Assets held solely for investment purposes by a significant number of investors [1, 2]. Examples include **stocks, bonds, gold, and silver** [1, 3]. While gold and silver have industrial uses, they qualify as investment assets because they are held by investors primarily for investment value [1, 3]. Forward and futures prices for investment assets are determined strictly by **no-arbitrage pricing models** linking spot prices, interest rates, and income stream cash flows [1, 4, 5].\n* **Consumption Assets**: Assets held primarily for consumption or industrial processing rather than investment [1, 2, 6, 7]. Examples include commodities such as **crude oil, natural gas, copper, corn, and pork bellies** [1, 2, 6, 7]. Because holders of consumption assets derive a physical benefit from holding the tangible commodity (a **convenience yield**), standard short-selling arbitrage arguments do not apply symmetrically, making the theoretical forward price an **upper bound** rather than an exact equality unless convenience yield and storage costs are factored in [1, 3, 4, 6].\n\n---\n\n### **2. Short Selling Mechanics**\n\n**Short selling** involves selling a security that the trader does not currently own [8-10].\n\n* **Borrowing Shares**: To execute a short sale, a trader's broker borrows shares from another investor (such as client shares held in margin accounts, often referred to as \"box stock\") and sells them in the open market [11, 12]. The short seller must eventually buy back (cover) the shares to return them to the lender [9-11].\n* **Paying Dividends**: If the underlying asset pays a cash dividend while the short position remains open, the short seller is obligated to pay the equivalent dividend amount to the lender of the stock [11, 13, 14].\n* **Margin Requirements & Collateral**: Brokers require short sellers to maintain cash or marginable securities in a margin account as collateral to cover potential losses as prices fluctuate [15-17].\n* **Short Squeeze**: A **short squeeze** occurs when a rapid increase in the asset price forces short sellers to cover their short positions simultaneously (buying shares to cut losses or meet margin calls), creating intense buying pressure that drives the price even higher [11, 17-19].\n\n---\n\n### **3. No-Arbitrage Forward Pricing (\\\\(F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}\\\\))**\n\nFor an investment asset providing no income, the relationship between the current spot price (\\\\(S_0\\\\)) and the forward price (\\\\(F_0\\\\)) for delivery at time \\\\(T\\\\) is governed by continuous compounding [1]:\n\n\\\\[\\mathbf{F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}}\\\\] [1]\n\n* **Notation**:\n  * \\\\(S_0\\\\): Current spot price of the asset [1, 4, 20].\n  * \\\\(F_0\\\\): Forward price today for delivery at time \\\\(T\\\\) [1, 4].\n  * \\\\(r\\\\): Continuously compounded risk-free interest rate per annum [21, 22].\n  * \\\\(T\\\\): Time to maturity of the forward contract expressed in years [22, 23].\n\n#### **Arbitrage Arguments**:\n* **Case 1 (\\\\(F_0 > S_0 e^{rT}\\\\))**: Overpriced forward contract. An arbitrageur borrows \\\\(S_0\\\\) at rate \\\\(r\\\\) for time \\\\(T\\\\), buys the spot asset for \\\\(S_0\\\\), and shorts a forward contract at \\\\(F_0\\\\) [1, 4, 24]. At maturity \\\\(T\\\\), the asset is delivered for \\\\(F_0\\\\) and the loan (\\\\(S_0 e^{rT}\\\\)) is repaid, locking in a risk-free profit of \\\\(\\mathbf{F_0 - S_0 e^{rT} > 0}\\\\) [1, 4, 24].\n* **Case 2 (\\\\(F_0 < S_0 e^{rT}\\\\))**: Underpriced forward contract. An arbitrageur short-sells the asset for \\\\(S_0\\\\), invests the cash proceeds at risk-free rate \\\\(r\\\\), and enters a long forward contract at \\\\(F_0\\\\) [1, 4, 24]. At maturity \\\\(T\\\\), the investment grows to \\\\(S_0 e^{rT}\\\\), the asset is bought at \\\\(F_0\\\\) to cover the short position, netting a risk-free profit of \\\\(\\mathbf{S_0 e^{rT} - F_0 > 0}\\\\) [1, 4, 24].\n\n---\n\n### **4. Known Cash Income (\\\\(F_0 = (S_0 - I) e^{rT}\\\\))**\n\nWhen an investment asset pays a known cash income (such as scheduled coupon payments on a bond) during the life of the forward contract, the forward price is adjusted for the present value of that income [1]:\n\n\\\\[\\mathbf{F_0 = (S_0 - I) e^{rT}}\\\\] [1]\n\n* **Definitions & Formulas**:\n  * \\\\(I\\\\): Present value of all cash income expected during the contract's life, discounted at risk-free rate \\\\(r\\\\) to time \\\\(t=0\\\\) [25-27].\n\n#### **Arbitrage Conditions Table**:\n\n| Market Condition | Arbitrage Action | Guaranteed Risk-Free Profit |\n| :--- | :--- | :--- |\n| **\\\\(F_0 > (S_0 - I) e^{rT}\\\\)** | Borrow \\\\((S_0)\\\\), Buy Spot Asset, Short Forward Contract [1, 4, 28] | \\\\(\\mathbf{F_0 - (S_0 - I) e^{rT}}\\\\) [1, 4] |\n| **\\\\(F_0 < (S_0 - I) e^{rT}\\\\)** | Short Spot Asset, Invest Proceeds \\\\((S_0)\\\\), Long Forward Contract [1, 4, 28] | \\\\(\\mathbf{(S_0 - I) e^{rT} - F_0}\\\\) [1, 4] |\n\n---\n\n### **5. Known Dividend Yield (\\\\(F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}\\\\))**\n\nWhen an investment asset provides a known continuous dividend yield at rate \\\\(q\\\\) per annum, the spot price is adjusted for continuous dividend accumulation [1]:\n\n\\\\[\\mathbf{F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}}\\\\] [1]\n\n* **Variables**:\n  * \\\\(q\\\\): Average continuously compounded yield per annum over the contract's life [23, 29].\n* **Applications**:\n  * **Stock Indices**: Modeled as assets paying a continuous dividend yield \\\\(q\\\\) across all constituent stocks [23, 29, 30].\n  * **Foreign Currencies**: A foreign currency yields a \"dividend\" equal to the foreign risk-free interest rate \\\\(r_f\\\\), yielding the currency forward formula \\\\(\\mathbf{F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - r_f)T}}\\\\) [30, 31].\n\n---\n\n### **6.
