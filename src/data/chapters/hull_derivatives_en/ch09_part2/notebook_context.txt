### 1. Market Makers and Bid-Offer Spreads

* **Role of Market Makers**: On option exchanges (such as the CBOE), market makers are appointed to facilitate trading by quoting continuous two-sided markets—both bid and offer prices—for option contracts in the absence of public orders [1-3]. Market makers trade exclusively for their own accounts and do not handle public customer orders [2].
* **Bid-Offer Spread Definitions**:
  * **Bid Price**: The price at which a market maker is willing to buy an option (and the price a trader receives when selling) [1, 4].
  * **Offer (Ask) Price**: The price at which a market maker is willing to sell an option (and the price a trader pays when buying) [1, 5, 6].
  * **Bid-Offer Spread**: The difference between the offer price and the bid price (\\(\text{Spread} = \text{Offer} - \text{Bid}\\)) [1, 7].
* **Spread Characteristics & Mechanics**:
  * The bid-offer spread, expressed as a percentage of the option's price, is typically significantly higher than the percentage spread on the underlying stock [1].
  * The spread serves as the market maker's compensation for providing market liquidity and taking on inventory risk [2, 8, 9].
  * Floor brokers executing spread trades (combining multiple option legs) often attempt to "split the quote"—executing at a net price between the current bid and offer—to reduce execution costs for traders [10].

### 2. Margin Requirements for Option Writers

Margin deposits act as good-faith capital to ensure that option writers fulfill their contractual obligations if market prices move against their positions [11, 12]. 

#### A. Naked (Uncovered) Call Calculation Rules
For an investor writing a naked call option on stock, the initial margin requirement per contract (100 shares) is calculated as the **maximum** of the following two formulas [12, 13]:

\\[\text{Margin Requirement}_{\text{Call}} = \max \left( C + 0.20 S - \text{OTM}, \; C + 0.10 S \right)\\]

Where:
* \\(C\\) = Total call option premium received.
* \\(S\\) = Total current market value of the underlying stock (\\(100 \times \text{stock price}\\)).
* \\(\text{OTM}\\) = Out-of-the-money amount \\(= \max(0, \text{Strike Price} - \text{Stock Price}) \times 100\\). (If the call is in-the-money, \\(\text{OTM} = 0\\)) [12, 13].

#### B. Naked Call Numerical Examples (Based on \\(XYZ\\) July 50 Call) [12, 13]

| Stock Price (\\(S/100\\)) | Call Price (\\(C/100\\)) | 20% of Stock Price | Out-of-the-Money Differential (\\(\text{OTM}\\)) | Total Margin Requirement |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **\$55** (In-the-Money) | \$7.00 (\$700) | \$1,100 | \$0 | **\$1,800** (\\(1,100 + 700 - 0\\)) |
| **\$50** (At-the-Money) | \$4.00 (\$400) | \$1,000 | \$0 | **\$1,400** (\\(1,000 + 400 - 0\\)) |
| **\$46** (Out-of-the-Money) | \$2.00 (\$200) | \$920 | \$400 (\\(50 - 46 \times 100\\)) | **\$720** (\\(920 + 200 - 400\\)) |
| **\$40** (Deep OTM) | \$1.00 (\$100) | \$800 | \$1,000 (\\(50 - 40 \times 100\\)) | **\$400** (10% Stock min: \\(400 + 0\\) / \\(400 \text{ floor}\\)) |

*Note*: The option premium received (\\(C\\)) can be applied directly toward fulfilling the total collateral requirement [12].

#### C. Naked (Uncovered) Put Calculation Rules
For an investor writing a naked put option on stock, the initial margin requirement per contract is the **maximum** of the following two formulas [14-16]:

\\[\text{Margin Requirement}_{\text{Put}} = \max \left( P + 0.20 S - \text{OTM}, \; P + 0.10 K \right)\\]

Where:
* \\(P\\) = Total put option premium received.
* \\(S\\) = Current market value of underlying stock (\\(100 \times \text{stock price}\\)).
* \\(K\\) = Total strike price value (\\(100 \times \text{strike price}\\)).
* \\(\text{OTM}\\) = Out-of-the-money amount \\(= \max(0, \text{Stock Price} - \text{Strike Price}) \times 100\\). (If the put is in-the-money, \\(\text{OTM} = 0\\)) [15, 16].

#### D. Naked Put Numerical Example [14-16]
* **Scenario**: Stock price \\(S = \$50\\). Write a 6-month put with strike \\(K = \$50\\) for a premium of 4 points (\\(P = \$400\\)).
  * \\(20\% \text{ of Stock Price} = 0.20 \times \$5,000 = \$1,000\\).
  * Plus Put Premium \\(P = \$400\\).
  * Out-of-the-money differential \\(\text{OTM} = \$0\\).
  * **Total Margin Requirement** \\(= \$1,000 + \$400 - \$0 = \mathbf{\$1,400}\\).
  * Minimum check (\\(P + 10\% K = \$400 + \$500 = \$900\\)). Since \\(\$1,400 > \$900\\), the margin requirement is **\$1,400**.

#### E. Daily Mark-to-Market Rule
Naked option positions are marked to market daily [17, 18]. The required collateral is recomputed each day using closing prices; if the underlying stock moves adversely, the option writer receives a margin call for additional collateral [17, 18]. If the market moves favorably, excess collateral is released back to the margin account [17, 18].

### 3. The Options Clearing Corporation (OCC)

#### A. Role of the OCC
The OCC acts as the central issuer, clearinghouse, and guarantor for all exchange-listed option contracts [19, 20]. By stepping in as the buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, the OCC eliminates counterparty credit risk between individual traders and standardizes options to allow an active secondary trading market [11, 20, 21].

#### B. Exercise Procedures & Assignment Mechanics
1. **Exercise Notice**: An option holder wishing to exercise instructs their broker, who submits an exercise notice to the OCC [22].
2. **Firm Assignment**: The OCC randomly selects a clearing member brokerage firm that maintains open short positions in that option series and assigns the exercise notice to that firm [22].
3. **Customer Assignment**: The assigned brokerage firm selects an individual customer who wrote the option using one of three approved methods [22, 23]:
   * At random [22, 23],
   * On a First-In, First-Out (FIFO) basis [22, 23], or
   * On another fair and equitable basis approved by the exchange [22, 23].
4. **Automatic Exercise**: To prevent investors from losing money due to oversight, the OCC automatically exercises any expiring option that is in-the-money by a specified margin (e.g., \\(3/4\\)-point or \\(0.01\\)) at expiration, unless the long option holder explicitly instructs their broker not to exercise [24-26].

#### C. Position Limits and Exercise Limits
* **Position Limit**: The maximum number of option contracts that an investor (or group of related accounts acting in concert) can hold on the **same side of the market** for a single underlying stock [27, 28].
  * *Bullish side*: Long Calls + Short Puts [27, 28].
  * *Bearish side*: Short Calls + Long Puts [27, 28].
  * Standard position limit tiers depend on trading volume and market capitalization (e.g., 250,000; 75,000; 60,000; 31,500; 22,500; or 13,500 contracts) [27, 28].
* **Exercise Limit**: Limits the maximum number of option contracts in a single class that can be exercised by an investor or group within a fixed timeframe (usually 5 consecutive business days) [29]. This prevents attempts to corner the market in the underlying stock [29].

### 4. Warrants, Employee Stock Options (ESOs), and Convertible Bonds

#### A. Definitions
* **Warrants**: Call options issued directly by a corporation (or financial institution) giving the holder the right to purchase the firm's common stock at a fixed price [30, 31]. Warrants must be paid for in full, typically have long maturities (several years), and carry no borrowing value for margin [30, 31].
* **Employee Stock Options (ESOs)**: Call options granted by a company to its employees and executives as compensation [32]. ESOs are non-transferable, cannot be traded on open exchanges, and usually carry vesting schedules to align employee incentives with shareholder value [32].
* **Convertible Bonds (and Convertible Preferred Stock)**: Corporate debt or preferred shares that can be exchanged for a predetermined number of common shares at the holder's option [30, 33-35]. They behave like a combination of a fixed-income bond (providing a downside yield floor) and an embedded call option on the common stock [33, 34, 36].

#### B. Dilution Effect
* **Exchange-Traded Options**: Exercising exchange-traded options results solely in the transfer of existing shares between traders via the secondary market [37, 38]. **No new shares are issued**, so there is **no dilution** of corporate equity or earnings per share (EPS) [38].
* **Warrants, ESOs, and Convertibles**: When warrants, ESOs, or convertibles are exercised or converted, the issuing corporation **creates and issues brand-new shares of common stock** [30, 31, 34]. This increases the total outstanding share count, causing a **dilution effect** that reduces the equity percentage and EPS of existing shareholders.

#### C. Comparison Summary

| Feature | Exchange-Traded Options | Warrants / ESOs / Convertibles |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Issuer** | Options Clearing Corporation (OCC) [19, 20] | The issuing corporation itself [30, 31] |
| **Share Creation & Dilution** | Transfers existing shares; **No dilution** [38] | Issues new shares; **Causes dilution** [30, 31] |
| **Maturity / Expiration** | Usually short-term (up to 9 months / LEAPS 2-3 yrs) [39, 40] | Typically long-term (e.g., 5 to 10+ years) [30, 31] |
| **Transferability** | Standardized; freely traded on exchanges [39-41] | ESOs non-transferable; Warrants/Convertibles trade separately [30, 31] |
