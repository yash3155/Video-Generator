  "answer": "### Fixed-for-Fixed Currency Swaps Mechanics\n\nA **fixed-for-fixed currency swap** involves exchanging principal and fixed-rate interest payments in one currency for principal and fixed-rate interest payments in another currency between two counterparties over a specified term [1].\n\n#### Principal Exchange Mechanics\n1. **At Inception**: Principal amounts in two different currencies are exchanged at the prevailing spot exchange rate \\\\(S_0\\\\) [1]. For example, if the spot exchange rate is \\\\(1.50 \\text{ USD} = 1.00 \\text{ GBP}\\\\), a principal of \\\\(\\$15 \\text{ million}\\\\) USD is exchanged for \\\\(£10 \\text{ million}\\\\) GBP [1].\n2. **Periodic Payments**: Over the life of the swap, each party pays fixed interest on the principal amount received in that currency. Because cash flows occur in different currencies, **no net payment calculation is performed**; both interest payments are made in full on each payment date [1].\n3. **At Maturity**: The principal amounts are re-exchanged at the **same spot rate \\\\(S_0\\\\)** that was used at inception, regardless of where the market spot exchange rate has moved during the contract life [1].\n\n---\n\n### Comparative Advantage in Foreign Borrowing\n\nThe **comparative-advantage argument** explains why two corporations might enter into a currency swap when each possesses a relative advantage in its home debt market, yet desires funding in a foreign currency [1].\n\n#### Illustrative Market Rate Table\nConsider two companies, **Company A** (higher credit quality) and **Company B** (lower credit quality), seeking 5-year fixed-rate debt in **US Dollars (USD)** and **Australian Dollars (AUD)**:\n\n| Company | USD Borrowing Rate | AUD Borrowing Rate |\n| :--- | :--- | :--- |\n| **Company A** | \\\\(5.0\\%\\\\) | \\\\(6.0\\%\\\\) |\n| **Company B** | \\\\(7.0\\%\\\\) | \\\\(7.2\\%\\\\) |\n| **Difference** | \\\\(2.0\\%\\\\) | \\\\(1.2\\%\\\\) |\n\n#### Calculation of Total Potential Gain\n* Company A pays \\\\(2.0\\%\\\\) less than Company B in the USD market (\\\\(7.0\\% - 5.0\\% = 2.0\\%\\\\)) [1].\n* Company A pays \\\\(1.2\\%\\\\) less than Company B in the AUD market (\\\\(7.2\\% - 6.0\\% = 1.2\\%\\\\)) [1].\n* Company A has an **absolute advantage** in both markets, but it has a **comparative advantage** in the USD market, where its rate advantage is larger (\\\\(2.0\\% > 1.2\\%\\\\)) [1].\n* Company B has a comparative advantage in the AUD market [1].\n\n\\\\[\\text{Total Potential Gain} = (7.0\\% - Valuation as Bond Portfolios\nIf a financial institution receives fixed payments in Currency \\\\(B\\\\) (domestic) and pays fixed payments in Currency \\\\(A\\\\) (foreign), the value of the swap \\\\(V_{\\text{swap}}\\\\) in domestic currency terms is:\n\n\\\\[V_{\\text{swap}} = B_B - S_0 \\cdot B_A\\\\]\n\nWhere:\n* \\\\(B_B\\\\): Value of the domestic-currency bond defined by the remaining domestic cash flows [2].\n* \\\\(B_A\\\\): Value of the foreign-currency bond defined by the remaining foreign cash flows [2].\n* \\\\(S_0\\\\): Current spot exchange rate expressed as domestic currency per unit of foreign currency [2].\n\n#### 2. Valuation as Forward FX Contracts\nAlternatively, each re-exchange of interest and final principal is treated as an individual forward foreign exchange contract [2]. The value of an individual forward contract paying \\\\(K\\\\) units of domestic currency and receiving \\\\(1\\\\) unit of foreign currency at time \\\\(t_i\\\\) is:\n\n\\\\[V_i = (S_0 e^{-r_A t_i} - K_i e^{-r_B t_i})\\\\]\n\nSumming the discounted net present value of all remaining cash flow exchanges yields the total swap value \\\\(V_{\\text{swap}}\\\\) [2].\n\n---\n\n### Credit Risk Asymmetry: Currency Swaps vs. Interest Rate Swaps\n\nCurrency swaps involve significantly higher credit risk exposure than vanilla Single-Currency Interest Rate Swaps (IRS) [2].\n\n1. **Principal Exchange at Maturity**: In a currency swap, full principal amounts in two different currencies are re-exchanged at maturity [1, 2]. In an IRS, principal amounts are identical, denominated in the same currency, and never actually exchanged [1].\n2. **Exposure Divergence**: Because exchange rates can move substantially over multi-year horizons, the un-hedged replacement cost of the principal re-exchange near maturity can grow to represent a vast proportion of the swap's total value [2]. If a counterparty defaults when exchange rates have moved adversely, the loss suffered is on the order of the **entire principal re-exchange amount**, whereas in an IRS, default exposure is limited strictly to the net interest rate differential [2].\n\n---\n\n### Credit Default Swaps (CDS)\n\nA **Credit Default Swap (CDS)** is a credit derivative contract that provides protection against default or credit events of a reference entity [2, 3].\n\n* **Buyer of Protection**: Pays a periodic fee (the CDS spread or coupon) to the seller [2, 3].\n* **Seller of Protection**: Agrees to pay a default payoff if a defined credit event occurs [2, 3].\n\n#### Default Payoff Formula\nIn the event of default, the seller pays the buyer the difference between the face value of the reference bond and its post-default market recovery value [2, 3]:\n\n\\\\[\\text{Default Payoff} = (1 - R) \\times L\\\\]\n\nWhere:\n* \\\\(R\\\\): The recovery rate of the reference bond (expressed as a fraction, e.g., \\\\(0.40\\\\) or \\\\(40\\%\\\\)) [2, 3].\n* \\\\(L\\\\): The principal or notional amount of the swap [2, 3].\n\n---\n\n### Case Study: The Hammersmith and Fulham Swap Scandal\n\n#### Business Snapshot 7.2\nIn the late 1980s, the **London Borough of Hammersmith and Fulham** (a local municipal authority in the United Kingdom) aggressively entered into over \\\\(£6\\text{ billion}\\\\) notional of speculative interest rate swap contracts to generate upfront cash premiums to cover local budget deficits [4].\n\n#### Judicial Ruling (*Ultra Vires*)\nWhen sterling interest rates rose sharply, the municipality faced massive mark-to-market losses.
