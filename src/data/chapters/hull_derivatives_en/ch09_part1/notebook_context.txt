### 1. Call and Put Options Basics and Definitions

* **Option Contract**: An option is a financial derivative giving its holder the **right, but not the obligation**, to buy or sell an underlying asset by a specified date at a predetermined price [1, 2].
* **Call Option**: Gives the buyer (holder) the **right to buy** the underlying asset by a certain date for a specified exercise or strike price (\\(K\\)) [1].
* **Put Option**: Gives the buyer (holder) the **right to sell** the underlying asset by a certain date for a specified exercise or strike price (\\(K\\)) [1].
* **Strike Price / Exercise Price (\\(K\\))**: The price stipulated in the option contract at which the underlying asset can be bought or sold [1].
* **Expiration Date / Maturity Date (\\(T\\))**: The final date on which the option contract can be exercised [1, 2].
* **Option Premium (\\(c\\) or \\(p\\))**: The upfront price paid by the buyer to the writer (seller) of the option to acquire the option contract [2, 3].
* **European vs. American Options**:
  * **European Option**: Can be exercised **only on the expiration date** (\\(T\\)) itself [2].
  * **American Option**: Can be exercised at **any time during its life** up to and including the expiration date [2].

### 2. The Four Basic Option Payoff Profiles

Let \\(S_T\\) denote the market price of the underlying stock at expiration time \\(T\\), \\(K\\) denote the strike price, \\(c\\) denote the call option premium, and \\(p\\) denote the put option premium [2].

1. **Long Call (Buying a Call)**:
   * **Gross Payoff Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Payoff} = \max(S_T - K, 0)\\] [2]
   * **Net Profit Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Profit} = \max(S_T - K, 0) - c\\] [2]
   * **Characteristics**: Maximum loss is capped at the premium paid (\\(c\\)); potential upside gain is theoretically uncapped as \\(S_T \to \infty\\) [4, 5].

2. **Short Call (Writing/Selling a Call)**:
   * **Gross Payoff Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Payoff} = -\max(S_T - K, 0)\\] [2]
   * **Net Profit Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Profit} = c - \max(S_T - K, 0)\\] [2]
   * **Characteristics**: Maximum gain is capped at the premium received (\\(c\\)); potential downside loss is theoretically uncapped as \\(S_T\\) increases [6, 7].

3. **Long Put (Buying a Put)**:
   * **Gross Payoff Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Payoff} = \max(K - S_T, 0)\\] [2]
   * **Net Profit Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Profit} = \max(K - S_T, 0) - p\\] [2]
   * **Characteristics**: Maximum loss is capped at the premium paid (\\(p\\)); maximum gain occurs if \\(S_T = 0\\), yielding \\(K - p\\) [4, 7].

4. **Short Put (Writing/Selling a Put)**:
   * **Gross Payoff Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Payoff} = -\max(K - S_T, 0)\\] [2]
   * **Net Profit Equation**: 
     \\[\text{Profit} = p - \max(K - S_T, 0)\\] [2]
   * **Characteristics**: Maximum gain is capped at the premium received (\\(p\\)); maximum loss occurs if \\(S_T = 0\\), yielding \\(p - K\\) [6, 7].

### 3. Gross Payoff vs. Net Profit

* **Gross Payoff**: Measures the immediate cash distribution resulting from exercising the option at expiration without accounting for the initial purchase price [2, 8].
* **Net Profit**: Accounts for the initial cash flow (the option premium \\(c\\) or \\(p\\)) paid or received at inception [2, 3].

#### Numerical Case Studies:
* **Long Call Numerical Example**:
  * An investor buys 1 contract (100 shares) of a December call option on Google with strike price \\(K = \$550\\) at premium \\(c = \$29.00\\) per share [9, 10].
  * **Upfront Outlay**: \\(100 \times \$29.00 = \$2,900\\) [11].
  * **Scenario A (\\(S_T = \$600\\))**:
    * **Gross Payoff**: \\(100 \times \max(600 - 550, 0) = \$5,000\\) [2].
    * **Net Profit**: \\(\$5,000 - \$2,900 = +\$2,100\\) [12].
  * **Scenario B (\\(S_T \le \$550\\))**:
    * **Gross Payoff**: \\(\$0\\) [2].
    * **Net Profit**: \\(-\$2,900\\) (maximum loss equal to premium paid) [12].

* **Short Put Numerical Example**:
  * A trader sells 1 contract (100 shares) of a September put option on Google with strike price \\(K = \$525\\) at bid price \\(p = \$22.40\\) per share [11, 13].
  * **Upfront Inflow**: \\(100 \times \$22.40 = \$2,240\\) [11, 13].
  * **Scenario A (\\(S_T \ge \$525\\))**: Option expires unexercised. Net profit = \\(+\$2,240\\) [13].
  * **Scenario B (\\(S_T = \$480\\))**: The option is exercised against the trader [13].
    * Trader must buy 100 shares at \\(\$525\\) when market value is \\(\$480\\).
    * **Gross Loss**: \\(100 \times (\$525 - \$480) = \$4,500\\) [13].
    * **Net Loss**: \\(\$4,500 - \$2,240 = -\$2,260\\) [13, 14].

### 4. Moneyness Definitions

Moneyness characterizes the relationship between the current stock price (\\(S\\)) and the option's strike price (\\(K\\)) [15, 16]:

| Moneyness State | Call Option (\\(K\\) vs. \\(S\\)) | Put Option (\\(K\\) vs. \\(S\\)) | Exercise Value / Payoff |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **In-the-Money (ITM)** | \\(S > K\\) [15-17] | \\(S < K\\) [15, 17, 18] | Positive intrinsic value (\\(> 0\\)) [15, 16, 18] |
| **At-the-Money (ATM)** | \\(S = K\\) [15, 19] | \\(S = K\\) [15, 19] | Zero intrinsic value [15, 19, 20] |
| **Out-of-the-Money (OTM)** | \\(S < K\\) [15, 16, 21] | \\(S > K\\) [15, 18, 21] | Zero intrinsic value (\\(= 0\\)) [15, 20, 22] |

### 5. Contract Specifications

* **Contract Size**: In North America, one standard exchange-traded stock option contract covers **100 shares** of stock [11, 23, 24].
* **Strike Price Intervals**:
  * Generally spaced **\\(\$2.50\\)** apart for low-priced stocks (e.g., under \\(\$25\\) or \\(\$35\\)), **\\(\$5.00\\)** apart for stocks priced between \\(\$25\\) and \\(\$200\\), and **\\(\$10.00\\)** apart for higher-priced stocks [25, 26].
* **Expiration Months**: Options trade on fixed four-month cycles (Jan/Apr/Jul/Oct, Feb/May/Aug/Nov, or Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec) plus the two nearest calendar months [25, 27].
* **Expiration Date**: Standard options expire on the **third Friday** of the expiration month (historically settled on the Saturday following) [28-30].
* **Position Limits**: The maximum number of option contracts an investor (or group of acting-together investors) can hold on the **same side of the market** [31-33].
  * Bullish side: Long Calls + Short Puts [32, 33].
  * Bearish side: Short Calls + Long Puts [32, 33].
  * Tiered limits (e.g., 250,000, 75,000, 25,000 contracts) depend on stock volume and float [32, 33].
* **Exercise Limits**: The maximum number of option contracts that an investor/group can exercise within **5 consecutive business days**, set equal to the position limit to prevent market corners or squeezes [31].

### 6. Stock Splits and Dividend Adjustments

Exchange-traded options are **not adjusted for ordinary cash dividends** [34, 35]. However, terms are explicitly adjusted for **stock splits** and **stock dividends** [34, 35]:

#### \\(N\\)-for-\\(M\\) Stock Split Adjustments:
1. **Strike Price Adjustment**:
   \\[\text{New Strike } K^* = K \times \left(\frac{M}{N}\right)\\] [23, 36]
2. **Contract & Shares Adjustment**:
   * **Round Lot Splits (e.g., 2-for-1, 3-for-1)**:
     * Number of contracts increases by factor \\(N/M\\) [23, 37].
     * Each contract **continues to cover 100 shares** [23, 37].
     * *Example*: Holding 5 January \$60 call contracts prior to a 2-for-1 split becomes holding **10 January \$30 call contracts**, each covering 100 shares [23].
   * **Non-Round Lot Splits (e.g., 3-for-2)**:
     * Number of contracts held **remains unchanged** [36].
     * Number of shares per contract increases to **\\(100 \times \left(\frac{N}{M}\right) = 150\\) shares** [36].
     * *Example*: A 3-for-2 split on a stock at \\(\$60\\) with strike \\(K = \$60\\) adjusts the strike to \\(K^* = \$60 \times (2/3) = \$40\\), covering **150 shares per contract** [36].

#### Stock Dividend Adjustments:
* A stock dividend of \\(x\%\\) is treated like an \\((1 + x/100)\\)-for-1 split [38].
* Shares per contract become \\(100 \times (1 + x/100)\\), and new strike \\(K^* = K / (1 + x/100)\\) [38].

### 7. Business Snapshot 9.1: The Gucci Group Takeover and Options Adjustments

* **Context**: In 2000–2001, Gucci Group NV was involved in a high-profile takeover battle between LVMH and PPR (Pinault-Printemps-Redoute) [39]. As part of the settlement, Gucci declared a **massive special cash dividend** of $\$13.50 per share [39].
* **Rule**: Standard stock options are never adjusted for regular cash dividends [34, 35]. However, when a company pays an **extraordinarily large cash dividend (special dividend)**, option exchanges (such as the Options Clearing Corporation / OCC) make adjustments to prevent distortion of option contracts.
* **Adjustment Execution**:
  * To ensure call option holders are not penalized and put option holders are not unfairly rewarded by the \\(\$13.50\\) stock price drop on the ex-dividend date, the OCC reduced option strike prices by exact dollar amounts corresponding to the special dividend (or adjusted deliverables) so option values were preserved fairly across participants.
