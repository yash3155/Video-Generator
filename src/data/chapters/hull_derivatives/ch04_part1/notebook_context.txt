Types of Interest Rates & Benchmark Risk-Free Rates\n\nFinancial markets rely on several key interest rate benchmarks to price fixed-income securities and derivative contracts [1, 2]:\n\n*   **Treasury Rates**: The interest rates earned on debt instruments issued by a government in its own currency (e.g., U.S. Treasury Bills, Notes, and Bonds) [3, 4]. Government treasury rates are historically considered virtually free of default risk because a sovereign government can print money to service its local currency debt [3, 4]. However, Treasury rates are often artificially low due to regulatory liquidity requirements on financial institutions and favorable tax treatments [4].\n*   **LIBOR (London Interbank Offered Rate)**: The historical benchmark rate at which large, creditworthy (AAA/AA-rated) banks borrowed unsecured funds from other banks in the interbank market [2, 5, 6]. LIBOR was quoted across multiple maturities (from overnight to 12 months) and currencies (USD, GBP, EUR, CHF, JPY) [6, 7]. Due to structural market shifts and manipulation concerns, LIBOR has been systematically replaced by transaction-based overnight risk-free rates (e.g., SOFR in USD) [5, 8].\n*   **Repo Rates (Repurchase Agreement Rates)**: A repurchase agreement (repo) is an agreement where a party holding securities sells them to a counterparty and agrees to buy them back at a slightly higher price on a specified future date. The percentage difference between the sale price and repurchase price defines the **repo rate**. Because repos are secured by collateral (such as Treasury securities), repo rates carry negligible credit risk and operate as low risk-free borrowing rates.\n*   **OIS (Overnight Indexed Swap) Risk-Free Rates**: An Overnight Indexed Swap (OIS) is an over-the-counter fixed-for-floating swap where the floating interest rate is tied to a daily overnight reference rate (such as the Fed Funds Effective Rate in the U.S. or EONIA/€STR in the Eurozone) compounded over the swap term [2]. Because overnight loans involve minimal credit default exposure over a 24-hour horizon, the **OIS zero-coupon yield curve** has become the primary, authoritative **risk-free rate curve used for discounting cash flows** in derivatives pricing [2].\n\n---\n\n### 2. Measuring Interest Rates: Continuous Compounding vs. Discrete Compounding\n\nInterest rates can be expressed under different compounding frequencies [1, 9]. \n\n#### Mathematical Formulation\nIf an amount \\\\(A\\\\) is invested for \\\\(t\\\\) years at an annual discrete interest rate \\\\(R_m\\\\) compounded \\\\(m\\\\) times per year, the terminal value is given by [1, 10]:\n\\\\[\\text{Terminal Value} = A \\left(1 + \\frac{R_m}{m}\\right)^{m t}\\\\]\n\nAs the compounding frequency \\\\(m \\to \\infty\\\\), compounding becomes continuous, yielding the exponential limit [11]:\n\\\\[\\text{Terminal Value} = A e^{R_c t}\\\\]\n\nwhere \\\\(R_c\\\\) is the continuously compounded interest rate [1].\n\n#### Equivalence & Conversion Formulas\nTo find the continuously compounded rate \\\\(R_c\\\\) that yields the exact same return as a discrete rate \\\\(R_m\\\\) compounded \\\\(m\\\\) times per year, set the compounding factors equal [1]:\n\\\\[e^{R_c} = \\left(1 + \\frac{R_m}{m}\\right)^m\\\\]\n\nTaking the natural logarithm of both sides gives the conversion formula [1]:\n\\\\[\\mathbf{R_c = m \\ln\\left(1 + \\frac{R_m}{m}\\right)}\\\\]\n\nConversely, to convert a continuously compounded rate \\\\(R_c\\\\) into an equivalent discrete rate \\\\(R_m\\\\) compounded \\\\(m\\\\) times per year [1]:\n\\\\[\\mathbf{R_m = m \\left(e^{\\frac{R_c}{m}} - 1\\right)}\\\\]\n\n#### Numerical Examples\n1. **Discrete to Continuous Conversion**: Suppose a bank quotes a rate of \\\\(10\\%\\\\) per annum (\\\\(R_m = 0.10\\\\)) with **semi-annual compounding** (\\\\(m = 2\\\\)). The equivalent continuously compounded rate \\\\(R_c\\\\) is:\n   \\\\[R_c = 2 \\ln\\left(1 + \\frac{0.10}{2}\\right) = 2 \\ln(1.05) = 2 \\times 0.048790 = \\mathbf{9.758\\% \\text{ per annum.}}\\\\]\n2. **Continuous to Discrete Conversion**: Suppose an asset earns a continuously compounded rate of \\\\(8\\%\\\\) per annum (\\\\(R_c = 0.08\\\\)). The equivalent discrete rate \\\\(R_m\\\\) compounded **quarterly** (\\\\(m = 4\\\\)) is:\n   \\\\[R_m = 4 \\left(e^{\\frac{0.08}{4}} - 1\\right) = 4 \\left(e^{0.02} - 1\\right) = 4 (1.0202013 - 1) = \\mathbf{8.081\\% \\text{ per annum.}}\\\\]\n\n---\n\n### 3. Zero Rates (Spot Rates)\n\nAn \\\\(n\\\\)-year **zero-coupon interest rate** (commonly called the \\\\(n\\\\)-year **zero rate** or spot rate) is the interest rate earned on an investment that starts today and lasts for \\\\(n\\\\) years, where both the principal and all accrued interest are paid in a single payment at the end of the \\\\(n\\\\)-year maturity [1]. No intermediate coupon payments are made during the life of the investment [1].\n\nThe discount factor \\\\(d(t)\\\\) representing the present value of \\\\(\\$1\\\\) received at time \\\\(t\\\\) discounted at the continuously compounded zero rate \\\\(r(t)\\\\) is [12]:\n\\\\[d(t) = e^{-r(t) t}\\\\]\n\n---\n\n### 4. Bond Pricing & Bond Yield\n\n#### Bond Pricing Formula\nThe theoretical price \\\\(P\\\\) of a coupon-bearing bond paying cash flows \\\\(c_i\\\\) at future time \\\\(t_i\\\\) (\\\\(i = 1, 2, \\dots, n\\\\)) and principal maturity payment \\\\(M\\\\) at time \\\\(t_n\\\\) is the sum of its cash flows discounted at their respective continuously compounded zero rates \\\\(r_i\\\\) [12]:\n\n\\\\[\\mathbf{P = \\sum_{i=1}^n c_i e^{-r_i t_i} + M e^{-r_n t_n}}\\\\]\n\nwhere:\n*   \\\\(P\\\\) = market/theoretical price of the bond [12].\n*   \\\\(c_i\\\\) = coupon cash flow paid at time \\\\(t_i\\\\) [12].\n*   \\\\(M\\\\) = principal / face value paid at maturity \\\\(t_n\\\\) [12].\n*   \\\\(r_i\\\\) = continuously compounded zero rate for maturity \\\\(t_i\\\\) [12].\n\n#### Bond Yield (Yield to Maturity / YTM)\nA bond's **yield** \\\\(y\\\\) is defined as the single uniform discount rate that, when applied to all future cash flows, equates the present value of the cash flows to the bond's observed market price \\\\(P\\\\) [12]:\n\n\\\\[P = \\sum_{i=1}^n c_i e^{-y t_i} + M e^{-y t_n}\\\\]\n\n---\n\n### 5.
