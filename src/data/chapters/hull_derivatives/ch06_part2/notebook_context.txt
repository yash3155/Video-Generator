Eurodollar Futures and SOFR Futures\n\n* **Definition and Overview**: Eurodollar futures contracts are interest rate futures based on 3-month Eurodollar time deposit rates (historically benchmarked to 3-month USD LIBOR and transitioning to SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate) [1-4]. A Eurodollar is a U.S. dollar deposit held in a commercial bank outside the United States [4].\n* **Contract Specifications and Price Quotation**:\n  * **Notional Principal / Contract Size**: **\\$1,000,000** per contract for 3-month contracts [4, 5].\n  * **Quotation Formula**: Eurodollar and SOFR futures prices are quoted as **\\\\(100 - Q\\\\)**, where \\\\(Q\\\\) is the annualized 3-month interest rate expressed as a percentage [4, 5]. For instance, a futures price quote of **98.00** implies an annualized interest rate of **2.00%** (\\\\(100 - 98 = 2\\\\)) [5].\n  * **Tick Value / Basis Point Value**: A 1 basis point (0.01%) move in the quoted index \\\\(Q\\\\) represents a **\\$25** change in contract value [4]. This is derived as:\n    \\\\[\\text{Contract Value Change} = \\$1,000,000 \\times 0.0001 \\times \\frac{1}{4} = \\$25\\\\] [4]\n  * **Settlement**: Eurodollar/SOFR futures are cash-settled contracts based on the underlying spot rate at expiration [2, 4].\n\n---\n\n### 2. Convexity Adjustments\n\nWhen converting between futures interest rates and forward interest rates, a **convexity adjustment** must be applied because futures contracts are settled daily (marked-to-market), whereas forward contracts are settled in a single payment at maturity [2, 5].\n\n* **Formula**:\n  \\\\[\\text{Forward Rate} = \\text{Futures Rate} - \\frac{1}{2} \\sigma^2 T_1 T_2\\\\]\n* **Variable Definitions**:\n  * \\\\(\\sigma\\\\): Annual volatility of the short-term interest rate [2].\n  * \\\\(T_1\\\\): Time until maturity of the futures contract (in years) [2].\n  * \\\\(T_2\\\\): Time until maturity of the rate underlying the futures contract (for a 3-month underlying rate, \\\\(T_2 = T_1 + 0.25\\\\)) [2].\n* **Impact & Direction**: Because daily mark-to-market margin settlements correlate positively with interest rate movements, the **futures rate is systematically higher than the corresponding forward rate**; hence, the convexity adjustment subtracts \\\\(\\frac{1}{2} \\sigma^2 T_1 T_2\\\\) from the futures rate to obtain the forward rate [2].\n\n---\n\n### 3. Macaulay Duration and Modified Duration\n\n**Duration** measures the price sensitivity of a fixed-income security or portfolio to changes in market interest rates [6, 7].\n\n#### A. Macaulay Duration\n* **Definition**: The weighted average maturity of a bond's cash flows, where the weights equal the present value of each cash flow divided by the total bond price [7].\n* **Formula (Continuous Compounding)**:\n  \\\\[D = \\sum_{i=1}^{n} t_i \\cdot \\left[ \\frac{c_i e^{-y t_i}}{B} \\right]\\\\]\n  where \\\\(c_i\\\\) is the cash flow at time \\\\(t_i\\\\), \\\\(y\\\\) is the continuously compounded yield, and \\\\(B = \\sum c_i e^{-y t_i}\\\\) is the total bond price [7].\n* **Price Change Sensitivity**:\n  \\\\[\\frac{\\Delta B}{B} \\approx -D \\cdot \\Delta y\\\\] [7]\n\n#### B. Modified Duration\n* **Definition**: The adjustment of Macaulay duration to reflect interest rates compounded \\\\(m\\\\) times per year [7].\n* **Formula**:\n  \\\\[D^* = \\frac{D}{1 + \\frac{y}{m}}\\\\]\n* **Percentage Price Change**:\n  \\\\[\\frac{\\Delta B}{B} \\approx -D^* \\cdot \\Delta y \\quad \\implies \\quad \\Delta B \\approx -B \\cdot D^* \\cdot \\Delta y\\\\] [7]\n\n---\n\n### 4. Duration-Based Hedging Strategy Using Futures\n\nTo hedge a fixed-income portfolio against interest rate changes using interest rate futures, the duration-based hedging ratio (\\\\(N^*\\\\)) determines the required number of futures contracts [7].\n\n* **Hedging Ratio Formula**:\n  \\\\[N^* = \\frac{P \\cdot D_P}{V_F \\cdot D_F}\\\\]\n* **Variable Definitions**:\n  * \\\\(P\\\\): Current market value of the portfolio being hedged [7].\n  * \\\\(D_P\\\\): Modified duration of the portfolio being hedged at the hedge maturity [7].\n  * \\\\(V_F\\\\): Settlement price of one interest rate futures contract (\\\\(\\text{Futures Price} \\times \\text{Contract Multiplier}\\\\)) [7].\n  * \\\\(D_F\\\\): Modified duration of the underlying bond/asset for the futures contract at hedge maturity [7].\n* **Hedge Direction**:\n  * **Short Futures Position (Sell \\\\(N^*\\\\))**: Taken when holding an asset portfolio to protect against **rising interest rates / falling bond prices** [7].\n  * **Long Futures Position (Buy \\\\(N^*\\\\))**: Taken when protecting against **falling interest rates / rising bond prices** [7].\n\n---\n\n### 5. Bank Asset-Liability Management (Business Snapshot 6.3)\n\n* **Core Issue / Structural Mismatch**: Commercial banks naturally experience a duration mismatch between assets and liabilities [8]. Bank **assets** (such as fixed-rate mortgages and long-term commercial loans) typically have **long durations**, whereas bank **liabilities** (such as short-term customer deposits and money market certificates) have **short durations** [8].\n* **Interest Rate Exposure**: If interest rates rise sharply, the market value of the bank's long-duration assets falls significantly more than the value of its short-duration liabilities, causing a severe reduction in equity/capital [8, 9].\n* **ALM Strategy via Derivatives**: Banks manage and immunize this balance sheet duration gap by entering into **duration-based hedging strategies** using interest rate futures or interest rate swaps [7, 8].
