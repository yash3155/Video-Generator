### Call & Put Options Basics

#### Core Concepts & Definitions
* **Option**: A financial contract giving the holder the **right**, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset by a specified date for a specified price [1, 2].
* **Call Option**: Gives the holder the right to **buy** an underlying asset by a certain date for a certain price [1].
* **Put Option**: Gives the holder the right to **sell** an underlying asset by a certain date for a certain price [1].
* **Strike Price (Exercise Price)**: The predetermined price specified in the option contract at which the asset can be bought or sold [1].
* **Expiration Date (Maturity Date)**: The precise final date on which the option contract expires [1].
* **Option Premium**: The up-front price paid by the buyer to the seller (writer) to acquire the option contract [3].
* **European vs. American Options**: 
  * A **European option** can be exercised *only* on the maturity date itself [1, 2].
  * An **American option** can be exercised at *any time* during its life up to expiration [2].

#### Options vs. Futures/Forward Contracts
| Feature | Futures / Forward Contracts | Options Contracts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Obligation** | Holder is **committed** (obligated) to buy or sell [2]. | Holder has a **choice** (right, no obligation) to buy or sell [2]. |
| **Up-front Cost** | Costs nothing up-front to enter (except margin deposits) [3]. | Requires payment of an up-front **option premium** [3]. |
| **Payoff Profile** | Symmetric (unlimited downside and upside) [4]. | Asymmetric (downside capped for buyer at premium paid) [4]. |

#### Real-World Market Quotes: CBOE Google Options (May 13, 2015)
* **Underlying Stock Price**: Bid **\$532.20**, Offer **\$532.34** [5].
* **Option Price Dynamics**:
  * **Call Option Prices** *decrease* as the strike price increases [6, 7].
  * **Put Option Prices** *increase* as the strike price increases [6, 7].
  * Both call and put options become **more valuable** as time to maturity increases [7].

#### Numerical Case Calculations
1. **Buying a Call Option (Google Dec \$550 Call)** [7]:
   * **Contract Details**: 1 contract = 100 shares; Offer price = **\$29.00** per share; Up-front cost = **\$2,900** [7-9].
   * **Scenario A (Stock \\(\le \$550\\) at Expiration)**: Option expires unexercised; total loss is capped at **\$2,900** [10].
   * **Scenario B (Stock rises to \$650)**: Exercising allows buying 100 shares at \$550 and selling at \$650 for a \$10,000 payoff [10]. Subtracting the \$2,900 initial premium yields a net profit of **\$7,100** [10].
2. **Selling (Writing) a Put Option (Google Sept \$525 Put)** [11]:
   * **Contract Details**: Bid price = **\$22.40** per share; Immediate up-front cash inflow = \\(100 \times \$22.40 = \mathbf{\$2,240}\\) [11].
   * **Scenario A (Stock stays above \$525)**: Option expires worthless; writer keeps maximum profit of **\$2,240** [11].
   * **Scenario B (Stock drops to \$480)**: Writer is assigned to buy 100 shares at \$525 when they are worth \$480, incurring a gross loss of \$4,500 [11]. Net loss after initial premium received = \\(\$4,500 - \$2,240 = \mathbf{\$2,260}\\) [11].

#### The Four Option Positions
1. **Long Call**: Buyer of a call (right to buy) [12, 13].
2. **Short Call**: Seller/Writer of a call (obligation to sell if exercised) [12, 13].
3. **Long Put**: Buyer of a put (right to sell) [13].
4. **Short Put**: Seller/Writer of a put (obligation to buy if exercised) [13].

### The Three Traders: Hedgers, Speculators, Arbitrageurs

```
                        ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
                        │          Derivatives Traders            │
                        └────────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                                             │
         ┌───────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                   ▼                                   ▼
┌─────────────────┐                 ┌─────────────────┐                 ┌─────────────────┐
│     Hedgers     │                 │   Speculators   │                 │  Arbitrageurs   │
│  Eliminate Risk │                 │   Bet on Trend  │                 │ Lock Riskless $ │
└─────────────────┘                 └─────────────────┘                 └─────────────────┘
```

#### 1. Hedgers
* **Definition**: Market participants who use futures, forwards, or options to **reduce or eliminate financial risk** arising from potential future price movements in an underlying asset [14].
* **Forward Contract Hedging Example (Example 1.1)** [15, 16]:
  * **ImportCo**: Owes **£10 million** in 3 months. Buys £10M in the 3-month forward market at **1.5742 USD/GBP**, locking in a total payment of **\$15,742,000** [15-17].
  * **ExportCo**: Receives **£30 million** in 3 months. Sells £30M in the 3-month forward market at **1.5736 USD/GBP**, locking in total proceeds of **\$47,208,000** [16-18].
  * *Key Trade-off*: Forward hedging **neutralizes risk** by setting a fixed price; it eliminates downside risk, but also eliminates any potential gain from favorable market moves [19, 20].
* **Option Hedging Example (Example 1.2)** [19, 21]:
  * **Investor Portfolio**: Owns 1,000 shares worth **\$28** each (\$28,000 value) [19, 21].
  * **Hedging Strategy**: Buys 10 July \$27.50 put contracts at **\$1** per option (\$1,000 total cost) [19, 21].
  * **Outcome**: Guarantees that shares can be sold for at least **\$27.50** (\$26,500 net value after option cost) if stock plummets, while preserving full participation in upside gains if stock price surges [22, 23]. Options act as **insurance** [20].

#### 2. Speculators
* **Definition**: Traders who take positions to **bet on the future direction** of market prices, seeking high returns through financial leverage [14, 24].
* **Speculation with Spot vs. Futures (Table 1.4)** [24, 25]:
  * **Scenario**: Speculator has \$20,000 margin capital and believes GBP will rise against USD [24, 25]. Current spot = **1.5470**, April futures = **1.5410** [25, 26].
  * **Strategy A (Spot Market)**: Requires **\$386,750** cash to buy £250,000 [24, 25, 27].
  * **Strategy B (CME Futures)**: Takes long position in 4 April futures contracts (£250,000 total) requiring only **\$20,000 initial margin** [25-27].
  * **Results at April Expiration**:
    * If Exchange Rate rises to **1.6000**: Futures yield **\$14,750 profit** on \$20k deposit vs. Spot profit of **\$13,250** on \$386.7k capital [25, 28].
    * If Exchange Rate falls to **1.5000**: Futures yield **\$10,250 loss** vs. Spot loss of **\$11,750** [25, 27, 28].
* **Speculation with Shares vs. Options (Table 1.5)** [25, 29]:
  * **Scenario**: Speculator has \$2,000 capital to bet on a \$20 stock [25, 29]. Two-month call option (\$22.50 strike) costs **\$1** [25, 29].
  * **Strategy A**: Buy 100 shares at \$20 (\$2,000 total) [25].
  * **Strategy B**: Buy 2,000 call options (20 contracts) at \$1 (\$2,000 total) [25, 30].
  * **Results at Expiration**:
    * If Stock rises to **\$27**: Share profit = **\$700**; Options profit = \\((2,000 \times \$4.50) - \$2,000 = \mathbf{\$7,000}\\) (**10x higher profit**) [31, 32].
    * If Stock falls to **\$15**: Share loss = **\$500**; Options loss = **\$2,000** (**100% loss of investment**) [32].

#### 3. Arbitrageurs
* **Definition**: Traders who simultaneously enter into transactions in two or more markets to **lock in a riskless profit** from price discrepancies [4, 33].
* **Dual-Listed Stock Arbitrage (Example 1.3)** [33, 34]:
  * **Market Quotes**: Stock price in New York = **\$152**; Stock price in London = **£100**; Exchange rate = **\$1.5500/£** [33, 34].
  * **Arbitrage Execution**:
    1. Buy 100 shares in New York for **\$15,200** [33, 34].
    2. Sell 100 shares in London for **£10,000** [33, 34].
    3. Convert £10,000 proceeds to USD at \$1.5500/£ = **\$15,500** [33, 34].
  * **Risk-Free Profit**: \\(100 \times [(\$1.55 \times 100) - \$152] = \mathbf{\$300}\\) [34, 35].
  * **Market Equilibrium Effect**: Buying in NY drives the dollar price up, while selling in London drives the sterling price down until the arbitrage window closes [35, 36].

### Leverage in Futures vs. Options

| Feature | Futures Leverage | Options Leverage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Capital Requirement** | Small initial margin deposit (~5–10% of contract value) [27, 29]. | Small option premium relative to full stock purchase price [25, 37]. |
| **Upside Potential** | Very large / theoretically unlimited [4, 28]. | Very large / magnified returns relative to outlay [31, 37]. |
| **Downside Risk** | **Symmetric & Unlimited**: Losses can far exceed the initial margin deposit [4]. | **Asymmetric & Capped**: Maximum loss is strictly limited to the up-front option premium paid [4]. |

### Business Snapshot 1.3: Hedge Fund "2-and-20" Fee Structure & Strategies

* **Industry Overview & Regulations**:
  * Global assets under management in hedge funds exceed **\$2 trillion** [38].
  * Hedge funds accept funds only from institutional/professional fund managers or wealthy individuals, operating free from mutual fund regulations regarding daily share redeemability, leverage limits, and public disclosures [38, 39].
* **"2-and-20" Fee Model**:
  * **2% Management Fee**: Calculated annually on total assets under management [38].
  * **20% Incentive/Performance Fee**: Calculated as 20% of total profits generated by the fund [38].
* **Key Hedge Fund Trading Strategies**:
  1. **Long/Short Equities**: Long undervalued stocks and short overvalued stocks to minimize overall market directional exposure [40].
  2. **Convertible Arbitrage**: Long position in undervalued convertible bonds paired with an actively managed short position in the underlying equity [40].
  3. **Distressed Securities**: Buying debt or equity securities of companies facing or near bankruptcy [40].
  4. **Emerging Markets**: Investing in corporate and sovereign debt/equity within developing nations [40, 41].
  5. **Global Macro**: High-conviction directional trades based on anticipated global macroeconomic trends [41].
  6. **Merger Arbitrage**: Trading equities after a public takeover announcement to profit if the deal successfully closes [41].

### Business Snapshot 1.4: Jérôme Kerviel's €4.9B Société Générale Rogue Trade

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                      Jérôme Kerviel (SocGen 2005-2008)                  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Mandate: Equity Index Arbitrage (DAX, CAC 40, Euro Stoxx 50)             │
│ Operational Exploit: Used compliance knowledge to fake hedging trades  │
│ Position Size: Unhedged directional bets reached tens of billions of € │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ January 2008 Unwinding Loss: €4.9 Billion ($7.2 Billion)                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

* **Trader Background**: Jérôme Kerviel joined Société Générale (SocGen) in 2000 in the compliance department [42]. In 2005, he was promoted to junior trader in the bank's **Delta One** team, trading European equity indices (DAX, CAC 40, Euro Stoxx 50) [42, 43].
* **Official Mandate**: Identify low-risk arbitrage opportunities between equity index futures and constituent share prices or across different exchanges [43].
* **The Fraud Mechanism**:
  * Kerviel used his intimate knowledge of internal middle-office and compliance controls to take **massive directional bets** on equity indices [43, 44].
  * He entered **fictitious offsetting trades** into SocGen's system to give the false appearance that his speculative positions were fully hedged [44].
  * His unauthorized, unhedged directional exposure grew to **tens of billions of euros** [44].
* **The Discovery and Loss**:
  * In January 2008, SocGen uncovered the unauthorized trades [44].
  * Over a 3-day liquidation period in a falling market, SocGen unwound the massive positions, incurring a net loss of **€4.9 billion** [44].
* **Historical Rogue Trader Context**:
  * **Nick Leeson (Barings Bank, 1995)**: Nikkei 225 index arbitrage mandate converted into hidden directional bets; lost **\$1 billion**, collapsing the 200-year-old bank [45].
  * **John Rusnak (Allied Irish Bank, 2002)**: Unauthorized FX trading resulted in a **\$700 million** loss [45, 46].
* **Core Risk Management Lesson**: Financial institutions must establish unambiguous risk limits and strictly monitor trading activity daily to ensure limits cannot be bypassed [46, 47].
