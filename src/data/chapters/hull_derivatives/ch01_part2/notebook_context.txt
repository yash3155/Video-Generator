### 1. Call & Put Options Basics

#### Core Concepts & Definitions
* **Option**: A financial instrument giving the holder the **right**, but not the **obligation**, to buy or sell an underlying asset by a specific date for a specific price [2, 3].
* **Call Option**: Gives the holder the right to **buy** an underlying asset by a specified date for a specified price [2].
* **Put Option**: Gives the holder the right to **sell** an underlying asset by a specified date for a specified price [2].
* **Strike Price / Exercise Price**: The fixed price specified in the contract at which the underlying asset can be bought or sold [2].
* **Expiration Date / Maturity Date**: The exact date specified in the contract on which the option expires [2].
* **European Option**: Can be exercised **only** on its maturity date [2].
* **American Option**: Can be exercised at **any time** during its life up to the expiration date [2].
* **Option Premium**: The up-front price paid by the option buyer to the option seller/writer [4]. Entering a futures contract costs nothing up front (except margin), whereas an option requires this initial payment [4].
* **The Four Option Positions**:
  1. **Long Call**: Buyer of a call (right to buy) [5, 6].
  2. **Short Call / Writer**: Seller of a call (obligation to sell if exercised) [5, 6].
  3. **Long Put**: Buyer of a put (right to sell) [5, 6].
  4. **Short Put / Writer**: Seller of a put (obligation to buy if exercised) [5, 6].

#### Key Price Relationships & Properties
* **Call Option Price**: Decreases as the strike price increases [7, 8].
* **Put Option Price**: Increases as the strike price increases [7, 8].
* **Time to Maturity**: Both call and put options tend to become more valuable as their time to maturity increases [7, 8].

#### Numerical Data & Market Examples
* **Standard Stock Option Size**: In the United States, one stock option contract covers **100 shares** [9, 10].
* **Google (GOOG) Market Quotes Example (May 13, 2015)** [11]:
  * Stock Price: Bid \$532.20, Offer \$532.34 [11].
  * **Buying a Call Option**: A trader buys 1 Dec \$550 call contract at the offer price of \$29.00 per share [8]. Total up-front cost = \$29.00 × 100 = **\$2,900** [8, 10].
    * *Outcome A (Stock ≤ \$550)*: Option expires unexercised; net loss = **\$2,900** [12].
    * *Outcome B (Stock rises to \$650)*: Exercise option to buy 100 shares at \$550 and immediately sell at \$650. Gross payoff = \$10,000; **net profit** = \$10,000 − \$2,900 = **\$7,100** [12].
  * **Selling a Put Option**: A trader sells 1 Sept \$525 put contract at the bid price of \$22.40 per share [13]. Up-front cash inflow = \$22.40 × 100 = **\$2,240** [13].
    * *Outcome A (Stock > \$525)*: Option is not exercised; net profit = **\$2,240** [13].
    * *Outcome B (Stock falls to \$480)*: Buyer exercises; trader must buy 100 shares at \$525 when worth only \$480. Gross loss = \$4,500; **net loss** = \$4,500 − \$2,240 = **\$2,260** [13].

### 2. The Three Traders (Hedgers, Speculators, Arbitrageurs)

#### A. Hedgers
* **Core Definition**: Traders who face existing price risk from potential market movements and use futures, forwards, or options to **reduce or eliminate** that risk [14-16].
* **Forward Contracts vs. Options for Hedging**:
  * **Forward/Futures Contracts**: Designed to **neutralize risk** by fixing/locking in the exact future price [17-19]. No up-front cost, but eliminates upside potential [18, 19].
  * **Options**: Act as **insurance** [19]. Protect against adverse price movements while allowing participation in favorable price movements, at the cost of an up-front premium [18, 19].
* **Real-World Examples & Numerical Calculations**:
  * **ImportCo (Forward Hedging)**: On May 13, 2015, ImportCo owes £10 million on August 13, 2015 [20, 21]. It buys £10M 3-month forward at 1.5742 USD/GBP, fixing its cost at **\$15,742,000** [20-22].
  * **ExportCo (Forward Hedging)**: On May 13, 2015, ExportCo expects to receive £30 million on August 13, 2015 [21, 22]. It sells £30M 3-month forward at 1.5736 USD/GBP, locking in **\$47,208,000** [17, 21, 22].
  * **Stock Investor (Option Hedging - Example 1.2)**: An investor owns 1,000 shares at \$28 (\$28,000 total) [18, 23]. Buys 10 July \$27.50 put contracts at \$1 each (total cost = **\$1,000**) [18, 23].
    * If stock falls below \$27.50, options are exercised to realize \$27,500 (\$26,500 net of option cost) [24].
    * If stock price rises above \$27.50, options expire worthless, but portfolio value appreciates fully above \$27,500 [24, 25].

#### B. Speculators
* **Core Definition**: Traders who have no underlying exposure but wish to **bet on the future direction** of a market variable to make a profit [14, 16, 26].
* **Speculation via Futures**:
  * Provides leverage by requiring only a small margin deposit rather than full asset purchase [27, 28].
  * **Numerical Data (Table 1.4)**: Speculator with \$20,000 bets £250,000 will rise (Spot = 1.5470, April Futures = 1.5410) [29, 30].
    * *Spot Purchase*: Costs \$386,750 up front [27, 30]. If exchange rate goes to 1.6000, profit = **\$13,250** [31, 32].
    * *Futures Contracts*: Requires \$20,000 initial margin [28, 30]. If exchange rate goes to 1.6000, profit = **\$14,750** [31, 32].
* **Speculation via Options**:
  * **Numerical Data (Table 1.5)**: Speculator has \$2,000 in October; stock is \$20 [28, 32].
    * *Strategy 1 (Buy Stock)*: Purchases 100 shares at \$20 = \$2,000 [30, 32]. If stock rises to \$27, profit = **\$700** [32, 33]. If stock falls to \$15, loss = **\$500** [34].
    * *Strategy 2 (Buy Call Options)*: Purchases 2,000 calls (20 contracts) with \$22.50 strike at \$1 = \$2,000 [28, 32]. If stock rises to \$27, payoff = 2,000 × \$4.50 = \$9,000; net profit = **\$7,000** (10 times higher than stock) [33, 34]. If stock falls to \$15, options expire worthless; loss = **\$2,000** (100% loss) [34].

#### C. Arbitrageurs
* **Core Definition**: Traders who take offsetting positions in two or more financial instruments/markets simultaneously to **lock in a riskless profit** [14, 16, 35].
* **Role in Financial Markets**: The actions of arbitrageurs cause prices in different markets to converge, ensuring that significant price disparities rarely persist in practice [36, 37].
* **Numerical Data (Example 1.3)**:
  * Dual-listed stock price: New York = \$152/share; London = £100/share; Exchange rate = \$1.5500 per £ [38, 39].
  * Trade: Buy 100 shares in NY for \$15,200; sell in London for £10,000; convert £10,000 to USD at \$1.5500 = \$15,500 [38, 39].
  * **Risk-free Profit** = \$15,500 − \$15,200 = **\$300** [36, 39].

### 3. Leverage in Futures vs. Options

| Feature | Futures Leverage | Options Leverage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source of Leverage** | Obtained via small **margin account deposits** relative to contract value [27, 28]. | Obtained via paying an **up-front premium** for contract rights [40]. |
| **Upside Potential** | Very large / unlimited [31, 40]. | Very large / magnified outcomes [34, 40]. |
| **Downside Risk** | **Very large / unlimited**: Losses can far exceed initial margin requirements [35, 40]. | **Strictly limited**: Maximum potential loss is capped at the up-front premium paid [34, 35]. |

### 4. Hedge Fund 2-and-20 Strategies (Business Snapshot 1.3)

#### Definitions & Key Characteristics
* **Hedge Funds**: Private investment funds that collect money from institutional investors or financially sophisticated individuals [41]. Unlike mutual funds, they do not publicly offer securities and are relatively free from regulatory constraints (e.g., liquidity rules, leverage caps, mandate disclosures) [41, 42].
* **Total Assets Under Management**: Approximately **\$2 trillion** globally [42].
* **Fee Structure ("2-and-20")**:
  * **2%** management fee based on total assets under management [42].
  * **20%** performance/incentive fee based on profits generated [42].

#### Manager's Three-Step Workflow
1. Evaluate risks to which the fund is exposed [43].
2. Decide which risks are acceptable and which will be hedged [43].
3. Devise strategies (primarily involving derivatives) to hedge unacceptable risks [43].

#### Six Core Hedge Fund Strategies
1. **Long/Short Equities**: Buy undervalued stocks and short overvalued stocks to minimize net market exposure [44].
2. **Convertible Arbitrage**: Take long positions in undervalued convertible bonds combined with short positions in the underlying stock [44].
3. **Distressed Securities**: Purchase debt or equity securities of companies in or facing bankruptcy [44].
4. **Emerging Markets**: Invest in sovereign debt or corporate debt/equity of emerging/developing nations [44, 45].
5. **Global Macro**: Position portfolios to capitalize on global macroeconomic trends [45].
6. **Merger Arbitrage**: Take positions following merger announcements to profit if deals close [45].

### 5. Jérôme Kerviel's €4.9B SocGen Rogue Trade (Business Snapshot 1.4)

#### Case Study Facts & Numerical Data
* **Trader**: Jérôme Kerviel joined Société Générale (SocGen) compliance in 2000 and was promoted to junior trader on the Delta One team in 2005 [46].
* **Trading Mandate**: Search for low-risk equity index arbitrage opportunities (e.g., DAX, CAC 40, Euro Stoxx 50) [46, 47].
* **Rogue Trading Scheme**: Used internal compliance knowledge to bypass risk controls [47]. Took massive unhedged directional positions on stock indices while entering **fictitious trades** to make positions look hedged [47, 48].
* **Exposure & Loss**: Unhedged directional positions reached **tens of billions of euros** [48]. Discovered in January 2008; SocGen unwound positions over 3 days, suffering a net loss of **€4.9 billion** [48].

#### Author Analogies & Historical Comparisons
* **Nick Leeson (Barings Bank, 1990s)**: Assigned to arbitrage Nikkei 225 futures between Singapore and Osaka [49]. Instead took unauthorized directional futures and options bets, losing **\$1 billion** and causing the collapse of the 200-year-old bank [49].
* **John Rusnak (Allied Irish Bank, 2002)**: Lost **\$700 million** through unauthorized foreign exchange trading [49, 50].

#### Key Risk Control Lesson
* Financial and nonfinancial corporations must establish **unambiguous risk limits** and daily monitoring procedures to ensure derivatives are used strictly for their intended purposes [50, 51].
