# Chapter 3: Inflation and Real Returns

*Money That Grows: A Beginner's Primer on Interest, Rates and Inflation* - OpenLectern Contributors - CC BY 4.0

## 3.1 When prices rise (page 11)

**Inflation** is a general rise in prices over time. When prices rise, each dollar buys less: its **purchasing power** falls. A balance that grows by 5% in a year in which prices rise by 5% buys no more at the end of the year than at the start.

## 3.2 Measuring inflation with the CPI (page 12)

Statistics offices measure inflation with a **consumer price index**, or **CPI**. They price a fixed basket of goods and services that typical households buy, such as food, rent, transport and clothing, and compare its cost over time. The **inflation rate** between two dates is the percentage change in the index:

inflation = (CPI_new - CPI_old) / CPI_old

For example, if the index rises from 200 to 206 in one year, inflation for that year is (206 - 200) / 200 = 0.03, or 3%.

## 3.3 Nominal and real returns (page 13)

The **nominal return** is the percentage growth of your balance in dollars. The **real return** is the growth of what your balance can buy. The two are linked by the exact relationship

(1 + nominal) = (1 + real) x (1 + inflation)

which the American economist Irving Fisher popularised in his 1930 book *The Theory of Interest*. Rearranged, real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1. If an investment returns 7% while inflation is 3%, the real return is 1.07 / 1.03 - 1, about 0.0388, or 3.88%. The quick approximation "real return is about nominal minus inflation" gives 4%, which is close when both rates are small.

## 3.4 Limits and common mistakes (page 14)

- The CPI measures an average basket. Your own costs can rise faster or slower than the index.
- A common mistake is to celebrate a nominal gain without subtracting inflation. A savings account paying 2% while inflation is 4% loses purchasing power every year.
- The subtraction shortcut becomes inaccurate when inflation or returns are large; use the exact formula.
