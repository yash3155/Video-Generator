# Chapter 1: Simple and Compound Interest

*Money That Grows: A Beginner's Primer on Interest, Rates and Inflation* - OpenLectern Contributors - CC BY 4.0

## 1.1 Why money has a price (page 1)

When you lend money, you give up the chance to use it yourself. Interest is the price a borrower pays for using someone else's money for a period of time. The amount you lend or deposit at the start is called the **principal**. The **interest rate** is the interest for one period expressed as a fraction or percentage of the principal; unless we say otherwise, the period in this book is one year, written "per annum" or **p.a.**

A saver who deposits money in a bank is a lender: the bank borrows the deposit and pays interest for it. A borrower who takes a loan pays interest to the lender. The same arithmetic works in both directions.

## 1.2 Simple interest (page 2)

With **simple interest**, interest is paid only on the original principal. If the principal is P dollars, the annual rate is r (as a decimal) and the time is t years, the interest earned is

I = P r t

and the balance at the end is P + I. The interest for each year is the same amount, so a simple-interest balance grows in a straight line.

## 1.3 Compound interest (page 3)

With **compound interest**, the interest earned in each period is added to the balance, and the next period's interest is calculated on that larger balance. Interest starts earning interest. If interest is added once per year, the balance after t years is

A = P (1 + r)^t

where A is the amount (balance) after t years, P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, and t is the number of years. Because each year multiplies the balance by the same factor (1 + r), a compound-interest balance grows along a curve that gets steeper over time. This pattern is called **exponential growth**.

## 1.4 A worked example (page 4)

Suppose you deposit 1,000 dollars at 5% per year for 3 years.

- Simple interest: I = 1,000 x 0.05 x 3 = 150 dollars, so the balance is 1,150 dollars.
- Compound interest, added once a year: after year 1 the balance is 1,050.00 dollars; after year 2 it is 1,102.50 dollars; after year 3 it is 1,157.63 dollars (rounded to the cent). Using the formula, A = 1,000 x 1.05^3 = 1,157.625.

The compound account earns 157.63 dollars of interest instead of 150 dollars. The extra 7.63 dollars is interest earned on earlier interest. The gap looks small after three years, but it widens every year, because the compound balance keeps multiplying while the simple balance only adds.

## 1.5 A two-hundred-year experiment (page 5)

In 1790 Benjamin Franklin's will left 1,000 pounds sterling each to the cities of Boston and Philadelphia. The money was to be lent at 5% interest to young married craftsmen who had finished their apprenticeships, and the repaid loans were to be lent again. Franklin calculated that after 100 years each fund would hold about 131,000 pounds. He directed that 100,000 pounds then be spent on public works and the remaining 31,000 pounds be left to compound for another 100 years, when he expected about 4,061,000 pounds.

His arithmetic was sound: 1,000 x 1.05^100 is about 131,501. The real funds fell far short. Fewer craftsmen borrowed than Franklin expected, some loans were never repaid, and money sometimes sat idle earning little. When the trusts were wound up around 1990, the Boston fund was worth roughly 5 million dollars and the Philadelphia fund roughly 2 million dollars. Compounding is powerful, but only for money that is actually invested at the assumed rate, year after year.

## 1.6 Limits and common mistakes (page 6)

- The formula A = P (1 + r)^t assumes the rate stays the same for the whole period and that nothing is withdrawn. Real rates change.
- A common beginner mistake is to multiply the yearly interest by the number of years and believe that this is compound interest. That calculation is simple interest; it ignores interest on interest.
- Another mistake is to use the rate as a whole number. A rate of 5% must be written as 0.05 in the formulas.
- Over short periods the two methods give similar answers. Over long periods the difference becomes very large.
