# Orientation research: Money That Grows: A Beginner's Primer on Interest, Rates and Inflation

## Chapter 1: Simple and Compound Interest (p1)

## Research context: Money That Grows - Chapter 1, Part 1 (Simple and Compound Interest)

### Chapter scope

- This part covers the whole of Chapter 1: sections 1.1 to 1.6, pages 1 to 6.
- 1.1 Why money has a price (p. 1); 1.2 Simple interest (p. 2); 1.3 Compound interest (p. 3); 1.4 A worked example (p. 4); 1.5 A two-hundred-year experiment (p. 5); 1.6 Limits and common mistakes (p. 6).
- The next part of the course is Chapter 2, which compares quoted rates (APR and APY) and introduces the Rule of 72. Nothing from Chapter 2 belongs in this part.

### Fact ledger

#### Definitions

- D1. Interest is the price a borrower pays for using someone else's money for a period of time. (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 1)
- D2. The principal is the amount lent or deposited at the start. (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 1)
- D3. The interest rate is the interest for one period expressed as a fraction or percentage of the principal; in this book the period is one year, written "per annum" or p.a. (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 1)
- D4. A saver who deposits money in a bank is a lender: the bank borrows the deposit and pays interest for it; the same arithmetic works for a borrower paying a lender. (Ch. 1, §1.1, p. 1)
- D5. Simple interest is interest paid only on the original principal. (Ch. 1, §1.2, p. 2)
- D6. Compound interest adds each period's interest to the balance, so the next period's interest is calculated on the larger balance: interest starts earning interest. (Ch. 1, §1.3, p. 3)
- D7. Exponential growth is the pattern in which each period multiplies the balance by the same factor, so the curve gets steeper over time. (Ch. 1, §1.3, p. 3)

#### Rules and formulas

- R1. Simple interest formula: I = P r t, where I is the interest in dollars, P the principal in dollars, r the annual rate as a decimal (per year), and t the time in years. The end balance is P + I. (Ch. 1, §1.2, p. 2)
- R2. Under simple interest the interest for each year is the same amount, so the balance grows in a straight line. (Ch. 1, §1.2, p. 2)
- R3. Compound interest formula with interest added once per year: A = P (1 + r)^t, where A is the balance in dollars after t years, P the principal in dollars, r the annual rate as a decimal, and t the number of years. In LaTeX: A = P(1 + r)^{t}. (Ch. 1, §1.3, p. 3)
- R4. Each year a compound balance is multiplied by the same factor (1 + r). (Ch. 1, §1.3, p. 3)

#### Worked numerical example

- N1. Inputs: principal 1,000 dollars, rate 5% per year (r = 0.05), time 3 years. (Ch. 1, §1.4, p. 4)
- N2. Simple interest: I = 1,000 x 0.05 x 3 = 150 dollars; balance 1,150 dollars. (Ch. 1, §1.4, p. 4)
- N3. Compound interest, added yearly: year 1 balance 1,050.00 dollars; year 2 balance 1,102.50 dollars; year 3 balance 1,157.63 dollars (rounded to the cent); by formula A = 1,000 x 1.05^3 = 1,157.625. (Ch. 1, §1.4, p. 4)
- N4. The compound account earns 157.63 dollars of interest instead of 150 dollars; the extra 7.63 dollars is interest earned on earlier interest, and the gap widens every year. (Ch. 1, §1.4, p. 4)

#### Historical case

- H1. In 1790 Benjamin Franklin's will left 1,000 pounds sterling each to Boston and Philadelphia, to be lent at 5% interest to young married craftsmen who had finished their apprenticeships; repaid loans were to be lent again. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- H2. Franklin calculated that each fund would hold about 131,000 pounds after 100 years; 100,000 pounds were then to be spent on public works and 31,000 pounds left to compound for another 100 years, reaching about 4,061,000 pounds. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- H3. Franklin's arithmetic was sound: 1,000 x 1.05^100 is about 131,501. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- H4. The real funds fell far short: fewer craftsmen borrowed than expected, some loans were never repaid, and money sometimes sat idle; when the trusts were wound up around 1990, Boston's fund was worth roughly 5 million dollars and Philadelphia's roughly 2 million dollars. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- H5. Lesson the author draws: compounding is powerful, but only for money actually invested at the assumed rate, year after year. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)

#### Assumptions, limitations and common mistakes

- L1. Assumption: A = P (1 + r)^t assumes the rate stays the same for the whole period and nothing is withdrawn; real rates change. (Ch. 1, §1.6, p. 6)
- M1. Common mistake: multiplying the yearly interest by the number of years and calling it compound interest; that is simple interest and ignores interest on interest. (Ch. 1, §1.6, p. 6)
- M2. Common mistake: using the rate as a whole number; 5% must be written as 0.05 in the formulas. (Ch. 1, §1.6, p. 6)
- L2. Over short periods the two methods give similar answers; over long periods the difference becomes very large. (Ch. 1, §1.6, p. 6)

### Real-world problem

- Actors: Benjamin Franklin and the cities of Boston and Philadelphia; young married craftsmen who needed loans to start businesses. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- Situation: Franklin wanted a modest gift of 1,000 pounds per city to grow into a large public fund over two centuries by lending it at 5% and re-lending the repayments. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- Failure: the funds reached only roughly 5 million and 2 million dollars by about 1990, far below Franklin's projection, because money was not continuously invested at the assumed rate. (Ch. 1, §1.5, p. 5)
- Invention or idea: compound interest, A = P (1 + r)^t, where interest earns interest and growth is exponential; it explains both Franklin's projection and why idle money breaks it. (Ch. 1, §1.3, p. 3)

### Terms and acronyms

- Interest: the price paid for using someone else's money for a time. (p. 1)
- Principal (P): the starting amount lent or deposited. (p. 1)
- Interest rate (r): interest per period as a fraction or percentage of the principal. (p. 1)
- p.a.: per annum, meaning per year. (p. 1)
- Simple interest: interest on the original principal only. (p. 2)
- Compound interest: interest added to the balance so that interest earns interest. (p. 3)
- Exponential growth: growth by the same multiplying factor each period. (p. 3)

## Chapter 2: Rates That Compare: APR, APY and the Rule of 72 (p1)

## Research context: Money That Grows - Chapter 2, Part 1 (APR, APY and the Rule of 72)

### Chapter scope

- This part covers the whole of Chapter 2: sections 2.1 to 2.4, pages 7 to 10.
- 2.1 Two numbers for one loan (p. 7); 2.2 Converting APR to APY (p. 8); 2.3 The Rule of 72 (p. 9); 2.4 Limits and common mistakes (p. 10).
- Inflation and real returns belong to Chapter 3, not to this part.

### Fact ledger

#### Definitions

- D1. APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly rate before compounding within the year is taken into account; if interest is added n times a year, each period pays APR / n. (Ch. 2, §2.1, p. 7)
- D2. APY (annual percentage yield) is the rate actually earned in one year once interest added during the year also earns interest. (Ch. 2, §2.1, p. 7)
- D3. Savings products usually advertise the APY because it is the larger number; loans usually advertise the APR because it is the smaller one. (Ch. 2, §2.1, p. 7)

#### Rules and formulas

- R1. APY = (1 + APR / n)^n - 1, with both rates as decimals and n the number of compounding periods per year. In LaTeX: \mathrm{APY} = \left(1 + \frac{\mathrm{APR}}{n}\right)^{n} - 1. (Ch. 2, §2.2, p. 8)
- R2. The more often interest is added, the larger the gap between APR and APY, but each extra period adds less. (Ch. 2, §2.2, p. 8)
- R3. Rule of 72: years to double is approximately 72 divided by the yearly rate written as a percentage. (Ch. 2, §2.3, p. 9)

#### Worked numerical examples

- N1. A credit card with APR 12% compounded monthly (n = 12): APY = 1.01^12 - 1, about 0.1268, or 12.68%. (Ch. 2, §2.2, p. 8)
- N2. Daily compounding of a 12% APR gives an APY of about 12.75%. (Ch. 2, §2.2, p. 8)
- N3. At 6% a year money doubles in about 72 / 6 = 12 years; at 9% in about 8 years. (Ch. 2, §2.3, p. 9)
- N4. The exact doubling time at 6% is ln(2) / ln(1.06), about 11.9 years. (Ch. 2, §2.3, p. 9)

#### Historical case

- H1. Luca Pacioli described the Rule of 72 in his 1494 book Summa de arithmetica without explaining where it came from. (Ch. 2, §2.3, p. 9)
- H2. Merchants used 72 because it has many divisors (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12), which makes mental arithmetic easy. (Ch. 2, §2.3, p. 9)

#### Limitations and common mistakes

- L1. The Rule of 72 is most accurate for rates between about 6% and 10%; at very high rates it underestimates the doubling time. (Ch. 2, §2.4, p. 10)
- M1. Comparing an APR on one product with an APY on another is a common mistake; convert both to APY first. (Ch. 2, §2.4, p. 10)
- L2. The APY formula assumes a fixed rate for the year and no withdrawals. (Ch. 2, §2.4, p. 10)

### Real-world problem

- Actors: a borrower with a credit card and a saver comparing accounts. (Ch. 2, §2.1, p. 7)
- Situation: two products quote different kinds of rate, so the numbers cannot be compared directly. (Ch. 2, §2.1, p. 7)
- Failure: a borrower who reads 12% APR and never repays owes 12.68% more after a year. (Ch. 2, §2.2, p. 8)
- Invention or idea: the APY conversion formula and the Rule of 72 as a mental shortcut. (Ch. 2, §2.2-2.3, pp. 8-9)

### Terms and acronyms

- APR: annual percentage rate. (p. 7)
- APY: annual percentage yield. (p. 7)
- Compounding period (n): how many times per year interest is added. (p. 8)
- Rule of 72: doubling-time shortcut. (p. 9)

- Later: Chapter 3 uses yearly growth rates to separate nominal and real returns.

## Chapter 3: Inflation and Real Returns (p1)

## Research context: Money That Grows - Chapter 3, Part 1 (Inflation and Real Returns)

### Chapter scope

- This part covers the whole of Chapter 3: sections 3.1 to 3.4, pages 11 to 14.
- 3.1 When prices rise (p. 11); 3.2 Measuring inflation with the CPI (p. 12); 3.3 Nominal and real returns (p. 13); 3.4 Limits and common mistakes (p. 14).
- This is the last chapter of the book.

### Fact ledger

#### Definitions

- D1. Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. (Ch. 3, §3.1, p. 11)
- D2. Purchasing power is what a dollar can buy; it falls when prices rise. (Ch. 3, §3.1, p. 11)
- D3. The CPI (consumer price index) prices a fixed basket of goods and services that typical households buy, such as food, rent, transport and clothing, and tracks its cost over time. (Ch. 3, §3.2, p. 12)
- D4. The nominal return is the percentage growth of a balance in dollars. (Ch. 3, §3.3, p. 13)
- D5. The real return is the growth of what the balance can buy. (Ch. 3, §3.3, p. 13)

#### Rules and formulas

- R1. Inflation rate = (CPI_new - CPI_old) / CPI_old. In LaTeX: \pi = \frac{\mathrm{CPI}_{new} - \mathrm{CPI}_{old}}{\mathrm{CPI}_{old}}. (Ch. 3, §3.2, p. 12)
- R2. Exact Fisher relation: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) x (1 + inflation); so real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1. (Ch. 3, §3.3, p. 13)
- R3. Approximation: real return is about nominal minus inflation, close when both rates are small. (Ch. 3, §3.3, p. 13)

#### Worked numerical examples

- N1. A balance growing 5% in a year when prices rise 5% buys no more at the end of the year. (Ch. 3, §3.1, p. 11)
- N2. CPI from 200 to 206 in one year: inflation = (206 - 200) / 200 = 0.03, or 3%. (Ch. 3, §3.2, p. 12)
- N3. Nominal 7%, inflation 3%: real = 1.07 / 1.03 - 1, about 0.0388, or 3.88%; the shortcut gives 4%. (Ch. 3, §3.3, p. 13)

#### Historical case

- H1. The American economist Irving Fisher popularised the relationship in his 1930 book The Theory of Interest. (Ch. 3, §3.3, p. 13)

#### Limitations and common mistakes

- L1. The CPI measures an average basket; personal costs can rise faster or slower. (Ch. 3, §3.4, p. 14)
- M1. Celebrating a nominal gain without subtracting inflation; a 2% savings account with 4% inflation loses purchasing power every year. (Ch. 3, §3.4, p. 14)
- L2. The subtraction shortcut becomes inaccurate when inflation or returns are large. (Ch. 3, §3.4, p. 14)

### Real-world problem

- Actors: a saver watching both the balance and the prices of everyday goods. (Ch. 3, §3.1, p. 11)
- Situation: the balance grows in dollars while prices also rise. (Ch. 3, §3.1, p. 11)
- Failure: the saver believes they are richer although they can buy no more, or even less. (Ch. 3, §3.4, p. 14)
- Invention or idea: the CPI and the Fisher relation turn nominal growth into real growth. (Ch. 3, §3.2-3.3, pp. 12-13)

### Terms and acronyms

- Inflation, purchasing power (p. 11); CPI: consumer price index (p. 12); nominal return, real return (p. 13).
